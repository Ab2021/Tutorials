# Module 02 — The Biomedical Data Landscape

> **Program:** Biomedical Data Science for Industry (2026)
> **Time:** 20 hours (8 lecture, 4 case clinic, 8 lab)
> **Track fit:** Common core
> **Prerequisites:** Module 00; basic SQL helpful
> **Tools:** Python, SQL, FHIR JSON, OMOP CDM, DICOM metadata, a data-catalog worksheet
> **Industry question:** "Which data can answer this question, who controls it, what does it cost to obtain, and what will it never be able to tell us?"

---

## 1. Why industry cares

The most expensive mistake in biomedical data science is choosing the wrong data source. A claims database can show utilization but not disease severity. An EHR can show labs but not care outside the network. A wearable can show heart rate but not whether the patient took their medication. A trial dataset can show efficacy in a selected population but not effectiveness in routine care.

Data acquisition is slow, legal, expensive, and often the binding constraint. A data scientist who can map the landscape before writing code saves months.

This module teaches you to:

- Distinguish data generated by **care**, **payment**, **research**, **devices**, **patients**, and **administration**.
- Understand the major standards that make data usable across systems.
- Evaluate access pathways and constraints.
- Recognize what each source can and cannot measure.
- Build a data-source map for a real decision.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end of this module, you can:

- Classify biomedical data sources by producer, purpose, granularity, longitudinality, and bias.
- Explain the structure and use of EHR, claims, registry, trial, imaging, waveform, omics, wearable, and patient-reported data.
- Read and write basic FHIR resources and query an OMOP CDM.
- Interpret DICOM metadata and recognize image provenance issues.
- Map clinical concepts across ICD, SNOMED CT, LOINC, RxNorm, CPT, and NDC.
- Evaluate data quality across completeness, accuracy, timeliness, consistency, and fitness for purpose.
- Design a linkage strategy and explain its privacy and error implications.
- Choose between public benchmarks, licensed data, synthetic data, and prospective collection.
- Write a data-source decision memo with access, cost, quality, and regulatory considerations.

## 3. Mental model: six data universes

| Universe | Producer | Primary purpose | Strength | Fundamental limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clinical care | Clinicians, nurses, labs, imaging | Document and deliver care | Rich, longitudinal, clinically detailed | Fragmented, documentation-biased |
| Payment | Payers, billing departments | Reimburse and manage utilization | Complete encounters, costs, cross-provider | No clinical severity, coding incentives |
| Research | Investigators, consortia | Answer scientific questions | Protocolized, validated, consent | Selected populations, expensive, slow |
| Device / sensor | Devices, wearables, remote monitoring | Measure and transmit physiology | High frequency, real-world | Artifacts, adherence, proprietary formats |
| Patient-generated | Patients, caregivers | Report experience, symptoms, behavior | Patient-centered, outside care | Recall bias, missingness, validation |
| Administrative / public | Registries, public health, government | Surveillance, policy, safety | Population coverage, legal mandate | Lag, aggregation, limited clinical detail |

Most real projects combine at least two universes. Linkage is where much of the value — and much of the risk — lives.

## 4. Core data sources

### 4.1 Electronic health records

An EHR contains:

- Demographics and insurance.
- Encounters and admissions.
- Problem lists and diagnoses.
- Medications and orders.
- Laboratory results.
- Vital signs and flowsheets.
- Clinical notes.
- Imaging orders and reports.
- Procedures and surgical history.
- Immunizations.
- Allergies.
- Care plans and referrals.
- Billing and claims generated from the encounter.

| EHR data type | Structure | Temporal resolution | Key issue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demographics | Structured | Static / slow-changing | Missing social fields; identity complexity |
| Encounters | Structured | Event-level | Transfers create duplicate episodes |
| Diagnoses | Coded + text | Encounter-level | Rule-out, history, coding variation |
| Medications | Orders + administration | Event-level | Ordered ≠ administered ≠ taken |
| Labs | Structured | Timestamped | Unit changes, lab method changes |
| Vitals | Flowsheet | Timestamped | Defaults, device artifacts |
| Notes | Unstructured | Event-level | Copy-forward, negation, templates |
| Imaging | DICOM + report | Study-level | Large, site-specific protocols |

**Common EHR vendors:** Epic, Oracle Health (Cerner), MEDITECH, athenahealth, eClinicalWorks. Vendor differences affect data models, APIs, and available fields.

**Industry interpretation:** An EHR is an operational and billing system that happens to contain clinical data. It was not designed for research. Every analytic dataset requires reconstruction.

### 4.2 Claims and administrative data

Claims data include:

- Enrollment and eligibility.
- Medical claims: diagnoses, procedures, providers, dates, allowed/paid amounts.
- Pharmacy claims: fills, days supply, quantity, cost.
- Inpatient and outpatient utilization.
- Coordination of benefits.

**Strengths:** longitudinal across providers, relatively complete for insured populations, includes cost and utilization.

**Limitations:** no clinical measurements, code upcoding, limited lab values, race/ethnicity often missing or imputed, no care for uninsured, lag.

**Common sources:** CMS Medicare/Medicaid claims, commercial claims (e.g., Merative MarketScan, Optum), state all-payer claims databases.

### 4.3 Registries

Registries collect standardized data for a defined population or condition. Examples: cancer registries, implant registries, stroke registries, rare-disease registries, quality registries.

| Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|
| Condition-specific depth | Selection and reporting bias |
| Long follow-up | Expensive maintenance |
| Standardized definitions | Lag, incomplete ascertainment |
| Regulatory credibility | Limited generalizability |

### 4.4 Clinical trial data

Trial data include protocol-defined visits, eligibility, interventions, endpoints, adverse events, and adjudication.

**Strengths:** high-quality, prospective, validated endpoints, informed consent.

**Limitations:** highly selected populations, short duration, expensive, often inaccessible. Individual participant data may be available through platforms such as Vivli, YODA, or CSDR.

### 4.5 Imaging data

Imaging data are stored as DICOM objects containing pixel data and metadata: patient, study, series, acquisition parameters, device, slice thickness, orientation, and radiation dose.

| Modality | Data characteristics | Key provenance issues |
|---|---|---|
| CT | 3D volumes, Hounsfield units | Reconstruction kernel, slice thickness, contrast phase |
| MRI | Multiple sequences, intensity non-standardization | Field strength, coil, protocol |
| X-ray | 2D projections | Portable vs fixed, positioning, exposure |
| Ultrasound | Frames/clips, operator-dependent | Probe, angle, compression |
| Pathology | Whole-slide images, gigapixel | Stain, scanner, magnification |
| PET | Functional uptake | Tracer, uptake time, reconstruction |

**Industry interpretation:** DICOM metadata can leak identity and site. De-identification must handle burned-in annotations and private tags.

### 4.6 Physiological waveforms

Waveforms include ECG, EEG, EMG, respiratory signals, arterial pressure, photoplethysmography (PPG), and capnography.

| Signal | Sampling frequency | Common uses | Key artifacts |
|---|---:|---|---|
| ECG | 250–1,000 Hz | Arrhythmia, ischemia, QT | Baseline wander, motion, lead placement |
| PPG | 25–1,000 Hz | SpO2, heart rate, perfusion | Motion, ambient light, skin tone |
| EEG | 200–1,000 Hz | Seizure, sleep, anesthesia | Muscle, eye movement, electrode artifact |
| Respiratory | 10–100 Hz | Apnea, rate, effort | Motion, sensor displacement |
| Arterial pressure | 100–250 Hz | Hemodynamics | Damping, line flush |
| EMG | 1,000–10,000 Hz | Neuromuscular function | Crosstalk, fatigue |

### 4.7 Omics data

| Data type | What it measures | Common formats | Key issues |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genomics | DNA sequence / variants | FASTA, FASTQ, BAM, VCF | Coverage, reference genome, batch |
| Transcriptomics | RNA abundance | FASTQ, count matrices | Library prep, normalization |
| Single-cell RNA | Per-cell expression | H5AD, matrix market | Sparsity, doublets, batch |
| Spatial transcriptomics | Expression with location | H5AD, TIFF, spatial coordinates | Alignment, resolution |
| Proteomics | Protein abundance | mzML, protein groups | Missingness, normalization |
| Metabolomics | Small molecules | mzML, CSV | Identification confidence |
| Epigenomics | Chromatin accessibility, methylation | BED, bigWig, IDAT | Coverage, batch |
| Microbiome | Microbial composition | FASTQ, BIOM | Compositionality, contamination |

### 4.8 Wearables and patient-generated data

Wearables can provide continuous heart rate, RR intervals, accelerometry, sleep staging, oxygen saturation, skin temperature, and electrodermal activity. Patient-reported outcomes add symptoms, function, and quality of life.

**Strengths:** real-world, high frequency, patient-centered, potentially earlier detection.

**Limitations:** device-specific algorithms, proprietary raw data, adherence, charging, skin tone and body-composition effects, validation against clinical ground truth, and inequitable access.

### 4.9 Public health and environmental data

Examples: notifiable disease surveillance, immunization registries, vital statistics, air quality, weather, census, food environment, housing, transportation.

These are essential for social determinants and population health, but they are often aggregated, lagged, and measured at a different geographic unit than clinical data.

### 4.10 Literature, guidelines, and regulatory data

- PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library.
- ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP.
- FDA 510(k), De Novo, PMA, and MAUDE databases.
- EUDAMED (as available).
- Guidelines from professional societies.
- Product labels and instructions for use.

These are data sources for evidence synthesis, regulatory strategy, and competitive intelligence.

## 5. Standards and terminologies

### 5.1 Why standards matter

Standards enable:

- Exchange between systems.
- Semantic consistency.
- Reuse of algorithms and cohorts.
- Regulatory traceability.
- Multi-site research.

They do not eliminate ambiguity. A standard can carry a bad concept perfectly.

### 5.2 FHIR

Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (HL7 FHIR) models healthcare data as resources.

| Resource | Represents | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| `Patient` | Demographics | Cohort identification |
| `Encounter` | Care interaction | Admission, visit |
| `Observation` | Measurement or assertion | Lab, vital, survey |
| `Condition` | Diagnosis/problem | Problem list |
| `MedicationRequest` | Order | Prescription |
| `MedicationAdministration` | Administration | Inpatient dosing |
| `Procedure` | Procedure | Surgery, line placement |
| `DiagnosticReport` | Report | Lab/imaging report |
| `ImagingStudy` | Imaging study | DICOM linkage |
| `DocumentReference` | Document | Note metadata |
| `Observation` with LOINC | Coded measurement | Interoperable lab |

FHIR uses RESTful APIs, JSON/XML, references, extensions, and profiles. US interoperability is shaped by the ONC Cures Act and HTI-1 certification criteria.

**Minimal FHIR example:**

```json
{
  "resourceType": "Observation",
  "status": "final",
  "code": {
    "coding": [{
      "system": "http://loinc.org",
      "code": "8867-4",
      "display": "Heart rate"
    }]
  },
  "subject": {"reference": "Patient/123"},
  "effectiveDateTime": "2026-03-01T14:30:00Z",
  "valueQuantity": {"value": 88, "unit": "beats/min", "system": "http://unitsofmeasure.org", "code": "/min"}
}
```

**Pitfall:** FHIR resources are event- and API-oriented. Reconstructing a longitudinal cohort requires resolving references, handling versions, and deduplicating.

### 5.3 OMOP Common Data Model

OMOP CDM standardizes observational data into tables such as:

- `PERSON`, `OBSERVATION_PERIOD`, `VISIT_OCCURRENCE`.
- `CONDITION_OCCURRENCE`, `DRUG_EXPOSURE`, `PROCEDURE_OCCURRENCE`.
- `MEASUREMENT`, `OBSERVATION`, `NOTE`.
- `DEATH`, `COHORT`, `COHORT_DEFINITION`.

It uses standard vocabularies (SNOMED, LOINC, RxNorm) and maps source codes through `CONCEPT` and `SOURCE_TO_CONCEPT_MAP`.

**Strengths:** multi-site analytics, standardized methods, OHDSI tooling, reproducible cohort definitions.

**Limitations:** mapping loss, ETL complexity, limited imaging/waveform support, site-specific data quality.

### 5.4 Terminologies and code systems

| System | Scope | Example | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| ICD-10 / ICD-11 | Diseases, signs, causes | `I50.9` heart failure | Billing, registries, phenotyping |
| SNOMED CT | Clinical concepts | `84114007` heart failure | EHR problem lists, interoperability |
| LOINC | Lab and clinical observations | `8867-4` heart rate | Lab exchange |
| RxNorm | US medications | `866924` metformin | Medication normalization |
| NDC | US drug products | 11-digit code | Pharmacy claims |
| CPT | US procedures/services | `99213` office visit | Billing |
| HCPCS | Supplies, drugs, services | `J0135` | Billing |
| UCUM | Units of measure | `mg/dL`, `/min` | Unit consistency |
| DICOM | Imaging metadata | `StudyInstanceUID` | Imaging |
| HGNC | Gene symbols | `BRCA1` | Genomics |
| HPO | Human phenotypes | `HP:0001250` seizure | Rare disease, phenotyping |
| MeSH | Literature indexing | `D006331` heart failure | Literature search |

**Semantic mapping is not one-to-one.** A billing code can represent a rule-out; a SNOMED concept can be more specific than an ICD code; a lab LOINC code can have method-specific variants.

### 5.5 HL7 v2 and CDA

HL7 v2 is a widely used messaging standard, especially for ADT (admit/discharge/transfer), orders, and results. CDA is a document standard. Both remain common in legacy integrations.

## 6. Data access, licensing, and governance

### 6.1 Access pathways

| Pathway | Example | Requirements | Typical latency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public benchmark | MIMIC-IV demo, PhysioNet | Credentialing, training | Days |
| Registered access | UK Biobank, All of Us | Application, fee, institutional agreement | Weeks–months |
| Licensed commercial | Claims, EHR network | Contract, fee, data use limits | Weeks–months |
| Institutional | Local EHR | IRB, DUA, security review | Months |
| Prospective | Trial, registry, sensor study | Protocol, consent, ethics | Months–years |
| Synthetic | Synthea, generated data | License, validation | Immediate |

### 6.2 Legal and ethical instruments

- **HIPAA:** US privacy and security rules for covered entities and business associates.
- **GDPR:** EU data protection, lawful basis, data minimization, rights.
- **Common Rule:** US human-subjects research protections.
- **IRB / ethics committee:** review and approval.
- **Data use agreement (DUA):** contractual limits on use, sharing, retention.
- **Business associate agreement (BAA):** HIPAA obligations for vendors.
- **Consent:** broad, specific, dynamic, or waiver.
- **Data protection impact assessment (DPIA):** EU high-risk processing.
- **Institutional review:** security, privacy, export control.

### 6.3 De-identification

HIPAA offers two methods:

1. **Safe Harbor:** remove 18 categories of identifiers.
2. **Expert determination:** a qualified expert determines that re-identification risk is very small.

De-identification is not anonymization. Dates, rare conditions, geography, and free text can enable re-identification. Imaging metadata and burned-in annotations are common leaks.

### 6.4 Linkage

Linkage methods:

- **Deterministic:** exact match on identifiers.
- **Probabilistic:** match on name, date of birth, sex, address, etc., with weights.
- **Privacy-preserving:** hashing, secure multi-party computation, trusted third party, Bloom filters.

Linkage error creates both false matches and missed matches. Both bias downstream analyses. Report linkage quality.

## 7. Data quality

### 7.1 Dimensions

| Dimension | Question | Example check |
|---|---|---|
| Completeness | Are required values present? | Percent missing by site and year |
| Accuracy | Do values reflect truth? | Compare with chart review |
| Timeliness | When are data available? | Lab result lag |
| Consistency | Do values agree across sources? | EHR vs claims diagnosis |
| Validity | Do values conform to rules? | Units, ranges, code validity |
| Uniqueness | Are records duplicated? | Same patient, same event |
| Fitness | Is the data adequate for the decision? | Available at decision time |

### 7.2 Missingness mechanisms

- **MCAR:** missing completely at random.
- **MAR:** missing depends on observed variables.
- **MNAR:** missing depends on unobserved values.
- **Structural:** field not applicable.
- **Workflow:** not collected because of process.
- **Device:** sensor failure or non-wear.

**Industry interpretation:** most clinical missingness is not MCAR. A lab is ordered because a clinician is worried; its absence is information.

### 7.3 Data provenance

For every dataset, record:

- Source system and owner.
- Extraction date and query.
- Transformation code and version.
- Unit conversions.
- Inclusion/exclusion criteria.
- Linkage method and quality.
- De-identification method.
- Known limitations.
- Retention and destruction plan.

## 8. Comparing data sources: a decision framework

Ask:

1. **Does the source contain the outcome?** If not, can it be linked?
2. **Does it contain the exposure or intervention?**
3. **Does it contain the confounders?**
4. **Is the population representative of the deployment population?**
5. **Is the timing granular enough?**
6. **Is the data available at the decision time?**
7. **What is the access cost and latency?**
8. **What are the legal and ethical constraints?**
9. **What is the data quality?**
10. **Can the analysis be reproduced and audited?**

### 8.1 Source-selection matrix

| Question | EHR | Claims | Registry | Trial | Wearable | Omics | Public survey |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clinical severity | High | Low | Medium–high | High | Medium | Medium | Low |
| Cost / utilization | Medium | High | Low | Low | Low | Low | Low |
| Longitudinal follow-up | Medium | High | High | Protocol | High | Low | Repeated |
| Lab values | High | Low | Variable | High | Low | High | Low |
| Social determinants | Low | Low | Variable | Low | Medium | Low | High |
| Generalizability | Medium | High | Condition-specific | Low | Medium | Low | High |
| Access difficulty | High | High | Medium | Very high | Medium | Medium | Low |

## 9. Industry case studies

### 9.1 MIMIC-IV and the reproducibility of critical-care research

MIMIC-IV is a de-identified database of patients admitted to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. It has enabled thousands of studies and is a standard benchmark for EHR analytics.

**What transferred:** reproducible cohort definitions, open code, and transparent preprocessing.
**What did not:** single-center performance and practice patterns; models often fail to generalize.

Source: Johnson, A. E. W. et al., "MIMIC-IV, a freely accessible electronic health record dataset," *Scientific Data*, 2023. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-022-01899-x

### 9.2 All of Us and inclusive research data

The All of Us Research Program collects surveys, EHR data, physical measurements, and biospecimens from a diverse US cohort, with a focus on groups historically underrepresented in research.

**Industry lesson:** diversity by design improves subgroup analysis but requires careful consent, community engagement, and privacy protection.

Source: All of Us Research Program. https://allofus.nih.gov/

### 9.3 UK Biobank and deep phenotyping

UK Biobank combines questionnaires, physical measures, imaging, genomics, and linked health records in a large prospective cohort.

**Industry lesson:** deep phenotyping enables discovery but has healthy-volunteer and ancestry biases. Replication in diverse cohorts is essential.

Source: UK Biobank. https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/

### 9.4 Flatiron Health and real-world oncology

Flatiron Health curates oncology EHR data for research, including structured and unstructured data from community practices.

**Industry lesson:** real-world oncology data require abstraction and curation; technology alone does not create research-grade data.

Source: Flatiron Health. https://flatiron.com/

### 9.5 Wearable data in regulatory decisions

Wearables are increasingly used in trials and product development, but raw photoplethysmography and accelerometry are device-specific. Validation against clinical endpoints and clear intended use are essential.

**Industry lesson:** a sensor is not a biomarker until it is analytically and clinically validated.

Sources: FDA digital health guidance; Digital Medicine Society resources. https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence

## 10. Labs

### Lab 2.1 — Explore a FHIR bundle

**Objective:** Load a FHIR bundle and reconstruct a patient timeline.

**Data:** Public FHIR sample bundles or Synthea FHIR output.

**Steps:**

1. Parse `Patient`, `Encounter`, `Observation`, `Condition`, and `MedicationRequest`.
2. Resolve references and build a timeline.
3. Identify missing links and duplicate resources.
4. Map `Observation` codes to LOINC.
5. Write a cohort query for patients with a condition and a lab threshold.

**Deliverable:** timeline table and query.

**Acceptance criteria:** references resolved; timestamps normalized; code mappings documented.

### Lab 2.2 — Query an OMOP CDM

**Objective:** Build a cohort and compute a baseline characteristic table.

**Data:** OMOP synthetic dataset or Eunomia.

**Steps:**

1. Explore `PERSON`, `VISIT_OCCURRENCE`, `CONDITION_OCCURRENCE`, `DRUG_EXPOSURE`, `MEASUREMENT`.
2. Define a cohort with a condition and an age restriction.
3. Compute prevalence by year and site.
4. Compare source codes with standard concepts.
5. Document mapping loss.

**Deliverable:** SQL scripts and results table.

**Acceptance criteria:** cohort logic is reproducible; concept IDs are explained; mapping loss is quantified.

### Lab 2.3 — DICOM metadata audit

**Objective:** Inspect DICOM headers for provenance and privacy risks.

**Data:** Public DICOM sample or your own de-identified sample.

**Steps:**

1. Extract study, series, modality, manufacturer, and acquisition parameters.
2. Identify private tags and potentially identifying fields.
3. Check for burned-in annotations.
4. Compare protocols across sites or scanners.
5. Write a de-identification checklist.

**Deliverable:** metadata audit and checklist.

**Acceptance criteria:** no protected health information is displayed; risks are categorized; remediation is proposed.

### Lab 2.4 — Source-selection matrix

**Objective:** Choose data sources for three decisions.

**Data:** Instructor-provided scenarios.

**Steps:**

1. For each scenario, list the outcome, exposure, confounders, and timing.
2. Score candidate sources on the matrix in section 8.1.
3. Identify the minimum viable dataset.
4. Estimate access latency and cost.
5. Write a one-page recommendation.

**Deliverable:** decision memo.

**Acceptance criteria:** trade-offs explicit; limitations stated; no source treated as universally superior.

### Lab 2.5 — Data-quality profile

**Objective:** Profile a dataset and produce a quality report.

**Data:** MIMIC demo, Synthea, or a public table.

**Steps:**

1. Compute completeness, uniqueness, validity, and timeliness metrics.
2. Stratify by site, year, and demographic group.
3. Visualize missingness patterns.
4. Identify three plausible mechanisms for missingness.
5. Recommend handling strategies and sensitivity analyses.

**Deliverable:** quality report with figures.

**Acceptance criteria:** metrics defined; stratification included; mechanisms distinguished from fixes.

## 11. Assignment: data-source decision memo

Choose a decision from Module 00 and write a 1,500-word memo:

1. **Decision and population.**
2. **Candidate sources:** at least three.
3. **Comparison:** outcome, exposure, confounders, timing, generalizability.
4. **Access:** legal basis, cost, latency, restrictions.
5. **Quality:** known issues and mitigation.
6. **Linkage:** if needed, method and error.
7. **Recommendation:** primary and fallback source.
8. **What the data cannot answer.**

## 13. Regulatory, ethical, and commercial lens

- **Data rights:** the institution or payer may own the data; the patient has rights and interests. Read the DUA.
- **Secondary use:** research consent may not cover commercial product development.
- **International transfers:** GDPR and other laws restrict cross-border data movement.
- **Vendor access:** cloud vendors may process PHI only under a BAA and with appropriate safeguards.
- **Patient-generated data:** ownership and privacy expectations are still evolving; be transparent.
- **Value:** data acquisition cost is part of total cost of ownership. A marginally better dataset may not justify a six-month delay.

## 14. Failure modes and red flags

- Selecting a dataset because it is easy to access, not because it can answer the question.
- Treating an EHR as a complete record of care.
- Using claims without recognizing coding incentives.
- Assuming a public benchmark represents your population.
- Ignoring unit and code-version changes over time.
- Linking datasets without measuring linkage error.
- Using synthetic data to validate clinical performance.
- Forgetting that data must be available at decision time.
- Ignoring license restrictions on model training or redistribution.
