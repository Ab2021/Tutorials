# Module 01 — Clinical Foundations for Data Scientists

> **Program:** Biomedical Data Science for Industry (2026)
> **Time:** 32 hours (12 lecture, 8 case clinic, 12 lab)
> **Track fit:** Common core
> **Prerequisites:** None; curiosity about clinical work
> **Tools:** Python, pandas, a public ECG/physiology dataset, a synthetic EHR
> **Industry question:** "What does this clinical variable actually mean, how is it measured, and when would treating it as a feature be clinically wrong?"

---

## 1. Why industry cares

Biomedical data is not self-explanatory. A column called `BP` may be a triage reading, an averaged inpatient reading, an arterial-line value, or a patient-reported number. A diagnosis code may mean "confirmed," "rule out," "history of," or "billing convenience." A "sepsis" label may reflect a blood culture order, a billing code, or a clinician's note written after the fact.

Data scientists who lack clinical vocabulary make four predictable mistakes:

1. **They treat measurements as ground truth.** Blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and temperature are noisy, context-dependent estimates.
2. **They confuse documentation with biology.** Codes and notes reflect billing, workflow, and liability, not only disease.
3. **They miss confounding by indication.** Sicker patients receive more tests and more treatments, so treatment can look harmful.
4. **They optimize a metric that clinicians do not use.** A model may predict a code perfectly while being useless for the decision at hand.

Clinical literacy does not make you a clinician. It makes you a safer collaborator and a better data scientist.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end of this module, you can:

- Take a structured history and map each component to EHR fields and missing-data mechanisms.
- Interpret vital signs, common laboratory values, and basic ECG, spirometry, and imaging findings at a level sufficient to validate data and communicate with clinicians.
- Explain the physiology behind common cardiovascular, respiratory, neurologic, and metabolic variables used in machine learning.
- Distinguish symptoms, signs, diagnoses, procedures, and outcomes, and choose the right one for a model target.
- Critique a phenotype definition for sensitivity, specificity, and workflow bias.
- Identify documentation and coding artifacts that create leakage or label noise.
- Apply pretest probability and test-characteristic reasoning to evaluate a diagnostic model.
- Recognize automation bias, anchoring, and other human-factor risks in clinical AI.
- Use neurodiversity-affirming and patient-centered language.
- Write a clinical data-plausibility memo that a clinician reviewer would respect.

## 3. Mental model: from patient to data

```
Patient state
   │
   ├── biology (disease, physiology)
   ├── behavior (symptoms, adherence, diet, sleep)
   ├── environment (access, housing, occupation, social support)
   └── care process (tests, treatments, documentation, billing)
          │
          ▼
   Measurement (vital sign, lab, image, waveform, note, code)
          │
          ▼
   Record (EHR, registry, claim, device, wearables)
          │
          ▼
   Analytic dataset (cohort, features, labels, timestamps)
```

Each layer adds error and meaning. The clinical question is always: **which layer does my variable represent, and which layer does my decision need?**

### 3.1 The clinical reasoning loop

1. **History:** elicit the patient's story.
2. **Examination:** identify signs.
3. **Differential diagnosis:** generate plausible explanations.
4. **Pretest probability:** estimate likelihood before testing.
5. **Testing:** update probability using test characteristics.
6. **Management:** act, monitor, reassess.

Data science usually enters at steps 3–6, but the data were generated by steps 1–2 and by the care process itself.

## 4. Core clinical concepts

### 4.1 History taking

A structured history includes:

| Component | Content | Common EHR representation | Analytic trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chief complaint | Why the patient presented | Reason for visit, chief complaint | Free text; inconsistent |
| History of present illness | Onset, location, duration, character, aggravating/relieving factors, timing, severity | Note text, structured fields | Temporal information often missing |
| Past medical history | Chronic conditions, surgeries, hospitalizations | Problem list, ICD codes | Codes may be historical or rule-out |
| Medications | Prescribed, over-the-counter, adherence | Medication list, orders, claims | Orders ≠ actual use |
| Allergies | Drug and non-drug allergies | Allergy module | Free text, incomplete |
| Family history | Genetic and shared-environment risk | Family history module | Sparse, inconsistent |
| Social history | Tobacco, alcohol, drugs, occupation, housing, support | Social history fields, notes | Highly variable; stigmatizing language |
| Review of systems | Symptoms across systems | Structured questionnaires, notes | Often copied forward |

**Industry interpretation:** History variables are among the most predictive and the most poorly captured. A model that relies on a well-documented social history may perform differently in a safety-net hospital where documentation time is scarce.

### 4.2 Vital signs

| Vital sign | Normal adult range (resting) | Measurement notes | Data-science caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart rate | ~60–100 bpm | Manual, monitor, wearable | Activity, anxiety, medications |
| Blood pressure | ~120/80 mmHg (normal); thresholds vary | Cuff size, position, arterial line | White-coat, hypotension, averaging |
| Respiratory rate | ~12–20 breaths/min | Manual, impedance, capnography | Often charted by default; poor quality |
| Temperature | ~36.5–37.5 °C | Oral, rectal, tympanic, temporal | Site and method matter |
| Oxygen saturation | ~95–100% on room air | Pulse oximetry | Skin pigmentation bias, perfusion, motion |
| Pain | 0–10 scale | Patient report | Subjective, culturally mediated |
| Weight / BMI | Context-dependent | Scale, estimate | Fluid status, amputation, pregnancy |

**Early warning scores.** NEWS2 combines respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, systolic blood pressure, pulse, consciousness, and temperature. qSOFA uses respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure, and altered mentation. These are screening tools, not diagnoses. Their performance depends on the population and the threshold.

**Critical point:** Pulse oximetry has known accuracy limitations in patients with darker skin pigmentation, and racial bias in oxygen saturation measurement has been documented. Do not assume `SpO2` is an unbiased biological measurement.

### 4.3 Laboratory values

| Test | What it reflects | Common units | Caveats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hemoglobin | Oxygen-carrying capacity | g/dL or g/L | Hydration, transfusion, altitude |
| White blood cell count | Immune response | cells/µL | Stress, steroids, malignancy |
| Platelets | Clotting capacity | cells/µL | Sepsis, heparin, dilution |
| Sodium | Osmolar balance | mmol/L | Correction rate matters |
| Potassium | Cardiac/muscle excitability | mmol/L | Hemolysis, sampling |
| Creatinine | Renal filtration | mg/dL or µmol/L | Muscle mass, medications |
| eGFR | Estimated renal function | mL/min/1.73 m² | Equation-dependent; not valid in all populations |
| Glucose | Energy metabolism | mg/dL or mmol/L | Fasting status, stress, diabetes |
| HbA1c | Average glycemia | % or mmol/mol | Anemia, hemoglobin variants |
| Troponin | Myocardial injury | ng/L (assay-specific) | Many causes; serial change matters |
| BNP / NT-proBNP | Ventricular wall stress | pg/mL | Obesity, renal function, age |
| Lactate | Tissue hypoperfusion / stress | mmol/L | Many causes; not specific |
| Bilirubin | Liver / bile metabolism | mg/dL or µmol/L | Indirect vs direct |
| INR | Coagulation | ratio | Warfarin, liver disease |
| Arterial blood gas | Ventilation, oxygenation, acid-base | pH, PaCO2, PaO2, HCO3 | Sampling, timing |

**Reference ranges are population- and lab-specific.** Do not hard-code adult ranges for children, pregnant patients, or patients with chronic disease. Use the lab's own reference intervals and flag values with the associated units.

### 4.4 Cardiovascular physiology

Core relationships:

- **Cardiac output (CO) = heart rate (HR) × stroke volume (SV).**
- **Stroke volume** depends on preload, afterload, and contractility.
- **Mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≈ diastolic BP + (systolic BP − diastolic BP)/3.**
- **Oxygen delivery (DO₂) ≈ CO × arterial oxygen content.**
- **Arterial oxygen content (CaO₂) ≈ 1.34 × Hb × SaO₂ + 0.003 × PaO₂.**

Heart failure is a clinical syndrome, not a single disease. Key categories:

| Category | Ejection fraction | Typical physiology | Data implications |
|---|---|---|---|
| HFrEF | ≤ 40% | Systolic dysfunction, reduced contractility | Often responds to neurohormonal therapy |
| HFmrEF | 41–49% | Mixed | Less evidence, heterogeneous |
| HFpEF | ≥ 50% | Diastolic dysfunction, stiffness | Common in older, obese, hypertensive patients; underdiagnosed |

Common heart-failure variables: dyspnea, orthopnea, edema, JVP, crackles, weight gain, BNP/NT-proBNP, echocardiographic EF, LA size, E/e′, and diuretic use.

**Industry interpretation:** A readmission model for heart failure must account for disease severity, social support, medication adherence, access to follow-up, and documentation. The strongest predictor may be a social variable, which raises fairness and actionability questions.

### 4.5 Electrocardiography

An ECG records the heart's electrical activity through surface electrodes. A standard 12-lead ECG provides 10 seconds of data across 12 perspectives.

**Systematic interpretation:**

1. **Rate:** bradycardia, normal, tachycardia.
2. **Rhythm:** sinus, atrial fibrillation, flutter, ectopy, blocks.
3. **Axis:** normal, left, right, extreme.
4. **Intervals:** PR, QRS, QT/QTc.
5. **Chamber enlargement:** atrial, ventricular.
6. **Ischemia/infarction:** ST elevation/depression, T-wave changes, Q waves.
7. **Other:** electrolyte effects, drug effects, accessory pathways.

| Finding | Typical threshold / description | Data issue |
|---|---|---|
| Sinus bradycardia | HR < 60, sinus P waves | Athletes, medications |
| Sinus tachycardia | HR > 100, sinus P waves | Fever, pain, sepsis, anemia |
| Atrial fibrillation | Irregularly irregular, absent P waves | Paroxysmal; single ECG may miss |
| First-degree AV block | PR > 200 ms | Often benign |
| Bundle branch block | QRS ≥ 120 ms with morphology | Can mask infarction |
| QT prolongation | QTc thresholds vary by sex/age | Drug risk; measurement error |
| ST-elevation MI | ST elevation in contiguous leads | Time-critical; false positives |
| Left ventricular hypertrophy | Voltage criteria | Obesity, age, race effects |

**ECG data science pitfalls:**

- A 10-second strip may not capture paroxysmal arrhythmia.
- Lead placement and device quality affect morphology.
- Labels from automated reads may contain systematic errors.
- "Normal" labels may be assigned by exclusion, not by expert review.
- Patient-level leakage occurs when multiple ECGs from the same patient are split across train and test.
- Time-to-event and urgency matter: a correct diagnosis 30 minutes late may be harmful.

### 4.6 Respiratory physiology

Core concepts:

- **Tidal volume (Vt):** air moved per breath.
- **Minute ventilation (VE) = Vt × respiratory rate.**
- **FEV1:** forced expiratory volume in 1 second.
- **FVC:** forced vital capacity.
- **FEV1/FVC:** airflow obstruction marker.
- **Diffusing capacity (DLCO):** gas exchange across the alveolar-capillary membrane.
- **PaO2 / FiO2 ratio (P/F):** oxygenation severity.
- **Dead space:** ventilated but not perfused lung.
- **Shunt:** perfused but not ventilated lung.

| Pattern | FEV1/FVC | FVC | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Obstructive | Reduced (< 0.70 post-bronchodilator) | Normal or reduced | COPD, asthma |
| Restrictive | Normal or increased | Reduced | Interstitial disease, neuromuscular, chest wall |
| Mixed | Reduced | Reduced | Combined disease |

**Acid-base interpretation:**

1. Look at pH: acidemia (< 7.35) or alkalemia (> 7.45).
2. Look at PaCO2: respiratory acidosis if high, respiratory alkalosis if low.
3. Look at HCO3: metabolic acidosis if low, metabolic alkalosis if high.
4. Check compensation and calculate the anion gap when metabolic acidosis is present.

**Industry interpretation:** Respiratory rate is often the most predictive vital sign for deterioration, but it is frequently imputed or defaulted in EHRs. A model trained on charted respiratory rate may learn documentation behavior rather than physiology.

### 4.7 Neurology and neurodiversity

Neurologic assessment includes:

- **Mental status:** alertness, orientation, attention, language, memory.
- **Cranial nerves:** pupils, eye movements, facial strength, hearing, swallowing.
- **Motor:** tone, strength, coordination, gait.
- **Sensory:** pain, temperature, proprioception, vibration.
- **Reflexes:** deep tendon, Babinski.
- **Meningeal signs:** neck stiffness, Kernig, Brudzinski.

**Localization** is the core neurologic skill: identify where the lesion is, not just what the symptom is.

| Syndrome | Typical presentation | Common data |
|---|---|---|
| Stroke | Sudden focal deficit | NIHSS, imaging, time last known well |
| Seizure | Altered awareness, abnormal movements | EEG, history, medications |
| Delirium | Acute fluctuating attention | CAM-ICU, chart review |
| Peripheral neuropathy | Distal sensory loss, weakness | Exam, nerve conduction |
| Multiple sclerosis | Disseminated CNS events | MRI, CSF, relapse history |

**Neurodiversity lens.** Autism, ADHD, dyslexia, and other neurodivergent conditions are forms of human diversity, not merely deficits. In data science:

- Do not use "normal" as a synonym for "good."
- Avoid pathologizing language unless clinically necessary.
- Consider sensory and cognitive accessibility in tools.
- Recognize that diagnostic criteria and access vary by sex, gender, culture, and age.
- Ask whether a model's label reflects impairment, difference, or unmet support needs.

### 4.8 Imaging fundamentals

| Modality | Physics | Best for | Radiation | Data characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| X-ray | Ionizing radiation attenuation | Chest, bone, abdomen | Yes | 2D projection; portable; common |
| CT | X-ray attenuation from multiple angles | Trauma, chest, abdomen, neuro | Yes | 3D volumetric; Hounsfield units |
| MRI | Magnetic fields and radiofrequency | Soft tissue, brain, spine, joints | No | Multiple sequences; 3D |
| Ultrasound | High-frequency sound | Pregnancy, cardiac, abdominal, procedural | No | Operator-dependent; real-time |
| PET | Radiotracer metabolism | Oncology, neuro, cardiac | Yes | Functional; often fused with CT |
| Mammography | Low-dose X-ray | Breast cancer screening | Yes | 2D/3D; dense tissue challenge |
| Pathology | Light microscopy | Tissue diagnosis | No | Whole-slide images; gigapixel |

**Radiology workflow:** order → protocol → acquisition → reconstruction → interpretation → report → communication of critical findings. A model that does not fit this workflow will not be used, even if accurate.

### 4.9 Documentation and coding

Clinical notes serve care, communication, legal, and billing purposes. They are not research annotations.

| Artifact | What it is | Analytic use | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem list | Active and historical conditions | Cohort definition | Incomplete, stale |
| ICD-10/11 code | Diagnosis/procedure classification | Phenotyping, billing | Rule-out, history, coding variation |
| CPT code | Procedure/service | Utilization, cost | Bundling, site variation |
| DRG | Inpatient payment group | Cost, severity | Payment-oriented, not clinical |
| Medication order | Prescribed drug | Exposure | Not adherence |
| Medication administration | Nurse-recorded administration | Actual inpatient exposure | Timing errors |
| Lab order | Test requested | Care intensity | Confounding by indication |
| Note | Narrative documentation | NLP, phenotyping | Copy-forward, template, negation |
| Flowsheet | Structured repeated measurements | Vitals, scores | Default values, missingness |

**Copy-forward** is a major source of label leakage: a note may contain an old diagnosis or a future plan that was copied into the current encounter.

### 4.10 Clinical reasoning, tests, and bias

**Pretest probability** is the probability of disease before a test. A test's usefulness depends on pretest probability, sensitivity, specificity, and the consequences of false results.

- **Sensitivity** = TP / (TP + FN): ability to detect disease.
- **Specificity** = TN / (TN + FP): ability to rule out disease.
- **Positive predictive value** depends on prevalence.
- **Negative predictive value** depends on prevalence.
- **Likelihood ratio positive** = sensitivity / (1 − specificity).
- **Likelihood ratio negative** = (1 − sensitivity) / specificity.

**Cognitive biases relevant to clinical AI:**

| Bias | Description | AI analogue |
|---|---|---|
| Anchoring | Fixating on an initial diagnosis | Model overweights first available data |
| Availability | Overestimating recent or memorable diagnoses | Training data skew |
| Confirmation | Seeking supporting evidence | Feature selection bias |
| Automation bias | Over-trusting automated output | Clinician accepts wrong alert |
| Alarm fatigue | Ignoring repeated alerts | Low positive predictive value |
| Base-rate neglect | Ignoring prevalence | Poor PPV in low-prevalence settings |
| Diagnostic momentum | Continuing a prior diagnosis | Label leakage from history |

## 5. Data semantics: mapping clinical concepts to variables

| Clinical concept | Common variables | What it really means | Common analytic error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fever | Temperature, antipyretic order | Host response, measurement site | Treating one reading as truth |
| Sepsis | Culture, antibiotics, lactate, SOFA | Syndrome, not a single test | Label derived from treatment |
| Heart failure | ICD code, BNP, EF, diuretic | Clinical syndrome | Code-based label includes history |
| Myocardial infarction | Troponin, ECG, angiography | Injury plus ischemia | Assay and timing differences |
| Acute kidney injury | Creatinine, urine output | Functional change | Baseline creatinine unknown |
| Delirium | CAM-ICU, notes, antipsychotics | Acute brain dysfunction | Under-recognition |

| Readmission | Admission timestamp | Utilization event | Planned readmissions, transfers |
| Mortality | Death date, discharge status | Vital status | Linkage lag, out-of-hospital deaths |
| Adherence | Pharmacy fills, pill counts | Behavior | Primary non-adherence, gaps |

## 6. Industry case studies

### 6.1 Sepsis prediction and alert fatigue

Epic's Sepsis Model was widely deployed, then externally evaluated in a large health system. The evaluation found substantially lower discrimination than the vendor-reported performance and a high false-alert burden. The lesson is not that sepsis prediction is impossible; it is that **vendor validation, local case mix, label definitions, and alert workflow must be independently evaluated**.

**What transferred:** early warning can change care when integrated with a response team and clear ownership.
**What did not:** a single AUC claim, decontextualized from local prevalence and workflow.

Source: Wong, A. et al., "External Validation of a Widely Implemented Proprietary Sepsis Prediction Model in Hospitalized Patients," *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2021. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2021.2626

### 6.2 ECG algorithms and hidden stratification

Deep-learning ECG models can detect reduced ejection fraction and other conditions from waveforms. Performance can be strong in curated datasets but degrade across devices, lead configurations, and populations. A key risk is **hidden stratification**: a model may perform well overall but poorly on clinically important subgroups.

**Industry lesson:** report per-lead, per-device, per-site, and per-subgroup performance; validate on the target acquisition workflow.

Source: Attia, Z. I. et al., "Screening for cardiac contractile dysfunction using an artificial intelligence-enabled electrocardiogram," *Nature Medicine*, 2019. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-018-0240-2

### 6.3 Pulse oximetry and racial bias

Pulse oximetry can overestimate arterial oxygen saturation in patients with darker skin pigmentation, and this bias has been associated with delayed recognition of hypoxemia. A model that uses SpO2 as a feature inherits this measurement bias.

**Industry lesson:** measurement bias is a data-quality and fairness issue, not only a clinical one. Validate sensor performance across skin tones and perfusion states.

Sources: Sjoding, M. W. et al., "Racial Bias in Pulse Oximetry Measurement," *New England Journal of Medicine*, 2020. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMc2029240; FDA, pulse oximeter safety communications.

### 6.4 Heart failure readmission

Heart failure is a common target for readmission models. The strongest predictors often include prior utilization, social factors, medication burden, and renal function. A model may accurately rank risk while offering no actionable mechanism.

**Industry lesson:** pair prediction with an intervention menu (e.g., transitional care, medication reconciliation, remote monitoring) and evaluate the joint intervention, not just the model.

Source: Frizzell, J. D. et al., "Prediction of 30-Day All-Cause Readmissions in Patients Hospitalized for Heart Failure," *JAMA Cardiology*, 2017. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2016.3956

### 6.5 Neurodiversity and accessible clinical tools

Clinical tools often assume a neurotypical user. Bright lights, complex forms, time pressure, and ambiguous instructions can exclude autistic, ADHD, and dyslexic patients and clinicians. Participatory design and accessible interfaces improve data quality and trust.

**Industry lesson:** accessibility is not a niche feature; it affects who is represented in the data and who can use the product.

Source: Armstrong, T., *The Power of Neurodiversity*; WHO, *Global report on health equity for persons with disabilities*, 2022.

## 7. Labs

### Lab 1.1 — From vignette to schema

**Objective:** Convert three written clinical vignettes into a structured patient schema and identify missingness.

**Data:** Instructor-provided de-identified vignettes; optional Synthea records.

**Steps:**

1. Define a JSON schema for demographics, history, vitals, labs, medications, diagnoses, and outcomes.
2. Extract each vignette manually or with an LLM, then verify every field.
3. Mark each missing field as unknown, not assessed, not applicable, or not documented.
4. Write a data-quality report with a missingness table.
5. Propose which fields would be available at three decision time points: triage, admission, discharge.

**Deliverable:** schema, extraction, missingness report, and a one-page clinical review.

**Acceptance criteria:** every field has a definition, unit, allowed values, and provenance; missingness categories are distinguished; a clinician reviewer agrees with at least 90% of extracted concepts.

### Lab 1.2 — ECG plausibility and interval measurement

**Objective:** Load a public 12-lead ECG, inspect lead quality, and measure rate and intervals.

**Data:** PTB-XL or a PhysioNet ECG dataset.

**Steps:**

1. Plot all leads with correct units and calibration.
2. Identify the lead with the cleanest P waves and QRS complexes.
3. Estimate heart rate using the RR interval.
4. Measure PR, QRS, and QT intervals on at least five beats.
5. Compare with the dataset's automated annotations.
6. Document three reasons automated annotations may disagree.

**Deliverable:** annotated plot and measurement table.

**Acceptance criteria:** measurements are within a clinically plausible range; uncertainty is stated; calibration and lead names are preserved.

### Lab 1.3 — Spirometry and blood gas interpretation

**Objective:** Interpret five spirometry/ABG cases and derive features for a model.

**Data:** Instructor-provided cases; public spirometry datasets if available.

**Steps:**

1. Classify each spirometry pattern.
2. Interpret each ABG for acid-base status and compensation.
3. Derive FEV1 percent predicted, FEV1/FVC, P/F ratio, and anion gap.
4. Identify which values are measured, calculated, or imputed.
5. Write a feature dictionary with units and clinical interpretation.

**Deliverable:** case table and feature dictionary.

**Acceptance criteria:** correct pattern classification; units consistent; no formula applied outside its valid population without a warning.

### Lab 1.4 — Phenotype definition under ambiguity

**Objective:** Build three competing definitions of "sepsis" or "heart failure" and compare cohort size and characteristics.

**Data:** MIMIC-IV demo or Synthea; credentialed MIMIC if available.

**Steps:**

1. Define a code-based phenotype.
2. Define a lab/vital-based phenotype.
3. Define a treatment-based phenotype.
4. Compare cohort counts, demographics, severity, and outcomes.
5. Explain which definition fits a proposed decision and why.

**Deliverable:** phenotype comparison report.

**Acceptance criteria:** definitions are reproducible; inclusion/exclusion criteria are explicit; differences are quantified; clinical plausibility is discussed.

### Lab 1.5 — Test characteristics and decision thresholds

**Objective:** Calculate sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and likelihood ratios for a diagnostic test at two prevalence levels.

**Data:** Simulated confusion matrices or a public dataset.

**Steps:**

1. Compute test characteristics at a chosen threshold.
2. Recompute PPV/NPV at prevalence 1%, 10%, and 30%.
3. Plot a decision curve.
4. Explain how the optimal threshold changes with the harm ratio.
5. Write a 200-word recommendation for a clinical stakeholder.

**Deliverable:** analysis notebook and memo.

**Acceptance criteria:** formulas correct; prevalence effect explained; recommendation tied to harms and benefits.

## 8. Assignment: clinical data-plausibility memo

Choose a public biomedical dataset or a de-identified sample. Write a 1,500–2,000 word memo addressed to a clinical reviewer.

Required sections:

1. **Dataset and intended decision.** What is the data, and what decision could it support?
2. **Clinical concept map.** Five key variables, their clinical meaning, measurement method, and unit.
3. **Plausibility checks.** Range checks, temporal checks, cross-variable checks, and duplicate checks.
4. **Missingness and documentation.** Which fields are missing, and why? Distinguish biological, workflow, and technical missingness.
5. **Label critique.** How is the outcome defined? What does the label actually capture?
6. **Bias and equity.** At least two mechanisms by which the data could produce inequitable performance.
7. **Recommendation.** Proceed, proceed with conditions, or stop. State the conditions.

| Criterion | Weight | Novice | Competent | Expert |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Clinical accuracy | 25% | Misuses terms or units | Correct definitions and units | Explains mechanisms and edge cases |
| Data plausibility | 20% | Only range checks | Range, temporal, cross-variable checks | Connects plausibility to clinical workflow |
| Label critique | 20% | Accepts label at face value | Identifies code/treatment ambiguity | Quantifies impact and proposes validation |
| Bias and equity | 15% | Generic statement | Names specific mechanisms | Links to measurement, access, and deployment |
| Communication | 10% | Jargon-heavy or vague | Clear and structured | Persuasive to a clinical audience |

## 10. Regulatory, ethical, and commercial lens

- **Clinical decision support:** if your output informs diagnosis or treatment, evaluate the FDA CDS criteria and local law. A "research only" label does not automatically remove obligations.
- **Measurement bias:** a device-related bias (e.g., pulse oximetry) can create a regulated safety issue. Report it.
- **Patient language:** use "patient with diabetes," not "diabetic"; avoid "non-compliant" when "did not fill" or "could not access" is more accurate.
- **Consent and secondary use:** clinical data may be used for research under waivers or consent, but commercial use may have stricter terms.
- **Value:** clinical plausibility reduces rework. A model that a clinician rejects at review has zero value, regardless of AUC.

## 11. Failure modes and red flags

- Using `discharge_disposition` or future notes as features.
- Treating `NaN` as "normal."
- Assuming all blood pressures were measured the same way.
- Using diagnosis codes as a gold standard without chart review.
- Ignoring unit differences (mg/dL vs mmol/L).
- Mixing arterial and venous labs.
- Using a 10-second ECG to rule out paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.
- Reporting overall AUC without subgroup or site performance.
- Confusing "statistically significant" with "clinically important."
- Building a model for a decision no one owns.

## 12. Interview and discussion questions

1. **What is the difference between a symptom, a sign, and a diagnosis?**
   Hint: patient-reported vs observed vs synthesized label.
2. **Why is respiratory rate often unreliable in EHR data?**
   Hint: manual charting, defaults, imputation, monitor artifacts.
3. **A model predicts sepsis with AUC 0.92. What else do you need?**
   Hint: prevalence, calibration, alert burden, time to detection, outcomes, workflow.
4. **How would you define heart failure for a readmission model?**
   Hint: codes plus BNP/EF/diuretic evidence; distinguish history from active disease.
5. **What is confounding by indication? Give a clinical example.**
   Hint: sicker patients get more treatment; treatment appears harmful.
6. **Why can a test with 99% sensitivity still have low PPV?**
   Hint: low prevalence.
7. **What is automation bias, and how do you design against it?**
   Hint: explanations with uncertainty, forced review, audit, training, override tracking.
8. **How does pulse oximetry bias affect a model?**
   Hint: measurement error correlated with race/skin tone; differential misclassification.
9. **What is copy-forward, and why is it dangerous?**
   Hint: stale diagnoses and future plans leak into current notes.
10. **How would you evaluate a neurologic deterioration model?**
    Hint: define deterioration, time window, competing risks, alert burden, subgroup performance.
11. **What does "clinically plausible" mean for a feature?**
    Hint: range, temporal sequence, physiology, measurement context.
12. **When would you prefer a simple score like NEWS2 to a complex model?**
    Hint: transparency, validation, implementation cost, trust, regulatory burden.

| Anion gap | Electrolyte-based marker of metabolic acidosis |
| Neurodiversity | Framing neurological differences as natural variation |
