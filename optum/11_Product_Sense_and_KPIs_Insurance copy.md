# 📊 Product Sense & KPIs in the Insurance / Healthcare Domain
### Optum Sr. AI/ML Engineer — Interview Preparation

> **Why this matters:** A Senior AI/ML Engineer is expected to do more than build models; you must ensure the model drives actual business value. "Product Sense" interviews test your ability to translate a vague business problem into a concrete AI solution, define how to measure its success, and balance competing business trade-offs (e.g., accuracy vs. member experience).

---

## PART 1: THE INSURANCE & HEALTHCARE KPI GLOSSARY
Before designing an AI product, you must speak the language of the business. These are the core metrics every insurance/healthcare product ultimately tries to improve.

### 1. Fundamental Business KPIs
*   **MLR (Medical Loss Ratio) / Combined Ratio:** The holy grail of insurance. It is the percentage of premium dollars spent on clinical services and quality improvement. (e.g., An MLR of 85% means $0.85 of every dollar goes to care; $0.15 goes to admin/profit). *AI Goal: Lower the administrative cost or prevent fraudulent medical payouts to improve the ratio.*
*   **PMPM (Per Member Per Month):** The average cost or revenue generated by one member in one month. *AI Goal: Identify high-risk members early to lower their PMPM cost through preventive care.*
*   **CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost):** Cost to acquire a new member (marketing, broker fees).
*   **CLV (Customer Lifetime Value):** The total revenue a member generates over their tenure. *AI Goal: Predict CLV to prioritize marketing spend (Your EXL/Aetna project).*
*   **Retention Rate / Churn:** Percentage of members who renew their policy during open enrollment. 

### 2. Operational KPIs (Where AI has the most impact)
*   **Auto-Adjudication Rate:** The percentage of claims processed and paid/denied entirely by software without human touch. *AI Goal: Increase this safely using ML/Rules without increasing false payouts.*
*   **Turnaround Time (TAT):** Time taken to process a claim, prior authorization, or member query.
*   **Overturn Rate (Appeals):** The percentage of denied claims or prior authorizations that are later overturned on appeal. *AI Goal: A high overturn rate means your AI is falsely denying care, which leads to massive admin costs and regulatory risk.*
*   **SIU ROI (Special Investigations Unit Return on Investment):** Dollars saved by fraud detection minus the cost of the investigation team.

## PART 2: AI-SPECIFIC PRODUCT METRICS
When you deploy an AI model, you cannot just track "Accuracy" or "AUC". You must track Product/UX metrics.

### AI Product Engagement & Value Metrics
*   **Adoption Rate:** % of target users (e.g., physicians, claims adjusters) who actually use the AI tool daily.
*   **Task Completion Time:** Does the AI actually save time? (e.g., Time to review a medical chart dropped from 15 mins to 4 mins).
*   **Acceptance / Override Rate:** When the AI recommends an action (e.g., "Flag for fraud"), how often does the human investigator agree (accept) vs. disagree (override)? *High override rate = the model is annoying the user.*
*   **Time-to-Intervention:** How fast a risk is identified. (e.g., Your Chubb project reduced fraud detection from >30 days to <2 days).

### AI Safety & Guardrail Metrics (Crucial for Optum)
*   **Faithfulness Score (RAG):** % of LLM outputs that can be explicitly traced back to the source document (measuring hallucination).
*   **False Positive Rate (Member Friction):** % of legitimate claims flagged as fraud or denied. In healthcare, a false positive means a patient doesn't get care. This is a highly sensitive metric.

## PART 3: THE PRODUCT SENSE INTERVIEW FRAMEWORK
When asked "How would you build an AI product for X?", use the **GAME** framework.

1. **G**oal: What is the core business objective? (Revenue? Efficiency? Member Experience?)
2. **A**udience: Who are the users? (Members? Providers/Doctors? Internal Adjusters?)
3. **M**etrics: What are the North Star and Counter metrics?
4. **E**xecution & Trade-offs: How do we build it, and what are the risks/trade-offs?

## PART 4: DETAILED INTERVIEW QUESTIONS & SCENARIOS

### Scenario 1: The Automated Prior Authorization System
**Question: "Optum wants to use LLMs to automate Prior Authorization (approving or denying expensive medical procedures). How would you measure the success of this product?"**

**The Answer:**
> "I would break the metrics down into three categories: Business, User/Product, and Safety.
>
> **1. Business / North Star Metric:**
> *   **Auto-Adjudication Rate:** The percentage of prior auth requests handled end-to-end without a human nurse/doctor reviewing it.
> *   **Turnaround Time (TAT):** Reducing the time a patient waits for approval from 5 days to 5 minutes.
>
> **2. Counter Metrics (The Guardrails):**
> *   **Overturn/Appeal Rate:** If the AI auto-denies a procedure, and the doctor appeals and wins, the AI failed. A spiking overturn rate means our AI is too aggressive and causing massive administrative waste.
> *   **Provider CSAT (Customer Satisfaction):** Are doctors frustrated by the AI asking for unnecessary documents?
>
> **3. Model / AI Metrics:**
> *   **Override Rate:** For cases routed to a human, how often does the human disagree with the AI's drafted recommendation?
> *   **Faithfulness/Hallucination Rate:** Every AI decision must cite the exact medical policy rule. We measure how accurately it grounds its decisions.
>
> **The Trade-off:** The biggest trade-off here is Automation vs. Patient Safety. We cannot optimize purely for the Auto-Adjudication Rate, because the model might start illegally denying care. I would set a strict safety floor: 'Maximize Auto-Adjudication Rate subject to the constraint that the Overturn Rate does not exceed our historical human baseline of 4%.'"

### Scenario 2: Diagnosing a Failing AI Product
**Question: "You built an AI chatbot for health insurance members to ask questions about their benefits. The accuracy of the LLM is high (95%+), but the user retention is terrible—members use it once and never return. How do you diagnose this?"**

**The Answer:**
> "If model accuracy is high but retention is low, there is a disconnect between the AI's output and the User Experience. I would investigate the funnel step-by-step:
>
> **1. Analyze the 'Task Completion' Metric:**
> Is the bot actually solving their problem, or just giving them a long FAQ answer? If a member asks, 'Is my MRI covered?', and the bot answers 'MRIs are covered under section 4b with a 20% coinsurance', the answer is *technically accurate*, but the user still doesn't know what they owe. The task isn't complete.
>
> **2. Analyze Latency (Time-to-First-Token):**
> RAG pipelines over complex insurance documents can be slow. If it takes 15 seconds to stream an answer, users will drop off and call customer service instead. I'd check the P90 latency.
>
> **3. Analyze the Handoff Rate:**
> What percentage of conversations end with the user typing 'Speak to a human'? I would pull a random sample of 100 transcripts where the user requested a human to identify the intent the bot is failing to resolve.
>
> **4. Check Tone and Empathy:**
> In healthcare, people are often stressed or sick when asking about benefits. If the LLM's system prompt is too robotic or uses dense legal insurance jargon, members will abandon it.
>
> **Solution:** I would likely shift the product from a 'Knowledge Retrieval' bot to a 'Transactional Agent'. Instead of just quoting the policy, the bot needs tool access to look up their specific deductible met, and say: 'Yes, your MRI is covered. You have $200 left on your deductible, so your estimated out-of-pocket is $250. Would you like me to find an in-network facility?'"

### Scenario 3: The Fraud Detection Trade-off
**Question: "You are deploying a new Machine Learning model to detect fraudulent pharmacy claims. How do you decide where to set the probability threshold for flagging a claim?"**

**The Answer:**
> "This is a classic trade-off between Precision (not annoying innocent people) and Recall (catching all the fraud). The threshold isn't a math decision; it's a business and capacity decision.
>
> Here is how I determine the threshold:
> 
> **1. Calculate SIU Capacity:**
> How many claims can our Special Investigations Unit (SIU) manually review per week? If they can only review 500 claims, it doesn't matter if the model flags 5,000 claims. I must set the threshold so we only pass them the top 500 highest-probability cases.
>
> **2. Calculate the Cost of a False Positive (Member Friction):**
> If we flag a legitimate pharmacy claim as fraud, a sick member gets denied their medication at the pharmacy counter. This is a catastrophic user experience and risks regulatory fines. Therefore, the threshold for *auto-denying* a claim must be extremely high (e.g., 99% confidence). 
>
> **3. The Two-Tiered Approach (How I handled this at Chubb):**
> I wouldn't use just one threshold. I use a dual-threshold strategy:
> *   **Tier 1 (Extreme Confidence, >95%):** Auto-pend or auto-deny the claim. Zero human touch.
> *   **Tier 2 (Medium Confidence, 70%-94%):** Pay the claim so the member gets their medication, but route it to the async SIU queue for a retrospective investigation ('Pay and Chase'). 
> 
> By doing this, we protect the member experience (avoiding false positives at the pharmacy counter) while still catching fraud."

### Scenario 4: Buy vs. Build Strategy
**Question: "A business stakeholder wants us to buy an expensive off-the-shelf LLM product for clinical note summarization, but you think we should build it in-house using AWS Bedrock and open-source models. How do you make the business case?"**

**The Answer:**
> "I would structure the decision around Total Cost of Ownership (TCO), Data Privacy, and Customization.
>
> **1. Data Privacy & Compliance (The Trump Card in Healthcare):**
> If the off-the-shelf vendor does not offer a dedicated HIPAA-compliant environment with a strict BAA (Business Associate Agreement) and zero-data-retention guarantees, the conversation ends there. Building in-house on AWS Bedrock inside our own VPC ensures PHI never leaves our controlled environment.
>
> **2. Total Cost of Ownership (Unit Economics):**
> Off-the-shelf tools usually charge a SaaS subscription (e.g., PMPM or per-seat license). If we process 1 million clinical notes a day, the per-API-call cost of building it in-house on Bedrock using a fine-tuned lightweight model (like Llama 3 8B or Claude Haiku) will be exponentially cheaper at scale than paying a SaaS premium. I would model the break-even point.
>
> **3. Customization & Domain Specificity:**
> A generic LLM wrapper won't understand Optum's specific medical coding guidelines or proprietary formatting. By building in-house, we can use RAG over our own internal policy documents and fine-tune the model to output summaries in the exact SOAP format our doctors prefer.
>
> **4. The Compromise (Time to Market):**
> If the business needs it live in 2 weeks, building in-house might fail. I would propose a pilot: buy the vendor tool for 3 months for a small group of 50 doctors to validate that 'Clinical Summarization' actually improves their efficiency. While they pilot it, we build the bespoke, cheaper, HIPAA-compliant version in-house and swap it out once validated."
