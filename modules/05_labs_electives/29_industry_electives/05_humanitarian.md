## 1. Why this topic exists

A flood response manager must decide where to send a limited stock of household kits before the next storm.
Information is incomplete, roads open and close, and several agencies may serve the same locations.
People who cannot reach a distribution point may be invisible in the distribution register.
The physical shipment is therefore only one part of the decision.
An apparently efficient convoy can deliver the wrong item, arrive too late, or increase protection risks.

The affected person is the intended rights-holder and participant, not a unit of demand.
A humanitarian agency is usually the operational buyer and custodian of donated resources.
Its logistics team procures and moves supplies under delegated authority.
Program and protection teams define appropriate assistance and assess risks.
Local organizations and community representatives contribute contextual knowledge and feedback.
A donor may restrict the use of funds, but funding preferences are not a substitute for an impartial needs assessment.
The host government has responsibilities and authorities that depend on the context and applicable law.
A cluster or other coordination forum helps organizations exchange information; it does not automatically command them.

This topic focuses on a concrete decision: how many kits to pre-position and dispatch to each location during the next seven days.
The decision owner must distinguish people assessed, people estimated to need assistance, people targeted, kits dispatched, kits received, and people actually reached.
Each is a different quantity with different uncertainty and reporting rules.
A stock record is not evidence that assistance was appropriate or safely received.
A low number reached in an inaccessible area is not proof of low need.

Consequences of a poor decision include preventable delay, diversion, exclusion, household debt, damaged local markets, and exposure of sensitive information.
Moving supplies without community consultation can leave recipients with items they cannot use.
Publishing a precise map of a vulnerable group can create security risks even if the analyst intends to improve visibility.
Humanitarian logistics consequently combines analytical discipline with consent, access negotiation, program judgment, and accountability to affected people.

All numbers, places, organizations, and operational outcomes in the worked example and exercises are **synthetic** unless a public source is named.
The example is a teaching device, not a recommendation for an actual response.
Currency is U.S. dollars (USD); quantities are kits or households as specified; time is in days unless otherwise stated.
Regulatory discussion is educational, not legal advice; obtain qualified, context-specific advice before action.

## 3. The core mental model

```text
Affected people and local responders <---- feedback, consent, correction ----+
      |               |                                                     |
      v               v                                                     |
Needs evidence --> joint interpretation --> priority and modality decision  |
      |                 ^                      |                              |
      |                 | coordination         v                              |
      |           agency / local partner --> feasible allocation             |
      |                 ^                      |                              |
      |                 | stock / partner      v                              |
      +--> access, market, protection --> pre-position --> dispatch          |
                                                 |                           |
                                                 v                           |
                                         receipt --> safe distribution ------+
                                                 |
                                    verification and revised evidence
```

The diagram has four distinct flows.
People, supplies, and vehicles move through a physical network.
Needs, access, inventories, and commitments move through an information network.
Funds and restrictions move through financing and contracting arrangements.
Feedback, complaints, and decisions move through accountability channels.
None of these flows can be inferred reliably from another alone.

The response loop starts with uncertainty rather than with a complete order book.
A needs assessment draws from affected communities, local responders, administrative records, and other sources.
Each source has a coverage boundary and may miss households without phones, documentation, mobility, or political representation.
Assessment findings support a priority decision; they do not automatically determine it.
The decision may be to deliver kits, provide cash, support local services, defer a shipment, or collect more information.

A pre-positioning decision moves stock ahead of a possible demand shock.
A dispatch decision assigns available stock to a known route and destination.
A distribution decision concerns safe, appropriate receipt by eligible people.
A monitoring decision asks whether the intervention worked and whether the assumptions remain valid.
These decisions can have different owners, lead times, and evidence requirements.

Coordination reduces information gaps and unnecessary duplication when participants share consistent, timely data.
It does not turn reported activities into a guaranteed common operating picture.
Partner plans are not always funded commitments; commitments can be delayed or withdrawn.
A closed road is not necessarily a permanent barrier, and an open road is not necessarily safe for all users.
Access therefore has a time, geography, direction, mode, and confidence level.

Equity is not simply equal quantities per district.
An equal per-capita share may miss different severity, existing support, barriers, and household needs.
Conversely, a numerical severity score can encode historical exclusion if the assessment did not reach certain groups.
The response team should explain the allocation rule, check who is excluded, and provide a way to challenge decisions safely.

## 4. Definitions and vocabulary

| Term | Working definition and unit | Common misuse |
|---|---|---|
| Affected population | People exposed to the crisis in a defined area and period; persons | Treating all affected people as having identical needs |
| People in need | Estimated people requiring assistance under stated criteria; persons | Equating assessed people with all people in need |
| Target population | People the response intends to assist, subject to stated selection and resources; persons | Treating the target as assistance delivered |
| Household | Locally defined co-resident or shared-economy unit; households | Assuming identical size or composition everywhere |
| Assessment coverage | Share of a defined population or geography represented by a data collection method; percent | Calling convenience-sample reach a representative sample |
| Severity | Categorized intensity of need under defined indicators and thresholds | Treating an opaque composite score as an objective fact |
| Gap | Estimated requirement less eligible, non-overlapping assistance available in the same period and unit | Subtracting unverified partner intentions as delivered aid |
| Unmet need | Need not adequately addressed; households or persons | Equating it with demand observed at a distribution site |
| Response plan | Chosen course of action, roles, resources, and period | Treating a plan as a funded commitment |
| Commitment | An authorized, resource-backed promise with quantity, destination, and dates | Counting a tentative pledge as available stock |
| Forecast | Conditional estimate of a future outcome | Confusing forecast with target or guaranteed supply |
| Target | Desired outcome or planned coverage, not necessarily achieved | Calling a target an observed result |
| Pre-positioning | Placement of stock before a possible disruption or request | Counting stock near a border as delivered to households |
| Pipeline | Ordered, in-transit, received, and allocated goods tracked by status | Adding all statuses as if simultaneously available |
| Available-to-promise | Usable stock not already reserved or restricted; kits | Using physical count without expiry, ownership, or quality checks |
| Lead time | Elapsed time between defined process events; days | Using one average for open and closed routes |
| Access | Practical ability of people and assistance to move safely and legally to and from locations | Reducing access to road condition alone |
| Access constraint | Security, administrative, physical, social, or other barrier with a specified window | Assuming a road-open report implies a safe distribution |
| Route capacity | Deliverable volume along a route during a stated interval; kits per week | Confusing truck capacity with last-mile capacity |
| Cluster | Coordination arrangement for organizations in a humanitarian sector where activated | Assuming a cluster is a universal command hierarchy |
| 3W/4W/5W | Who does what, where, with optional when and for whom; reporting schema varies | Assuming all organizations report the same definitions |
| Logistics Cluster | Logistics-sector coordination and common-services mechanism where activated | Assuming it owns all agency stock or determines beneficiary eligibility |
| Deduplication | Identifying records referring to the same operational entity or assistance event under a defined rule | Merging people based only on a similar name |
| Disaggregation | Reporting data by meaningful groups where safe and feasible | Publishing small sensitive counts without disclosure review |
| Modality | Method of assistance, such as in-kind goods, cash, vouchers, or services | Assuming cash and in-kind are interchangeable in every market |
| Market assessment | Analysis of supply, prices, functionality, access, and risks relevant to assistance | Inferring a functioning market from a single open shop |
| Accountability to affected people | Commitments and practices for participation, information, feedback, and response | Counting complaints without acting on them |
| Protection | Efforts to reduce risks to people's safety, dignity, and rights | Treating protection as a separate box after route planning |
| Impartiality | Humanitarian action based on need without adverse distinction | Giving all places equal quantities regardless of need |
| Neutrality | Humanitarian actors do not take sides in hostilities or relevant controversies | Assuming neutrality eliminates all negotiation or legal obligations |
| Independence | Humanitarian objectives are autonomous from political, economic, military, or other objectives | Assuming operational partnerships are always impossible |
| Humanity | Addressing human suffering and protecting life, health, and dignity | Optimizing cost without considering harm |
| Minimum standard | Published sector benchmark intended to guide appropriate response in context | Treating every benchmark as automatically binding law |
| PDM | Post-distribution monitoring of receipt, use, quality, safety, and feedback | Declaring impact from a dispatch note |
| Equity | Fair attention to need, barriers, and consequences across groups | Enforcing a uniform numerical split without context |
| Data minimization | Collecting and sharing only data necessary for a specified purpose | Publishing names because a dashboard supports them |
| Confidence | Qualitative or quantitative assessment of evidence strength under a method | Presenting a judgmental score as a calibrated probability |
| Scenario | Coherent set of assumptions for a possible future state | Treating a single scenario as a prediction |
| Regret | Difference between an outcome and the best feasible outcome for the realized scenario | Calling a speculative model output a measured loss |

## 5. The mechanics, step by step

### Step 1 — Frame the decision and authority

Write a question that a named person can answer by a named deadline.
For example: the response manager must authorize a seven-day kit dispatch by 14:00 on Monday.
The manager can authorize stock already under the agency's control but cannot assign another agency's inventory without agreement.
Local partners advise on feasible distribution, cultural suitability, and protection risks.
The logistics manager confirms inventory, vehicle, and route constraints.
The program lead validates eligibility and appropriateness.
The protection lead reviews safe access and whether the plan exposes people to harm.
The finance or grants owner checks eligible funding and restrictions.

The input is a mandate, decision horizon, and resource envelope.
The output is a decision record with an owner, delegated authority, reviewers, and escalation path.
A decision without a named owner is an analysis, not an operational commitment.
If a legal or safety authority has not cleared the route, the model must not authorize travel.
If consent or safe collection cannot be established, restrict or defer sensitive data collection.

Separate these quantities in the brief:

- **Forecast:** possible households requiring kits during the next seven days, with uncertainty.
- **Plan:** quantities proposed for each destination and expected movement dates.
- **Target:** desired coverage, explicitly subject to access and funding.
- **Commitment:** approved, funded, allocated quantity with a named delivery owner.
- **Actual:** verified receipt and distribution, recorded separately from dispatch.

A change in access can invalidate the plan without making the original forecast dishonest.
A donor pledge is not stock until procurement and receipt conditions are satisfied.
The difference between desired coverage and available resources must remain visible.

### Step 2 — Define population, geography, and reference time

Choose the geographic unit in consultation with people who know the response area.
Administrative boundaries help coordinate reports but can obscure displaced people crossing borders.
Locations should have a stable code, name, boundary version, and reporting date.
Record whether a count is for residents, displaced households, host households, or another defined group.
Record a denominator source, age, and known exclusions.
A count taken before displacement is not necessarily a valid current denominator.

Choose one planning unit.
This chapter uses one kit per eligible household for a seven-day allocation exercise.
In a real operation, the content and adequacy of a kit depend on household composition, need, and context.
Do not convert persons to households using a single fixed factor without checking local household composition.
Do not silently mix households, persons, kits, and distribution events.

The input is a population registry or estimate plus geographic definitions.
The output is a dated denominator table with a documented scope and uncertainty.
If households move across areas during the week, use a reference date and make transfers explicit.
Where no reliable denominator exists, report a range and explain how decisions change across plausible values.

### Step 3 — Gather and triangulate needs evidence

Start with existing information to avoid repeated, extractive questioning.
Invite local organizations and community representatives to identify missing groups and appropriate languages.
Use multiple methods where feasible: household interviews, key informants, direct observation, remote evidence, and service records.
Each method observes different things and creates different selection biases.
A convenience sample reached by phone can exclude people without reliable phone access.
A distribution register observes participants, not all people with unmet needs.
A key informant can describe a community but should not be assumed to represent every group.

For each observation, retain collection date, method, geography, source, unit, scope, and quality notes.
Record whether the finding is direct observation, respondent report, partner estimate, or analyst inference.
Ask whether an apparent absence of need is actually an absence of access to the assessment.
Check accessibility for people with disabilities, older people, women, displaced groups, and people speaking minority languages without assuming their needs are uniform.
Obtain meaningful consent for personal data collection and offer safe alternatives where possible.

The input is the best available evidence with a documented collection boundary.
The output is a needs estimate, uncertainty range or qualitative confidence, and a list of excluded populations.
Where sources conflict, investigate definitions and dates before averaging.
If the conflict cannot be resolved before the deadline, present separate scenarios and identify the consequence of each.
Never fabricate a confidence interval from a non-probability sample.

### Step 4 — Assess severity, barriers, and priority

Define severity indicators before reviewing location rankings where practical.
Examples include inability to meet essential needs, exposure to weather, lack of alternative support, and protection risks.
Use locally appropriate criteria and validate them with program and protection staff and affected communities.
A score can structure discussion, but its weights are policy choices, not natural constants.
A missing observation should be labeled unknown; it is not equivalent to zero severity.

Identify barriers to assistance separately from severity.
A household may be eligible but unable to travel, unable to stand in a queue, or unable to understand the registration language.
A location can have low reported cases because access is blocked.
Record potential accommodations, such as accessible sites, outreach, safe referrals, and community-agreed delivery times.
Avoid publishing subgroup detail where it could identify or endanger people.

The input is needs evidence, agreed criteria, and a barrier assessment.
The output is a transparent prioritization rule and a record of its exclusions and contested judgments.
If severity differs substantially within a district, use a finer unit only where data quality and safety permit.
If one group is missing from the sample, do not rank it as low priority merely because its observed count is low.

### Step 5 — Reconcile overlap and estimate the gap

Bring agencies' location, activity, date, unit, status, and population definitions into a common table.
Clarify whether reported quantities are planned, funded, dispatched, received, or distributed.
A promised kit should not be subtracted as if it is already in a household's possession.
Align the time window and item definition before comparing records.
A water container and a shelter kit should not be deduplicated as if they were identical assistance.

Subtract only assistance that is relevant, credible, and non-overlapping under the chosen planning rule.
If individual deduplication is necessary and lawful, limit access, document matching errors, and provide a correction process.
A name and village alone are not a reliable unique identifier.
False matches can exclude households; missed matches can overstate unique coverage.
Where matching is unsafe or data are not shareable, use aggregate ranges and acknowledge remaining uncertainty.

A simple planning formula is:

```text
residual eligible households = max(0, estimated eligible households
                                  - verified non-overlapping coverage
                                  - reliable near-term committed coverage)
```

Keep verified coverage and committed coverage in separate columns even if both are subtracted in the planning case.
If a commitment fails, restore that amount to the gap and trigger a replan.
Count a partner commitment only when its recipient population, quantity, time window, and likelihood of delivery are understood.
This is a planning assumption, not a claim that those households have received support.

The input is a reconciled needs and partner-coverage table.
The output is a gap by geography and a coverage-status ledger.
If the same partner reports across two coordination platforms, reconcile the underlying activity before summing.
If different partners may serve the same household but no safe matching is possible, use an overlap range rather than assert a precise count.

### Step 6 — Choose the assistance modality and product

Ask what affected people prefer and what outcome the response aims to achieve.
In-kind kits can help where essential goods are unavailable or immediate delivery is necessary.
Cash or vouchers may offer choice where markets, payment mechanisms, and safe access function adequately.
Services may be more appropriate when goods alone do not solve the problem.
A combination can be appropriate if roles and duplication are understood.

Check local supply, trader capacity, prices, market access, gendered control of resources, security, and exclusion from payment channels.
Consider whether a large local purchase could raise prices or remove goods from other households.
Consider whether an imported item will arrive after the need window or conflict with local standards.
Check cultural appropriateness, usability, disability access, and whether households can transport or store the item.
Record the rationale for the chosen modality and who was consulted.

The input is the needs assessment, market evidence, operational options, and program safeguards.
The output is an agreed assistance specification with a review date.
If markets fail, cash cannot be assumed to procure unavailable goods.
If goods are available locally and people can access them safely, an in-kind-only plan requires justification.
The logistics model must not decide a modality solely because its shipment cost is easy to compute.

### Step 7 — Establish usable supply and funding

Start with a stock ledger by location, item, quantity, owner, condition, expiry, and restriction.
Reconcile physical counts with warehouse records and goods-received notes.
Subtract quarantine, damaged goods, already committed stock, and stock whose funding cannot legally support the intended use.
Separate confirmed stock from orders, supplier promises, and inbound shipments.
Count inbound goods as available only after the decision rule's required milestones are met.

For each procurement option, record supplier capacity, lead-time distribution, payment terms, quality checks, and delivery destination.
Do not assume international procurement is always faster or local procurement always safe for the market.
Examine border processes, customs documentation, and storage conditions.
Funding currency, expiry date, eligible cost categories, and geographic restrictions affect feasibility.
A donor restriction is an operational constraint to review, not proof that a distribution would be impartial.
Escalate conflicts between a restriction and humanitarian principles to authorized decision-makers.

The input is stock, procurement, and funding evidence as of a specified timestamp.
The output is available-to-promise stock plus a dated supply pipeline and risk register.
If inventory accuracy is weak, reduce the quantity treated as confirmed or conduct a count.
If stock is reserved for another emergency, obtain explicit release before reallocation.

### Step 8 — Model routes and access as time-dependent constraints

Map origin, transshipment point, destination, carrier, mode, and alternative routes.
Separate road passability from security clearance, administrative authorization, community acceptance, and last-mile accessibility.
A route may be usable outbound but unavailable for a safe return.
A bridge may allow small vehicles but prohibit heavy trucks.
A distribution site may be physically reachable for staff while inaccessible to some recipients.

Record who verified each access report, when, for which mode, and with what uncertainty.
Use an expiry time on access information because a reliable report can become stale rapidly.
Do not collect or publish sensitive movement or population information more precisely than necessary.
Keep personal and operational security controls proportionate to the situation.

Model a route cap as the quantity that can arrive within the chosen decision period, not the vehicle's nominal payload alone.
For example, two vehicles of 80 kits each do not imply 160 kits delivered if only one approved trip is possible.
Allow for loading, checkpoints, unloading, weather, staff availability, and return travel.
Where practical, represent a route as open, constrained, or closed in several scenarios.
Do not encode an unsafe or unauthorized route as a high-cost alternative: it is unavailable until cleared.

The input is a dated network and access evidence.
The output is a set of feasible route capacities and escalation triggers.
If safety clearance lapses, stop movement and return the stock to the available ledger.
If the only access option would expose recipients to unreasonable harm, seek a different delivery mechanism rather than optimizing through the constraint.

### Step 9 — Compare pre-positioning choices

A pre-positioning decision trades earlier availability against holding cost, expiry, relocation, damage, and opportunity cost.
Define candidate hubs that are actually available, secure enough, and authorized for the item.
For each hub, estimate lead time and deliverable capacity to each area under each scenario.
Represent supplies, transport, warehouses, and budgets as constraints.
Represent priority as a transparent policy decision rather than an unexplained coefficient.

An expected-value comparison can multiply each scenario's outcome by its probability and sum the results.
Use scenario probabilities only when there is a defensible basis and disclose their source.
If probabilities are not credible, compare a small set of scenarios without pretending they are calibrated.
Also show the worst plausible shortfall and who bears it; an average can hide an excluded area.
A robust choice may sacrifice a small amount of expected speed to preserve access in a difficult scenario.

The input is candidate hub capacity, usable inventory, route scenarios, and response objectives.
The output is a recommended pre-position quantity, alternative, assumptions, and reversibility.
Do not count goods already promised elsewhere as stock for both emergencies.
Do not pre-position perishables without checking the full storage and expiry requirements.
If a decision is reversible before storm onset, set a review point rather than pretending to know the final route now.

### Step 10 — Allocate scarce stock

Translate the agreed policy into a rule that can be explained to community representatives.
A simple proportional rule allocates stock according to residual need, subject to route limits.
A severity-weighted rule can prioritize higher-severity need but requires justified weights and an exclusion check.
A minimum-service rule can reserve a feasible minimum for each area, but may be infeasible under scarcity.
No rule resolves all ethical choices; present who benefits and who remains unserved.

For each area, cap allocations by residual need and route capacity.
Cap the total by usable available stock.
Use integer kits and a documented rounding procedure.
If an area reaches a route cap, redistribute unused supply only among areas with residual feasible capacity.
Record unallocated stock separately from unmet need; closed routes can make both positive simultaneously.
Never count a reserved kit as a delivered kit.

The input is the reconciled gap, access capacity, stock, and approved policy.
The output is a feasible proposal plus unmet need by area and a route-cap explanation.
A human decision-maker reviews distributional effects and can override the proposal with a documented reason.
If a minimum allocation conflicts with safety or available stock, escalate rather than silently violating a constraint.

### Step 11 — Coordinate, commit, and dispatch

Participate in the relevant coordination mechanisms appropriate to the response.
Confirm which organization is responsible for each activity and what information can safely be shared.
Exchange aggregate activity and stock status under agreed definitions and permissions.
A logistics common service, where offered, may transport an agency's cargo without taking ownership of its program or beneficiary decisions.
A cluster report can reveal potential overlap, but a reporting gap does not prove no activity exists.

Create a movement order with item, lot where relevant, owner, quantity, origin, destination, date, and handover contacts.
Reconcile warehouse issue notes, carrier documents, receiving records, and incident reports.
A signed receipt confirms a handover at the specified location; it does not prove households received usable assistance.
Define a process for shortages, damage, returns, security incidents, and revised arrival times.
Do not release a recipient list to a carrier simply because the carrier moves cargo.

The input is the approved allocation and confirmed operational clearances.
The output is a dated, resource-backed commitment and auditable custody trail.
If a partner cannot honor a commitment, report the change promptly and reopen the gap calculation.
If delivery is delayed, communicate a realistic update through accessible channels.

### Step 12 — Distribute safely and close the feedback loop

Agree distribution arrangements with communities and responsible partners.
Explain eligibility, quantities, timing, available accommodations, and how decisions can be challenged.
Use formats and languages that the intended recipients can actually access.
Offer confidential channels for complaints, including sensitive safeguarding concerns, with trained handling and referral.
Do not promise anonymity where the system cannot provide it.

Verify quantities received, distributed, damaged, returned, and remaining.
Reconcile physical stock without assuming every signature corresponds to a unique or satisfied household.
Use PDM to ask whether the assistance was received, appropriate, safe, and sufficient for its intended purpose.
Sample people who did not attend or could not access distribution where safe and feasible.
Do not infer representativeness from a sample of those easiest to contact.

The input is distribution evidence, complaint information, and access updates.
The output is a reconciled result, corrective actions, and revised decisions.
Set owners and deadlines for complaints and communicate aggregate action back to communities without exposing complainants.
A serious protection incident can require suspension and referral, not merely a dashboard annotation.
Monitor whether the correction reaches the people who experienced the original barrier.

### Step 13 — Reassess and learn

Set triggers before the first shipment: route closure, new displacement, a partner commitment failure, price shock, stock discrepancy, or protection incident.
A trigger names the threshold, observer, decision owner, and deadline for review.
For example, a new route cap below the planned quantity triggers a same-day allocation review.
A change in estimated need alone does not require a false precision claim; it requires a documented comparison with the previous estimate.

Compare forecast to actual receipt and distribution with the same population definition and time window.
Investigate whether the difference comes from need, stock, route, program eligibility, or reporting error.
Record decisions that were reasonable with information available at the time separately from outcomes caused by later surprises.
Update assumptions and data access permissions when the purpose or operating context changes.
Share lessons with local partners and communities in safe, useful form.

## 6. Worked example

### Decision and synthetic context

A synthetic flood response serves three districts: Alder, Birch, and Cedar.
The agency has 900 usable household kits at the Central warehouse on Monday morning.
Each kit is assumed appropriate for one eligible household for this exercise only.
A response manager must commit dispatch quantities for the next seven days.
The plan uses one kit per eligible household, integer quantities, and no within-week replenishment.
The distances, figures, and district names are invented; no real population or response is represented.

The table below uses current estimated eligible households rather than the entire affected population.
Partner commitments are confirmed for the same item and period, but are not yet delivered.
The agency and partners have agreed that these commitments refer to distinct household lists for this scenario.
That assumption must be verified in practice, under a lawful and safe data-sharing arrangement.

| District | Estimated eligible households | Verified prior coverage | Partner committed coverage | Planning residual |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Alder | 800 | 100 | 100 | 600 |
| Birch | 600 | 50 | 50 | 500 |
| Cedar | 400 | 20 | 80 | 300 |
| **Total** | **1,800** | **170** | **230** | **1,400** |

Alder's residual is 800 − 100 − 100 = 600 households.
Birch's residual is 600 − 50 − 50 = 500 households.
Cedar's residual is 400 − 20 − 80 = 300 households.
The total planning gap is 1,400 households, not 1,630, because this planning scenario subtracts both coverage and reliable commitments.
The delivered gap before partner fulfillment is 1,800 − 170 = 1,630 households.
These two numbers answer different questions and must not be substituted for one another.

The partner's 230 kits are commitments, not verified distribution.
If the commitments fail, the response has a larger unaddressed need than the planning residual implies.
The 900 kits at Central are a physical stock figure after damage, reservation, and funding checks.
They are available-to-promise, not yet dispatched or distributed.
The 1,400-household gap is an estimate, not a verified count of unique recipients.

### Constraints and policy

The program lead proposes proportional allocation based on residual eligible households.
The protection lead notes that Cedar's access information is the least reliable and requests an active review rather than treating its route cap as permanent.
The route assessments support at most 500 kits to Alder, 400 to Birch, and 120 to Cedar during the week.
These capacities include approved trips and last-mile handover; they are not just truck payloads.
All three are assumptions for the synthetic case.
The sum of capacities is 1,020 kits, greater than the 900 kits available.
Cedar's 120-kit limit is nevertheless binding for an unconstrained proportional plan.

The desired target is full coverage of the 1,400-household planning gap if resources and access allow.
The forecast is that these 1,400 households may require support during the week under the assessment and commitment assumptions.
The proposed plan assigns at most 900 kits, constrained by route caps.
A commitment exists only after the authorized response manager and responsible partners approve the proposal.
Nothing in the calculation proves that the commitment was fulfilled.

### First allocation pass

Without route limits, proportional shares of the 900 kits are:

```text
Alder: 900 × 600 / 1,400 = 385.714 kits
Birch: 900 × 500 / 1,400 = 321.429 kits
Cedar: 900 × 300 / 1,400 = 192.857 kits
```

Alder and Birch are within their route caps.
Cedar's calculated 192.857 kits exceed its 120-kit cap.
Assign Cedar 120 kits in this planning pass, leaving 780 kits for Alder and Birch.
The remaining residual needs are 600 and 500 households respectively.
The proportional shares of the 780 kits are:

```text
Alder: 780 × 600 / 1,100 = 425.4545... kits
Birch: 780 × 500 / 1,100 = 354.5454... kits
```

Flooring produces 425 kits for Alder and 354 kits for Birch, leaving one kit.
The largest fractional remainder is Birch's 0.5454..., so Birch receives the final kit.
The proposed integer allocation is Alder 425, Birch 355, and Cedar 120 kits.

| District | Gap before agency delivery | Route cap | Planned kits | Planning gap after planned kits | Planned share of gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Alder | 600 | 500 | 425 | 175 | 70.8% |
| Birch | 500 | 400 | 355 | 145 | 71.0% |
| Cedar | 300 | 120 | 120 | 180 | 40.0% |
| **Total** | **1,400** | **1,020** | **900** | **500** | **64.3%** |

The planned shares are rounded to one decimal place.
The remaining 500-household gap is conditional on both partner commitments and agency deliveries succeeding.
Before verification, it is not a measured count of households left unassisted.
The gap will rise if goods are damaged, a partner defaults, or eligible need is higher than estimated.
The result is feasible because no district exceeds its gap or route capacity and the total is 900 kits.
It is not necessarily equitable: Cedar's lower planned coverage is caused by an access constraint that deserves a separate response.

### Test an access improvement

Suppose a local partner confirms a safe, approved additional route that increases Cedar's cap from 120 to 200 kits.
The unconstrained proportional result now fits all three route caps.
Flooring gives 385, 321, and 192 kits, with two kits remaining.
Cedar has the largest remainder, followed by Alder, yielding 386, 321, and 193 kits.
The total stays 900 kits, and the residuals become 214, 179, and 107 households.
The access improvement shifts 73 kits toward Cedar relative to the base plan.
It does not create additional stock; Alder and Birch receive 39 and 34 fewer kits respectively.

## 7. Code, query, or calculation

This Python 3 lab uses only the standard library.
Copy the full block into `humanitarian_allocation.py` and run `python humanitarian_allocation.py`.
It reproduces the synthetic base allocation using constrained proportional shares and largest-remainder rounding.
The algorithm repeatedly fixes any area whose proportional share exceeds its effective cap, then redistributes the remaining stock.
Effective capacity is the smaller of the residual need and route capacity.
When no cap binds, remaining kits are assigned by fractional remainder; ties are broken by district name.
This deterministic rule is a teaching policy, not a universal humanitarian allocation standard.

```python
from fractions import Fraction


def allocate(gaps, routes, stock):
    """Return integer allocations under route, gap, and stock limits."""
    if set(gaps) != set(routes):
        raise ValueError("gaps and routes must have the same district keys")
    if stock < 0 or any(x < 0 for x in gaps.values()) or any(x < 0 for x in routes.values()):
        raise ValueError("all values must be nonnegative")
    if not all(isinstance(x, int) for x in [stock, *gaps.values(), *routes.values()]):
        raise ValueError("all quantities must be integer kits")

    caps = {name: min(gaps[name], routes[name]) for name in gaps}
    result = {name: 0 for name in gaps}
    remaining = min(stock, sum(caps.values()))
    active = {name for name in gaps if caps[name] > 0 and gaps[name] > 0}

    while active and remaining:
        weight = sum(gaps[name] for name in active)
        shares = {name: Fraction(remaining * gaps[name], weight) for name in active}
        binding = {name for name in active if shares[name] >= caps[name]}
        if binding:
            for name in binding:
                result[name] = caps[name]
                remaining -= caps[name]
            active -= binding
            continue

        for name in active:
            result[name] = shares[name].numerator // shares[name].denominator
        remainder = remaining - sum(result[name] for name in active)
        ranking = sorted(
            active,
            key=lambda name: (-(shares[name] - result[name]), name),
        )
        for name in ranking[:remainder]:
            result[name] += 1
        remaining = 0

    return result


def report(gaps, routes, stock):
    plan = allocate(gaps, routes, stock)
    print("District | Gap | Route cap | Planned | Remaining gap")
    for name in sorted(gaps):
        print(f"{name:8} | {gaps[name]:3} | {routes[name]:9} | "
              f"{plan[name]:7} | {gaps[name] - plan[name]:13}")
    print(f"Total planned: {sum(plan.values())} kits")
    print(f"Unallocated stock: {stock - sum(plan.values())} kits")
    print(f"Conditional remaining gap: {sum(gaps.values()) - sum(plan.values())} households")
    return plan


if __name__ == "__main__":
    gaps = {"Alder": 600, "Birch": 500, "Cedar": 300}
    routes = {"Alder": 500, "Birch": 400, "Cedar": 120}
    report(gaps, routes, 900)
```

Expected output:

```text
District | Gap | Route cap | Planned | Remaining gap
Alder    | 600 |       500 |     425 |           175
Birch    | 500 |       400 |     355 |           145
Cedar    | 300 |       120 |     120 |           180
Total planned: 900 kits
Unallocated stock: 0 kits
Conditional remaining gap: 500 households
```

The program takes already-reconciled residual gaps; it does not deduplicate household records.
It treats every eligible household as having the same weight.
It does not model time within the week, costs, protection, modality, preferences, or uncertainty.
It is not a general solver for multi-commodity routing or a real-time dispatch system.
The validation prevents negative or fractional input, but it does not verify whether the source data are credible.
The output is a proposed allocation, not evidence of safe receipt or a binding commitment.

Use aggregate identifiers in shared dashboards where record-level data are unnecessary.

Data quality includes timeliness, completeness, consistency, validity, and fitness for the decision.

Document corrections without overwriting the evidence that supported earlier decisions.

### Coordination conventions and humanitarian standards

OCHA's humanitarian coordination resources describe the cluster approach and the role of coordinating organizations by sector.
Whether a cluster is activated, which agencies lead it, and the local coordination architecture depend on the response.
The Logistics Cluster describes its coordination, information-management, and common-services functions on its public site.
These mechanisms support participating organizations; they do not imply that all agencies share one stock ledger or command structure.
A 3W, 4W, or 5W product organizes operational reporting, but field meanings and update cycles must be agreed locally.

The Sphere Handbook presents the Humanitarian Charter, Protection Principles, Core Humanitarian Standard, and minimum standards for selected humanitarian sectors.
Its indicators are used in context and require professional interpretation; they are not automatically statutes.
The Core Humanitarian Standard on Quality and Accountability states commitments to people affected by crisis.
An organization's adoption of a standard is distinct from a legal obligation or verified compliance.
Use the current edition applicable to the response rather than treating a classroom summary as a substitute.

Humanitarian principles of humanity, neutrality, impartiality, and independence are explained by OCHA.
The principles inform operational choices and access; their precise legal implications depend on context and applicable law.
They do not remove the need for safety review or give any organization blanket authorization to cross a border.
A calculation that treats need impartially can still result in unequal outcomes if access or data coverage is unequal.

### Law, funding, and sensitive information

International humanitarian law applies in situations of armed conflict under conditions specified by that law.
It is not interchangeable with humanitarian principles, and not every flood, emergency, or logistics operation is an armed conflict.
The ICRC's public international humanitarian law resources explain the relevant framework and protections.
Whether and how particular obligations apply requires legal analysis of the actual parties, territory, and facts.
National law can govern imports, customs, employment, transport, data protection, and other aspects of the response.
Sanctions and counterterrorism rules, including any applicable exceptions, require current expert review; do not assume a blanket exemption.
Donor agreements add contractual conditions but do not replace law or a protection assessment.

The ICRC/Brussels Privacy Hub Handbook on Data Protection in Humanitarian Action describes practical data-protection considerations.
Before collecting personal information, identify a legitimate purpose, lawful basis where applicable, retention period, security controls, and responsible controller or equivalent role.
Consent can be difficult to make meaningful when a person depends on assistance; do not assume a signature eliminates power imbalance.
Use the minimum information needed and provide accessible notice and a correction route.
Assess risks of sharing sensitive location, identity, disability, or protection information.
A hash of a name is not automatically anonymous, particularly when the possible names are predictable.
Safe aggregate reporting may still require suppression and review of repeated releases.

### Standards do not replace operational judgment

A standard may describe desired practice without specifying the safe choice for every road or household.
A legal requirement can differ across jurisdictions and can change over time.
An agency's internal standard operating procedure can be stricter than a donor's minimum.
Check the source, version, scope, and applicable authority before calling a rule mandatory.
Record unresolved interpretation questions and consult the appropriate specialist.

### Documented practice: emergency market analysis

The International Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement's *Market Analysis Guidance* provides a framework for considering markets in humanitarian response.

### Documented practice: accountability and quality

The Core Humanitarian Standard describes commitments that organizations can use to improve the quality and accountability of their work.

### Research evidence: disaster pre-positioning

### Synthetic application: tracing a discrepancy

The following invented custody record concerns 100 shelter kits sent to a partner warehouse.
The origin warehouse issues 100 kits on Tuesday.
The carrier acknowledges 100 kits at collection.
The receiving warehouse signs for 96 kits on Wednesday and records two damaged kits.
For clarity, the 96 received includes the two damaged kits.
Ninety-four kits are therefore recorded as received in usable condition.

The custody reconciliation is:

```text
100 issued = 96 received + 4 not accounted for at handover
96 received = 94 usable + 2 damaged
```

The four missing kits and the two damaged kits are different categories.
Counting only 94 usable kits as received and then subtracting damage again would double-count the damaged kits.
The six kits not usable at the receiving warehouse comprise four missing plus two damaged.
The case does not establish theft; a counting error, transit incident, paperwork error, or other cause is possible.

### Synthetic application: linked displacement and access scenarios

A simple risk model might assume high displacement and road closure occur independently.
Suppose each is assigned a 50% probability.
The independent model assigns a 25% probability to both occurring together.
That answer follows from the independence assumption, not from the two marginal percentages alone.
A major storm can plausibly cause both events, making independence a questionable operational assumption.

Consider an alternative synthetic joint distribution.
High displacement with a closed road has probability 40%.
High displacement with an open road has probability 10%.
Low displacement with a closed road has probability 10%.
Low displacement with an open road has probability 40%.
The probabilities sum to 100%, and each marginal remains 50%.
The joint high-need and closure scenario now has probability 40%, not 25%.

The two models agree on each marginal but disagree materially about the most stressful combined event.
If a hub works only when roads are open, the correlated scenario may expose more risk than the independent calculation suggests.
Neither probability table is an observed estimate for a real crisis.
The example demonstrates why the source and structure of scenario assumptions matter.
If evidence cannot support probabilities, compare the four coherent states without assigning numerical likelihoods.
Decision-makers should see both the response capacity and the affected groups in each state.

## 10. Failure modes and red flags

| Failure | Early warning | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Missing inaccessible groups | No assessment records from blocked areas | Access and observation are correlated | Need is systematically understated | Flag unknown areas, use safe indirect evidence, prioritize access review |
| Double-counted coverage | Same activity appears in several reports | Partners report through multiple channels | Gap appears smaller than reality | Reconcile event IDs, status, dates, and overlap assumptions |
| Planned equals delivered | Dashboard counts all pledges as completed | Statuses are collapsed | Households remain unsupported | Keep plan, commitment, dispatch, receipt, and distribution separate |
| Stale route report | Access data lack a timestamp | Conditions change rapidly | Unsafe travel or failed delivery | Require clearance, expiry, and revalidation |
| Stock not usable | Ledger includes expired or reserved goods | Physical count differs from available-to-promise | Broken promises and shortages | Reconcile condition, ownership, restrictions, and reservations |
| False precision | Need shown to the household despite weak sampling | Model hides uncertainty | Misleading rankings | Publish ranges or scenarios and missing-data notes |
| Unfair priority rule | Score weights have no rationale | Power or historical data shape rankings | Exclusion of high-need groups | Review criteria with communities and protection staff |
| Inaccessible distribution | Low attendance by some groups | Site, timing, or registration creates barriers | Planned assistance does not reach people | Consult, adapt, monitor non-attendance, and offer safe alternatives |
| Modality mismatch | Recipients sell or discard kits | Contents do not match needs | Waste, debt, or dignity harms | Consult users and compare cash, services, and in-kind options |
| Market harm | Price spike follows bulk purchasing | Demand exceeds local supply capacity | Non-recipients lose access | Assess market capacity and monitor prices |
| Data exposure | Detailed recipient file emailed widely | Unnecessary personal data sharing | Discrimination or physical risk | Minimize, restrict, encrypt as appropriate, and assess disclosure risk |
| Incorrect linkage | Similar names merged | Weak identifiers and matching rule | Eligible household excluded | Test errors, limit use, and provide correction process |
| Phantom equity | Equal district allocation presented as impartial | Need and barriers differ | Unequal unmet need | Compare residuals and coverage across relevant groups |
| Route cap treated as fate | Low coverage in one area is never escalated | Access constraint becomes an allocation excuse | Persistent exclusion | Assign an access owner and review trigger |
| Complaints without response | Cases accumulate with no closure owner | Feedback is measured but not acted upon | Loss of trust and repeated harm | Set response deadlines and safe referral procedures |
| Unauthorized movement | Model selects route without clearance | Feasibility uses cost but omits safety authority | Harm or legal exposure | Treat clearance as hard prerequisite and stop if it lapses |
| Funding mismatch | Grant expiry precedes planned delivery | Financial restriction ignored | Ineligible expenditure or disrupted delivery | Review grant conditions before commitment |
| Spurious impact | Dispatch volume labeled households helped | Output confused with outcome | Ineffective response persists | Verify receipt, use, and safety through PDM |
| Gender or disability proxy | Group membership alone sets a score | Stereotypes replace specific barriers | Stigma or wrong prioritization | Ask about needs and barriers safely; disaggregate carefully |
| Scenario independence | Storm demand rises while road failure is modeled independently | Common cause omitted | Risk understated | Use coherent joint scenarios and stress tests |

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why needed here | What breaks it | Test or response |
|---|---|---|---|
| One kit per eligible household | Makes needs and supply commensurate | Household composition or kit suitability varies | Validate kit specification and adapt program design |
| Gaps refer to the same seven days | Makes subtraction meaningful | Reports cover different periods | Align dates or report separately |
| Partner commitments do not overlap | Avoids subtracting the same household twice | Shared target lists or geographic overlap | Verify safely or use an overlap range |
| Partner commitments are reliable | Allows planning residual calculation | Funding, procurement, or access fails | Track status and restore failed quantities to gap |
| Prior coverage is adequate | Treats earlier support as satisfying current need | Item was damaged, insufficient, or consumed | Verify content, timing, and adequacy |
| 900 kits are available-to-promise | Caps the agency plan | Quarantine, reservation, expiry, or ledger error | Reconcile physical and financial stock |
| Route capacities are safe and authorized | Defines feasible movement | Security or administrative situation changes | Time-limit clearance and replan on change |
| Capacity is independent across districts | Permits separate caps | Shared vehicles, bridge, or staff limit total trips | Add shared network constraints |
| Gap weights represent agreed priority | Enables proportional allocation | Severity or barriers differ | Compare alternatives and document ethical decision |
| Locations are internally homogeneous | Allows district-level allocation | Severe pockets or exclusion within districts | Review finer safe data and community feedback |
| Scenario probabilities are meaningful | Supports expected-value comparison | Probabilities are judgmental or unstable | Present scenario outcomes and sensitivity separately |
| All 300 additional kits fit either hub | Makes the hub comparison feasible | Space, quality, or funding constraints differ | Check warehouse capacity and restrictions |
| Household estimates are current | Supports planning denominator | New displacement or return occurs | Update estimates and track uncertainty |
| Planned kit equals actual delivery | Allows conditional remaining-gap arithmetic | Loss, delay, refusal, or diversion occurs | Reconcile receipt, distribution, and PDM |
| Results reveal needs equitably | Allows use of observed evidence | Some groups cannot participate safely | Assess coverage and use alternative safe methods |

These assumptions are not claims about an actual operation.
A decision-maker should sign off the assumptions that materially change the allocation.
A missing legal or safety clearance is not an uncertainty to average away.
The appropriate response may be to pause, seek a different route, or choose another modality.

## 13. Self-check answers

### Exercise 1

A satisfactory map places affected people and local responders at both the assessment and feedback stages.
The response manager approves the agency's allocation within delegated authority.
Logistics confirms stock and feasible movement; program and protection teams assess appropriateness and safety.
A donor can set contractual constraints but does not become the affected community's representative.
A cluster or coordination forum supports information sharing and common services where available, not unilateral control over all agency stock.
A safe complaint or consultation channel must have an owner, response process, and an example of how it can change eligibility, location, or timing.

### Exercise 2

The planning gap is 740 − 90 − 110 = 540 households.
Before the commitments are fulfilled, the remaining gap after verified coverage is 740 − 90 = 650 households.
If 80 of the 110 committed kits fail with no replacement, the planning gap becomes 620 households.
This assumes the failed commitment referred to 80 distinct eligible households and that all other quantities remain unchanged.
If overlapping or inappropriate assistance is discovered, the gap must be recalculated rather than mechanically subtracted.

### Exercise 3

The unconstrained shares are 360 × 400 / 600 = 240 kits for North and 360 × 200 / 600 = 120 kits for South.
Both are integers and neither route cap binds.
The residuals are 160 and 80 households, respectively.
The total is 360 kits, within stock, and the quantities are below both the gaps and the route caps.
No rounding adjustment is necessary.

### Exercise 4

Cedar's planned coverage of its residual gap is 120 / 300 = 40%.
Inaccessible areas may have fewer assessment observations and recipients may be unable to reach a site.
A low report can therefore reflect barriers, missing data, or low actual need; the count alone cannot distinguish them.
Ask the access focal point and local partner to investigate safe alternative routes and delivery arrangements.
An interim action could be to reserve attention and collect safe additional information; it cannot assume an unapproved route is usable.
Explain the constraint and offer accessible feedback channels without publishing sensitive details.

### Exercise 5

Central yields 0.6 × 270 + 0.4 × 90 = 198 expected reachable kits.
Ridge yields 0.6 × 240 + 0.4 × 210 = 228 expected reachable kits.
Ridge costs USD 800 more, reaches 30 more kits in expectation, and has 120 more reachable kits in the closure scenario.
The incremental ratio is USD 26.67 per additional expected reachable kit under these assumptions.
Reachability is not actual receipt: authorization, last-mile handover, need, and distribution can still fail.
A sound recommendation states whether the extra cost is affordable and how the probability assumptions affect it.

### Exercise 6

A strong contract defines one row per organization, activity, location, item, reporting period, and status, with a unique activity identifier.
It specifies whether quantity counts kits, households, persons, or events and whether figures are incremental or cumulative.
It dates each report and its underlying activity, defines the location version, names an accountable owner, and states the reporting cadence.
It distinguishes planned, committed, dispatched, received, and distributed statuses and documents overlap handling.
Aggregate data may suffice for coordination; individual records need a specific purpose, lawful basis where applicable, restricted access, retention, and correction mechanisms.
The rubric does not require a single universal legal basis or a household-level identity exchange.

### Exercise 7

A strong answer weighs supply and price, accessibility, payment infrastructure, protection, and recipient preferences for each location.
It sets an owner and threshold for monitoring price, availability, complaints, and safe access.
A switch from cash to in-kind may be warranted if essential goods become unavailable, subject to local review.
A switch toward cash may be warranted if markets function and people can safely choose appropriate goods, subject to funding and protection review.
No choice should be presented as universally correct.

### Exercise 8

With Cedar's route cap increased to 200 kits, allocations are 386, 321, and 193 kits; residuals are 214, 179, and 107 households.
With Cedar's gap increased to 380 and its cap restored to 120, allocations are 425, 355, and 120 kits; residuals are 175, 145, and 260 households.
The latter totals 580 households conditionally unserved after the planned delivery.
The code cannot verify route safety, household eligibility, commitment reliability, or recipient preferences.
It cannot decide whether proportional shares are ethically preferable to a different approved policy.

### Exercise 9

A strong brief distinguishes the 1,400-household planning gap from the 1,630-household gap before partner commitments are fulfilled.
It presents 425/355/120 as a proposal, with 500 households remaining under the planning assumptions.
It identifies Cedar's constrained access, describes the alternative-access investigation, and sets a named same-day review trigger.
It includes a safe feedback channel, protected handling of sensitive data, a recipient-accessibility check, and a delivery reconciliation plan.
A weaker brief reports only total kits moved or declares full coverage without verification.

## 14. Interview questions

1. **What is the difference between affected people, people in need, a target, and people reached?**
   A strong answer defines each population and its time, evidence, and unit.
   A weak answer treats them as interchangeable dashboard labels.
2. **How would you estimate need in an inaccessible area?**
   A strong answer triangulates safe indirect evidence, states selection bias, and identifies uncertainty and access priorities.
   A weak answer assigns zero need because no survey was completed.
3. **What is a credible partner commitment?**
   A strong answer names quantity, population, period, resources, ownership, and confirmation status.
   A weak answer treats an informal intention as completed assistance.
4. **How do you prevent double-counting?**
   A strong answer aligns units, dates, status, geography, and overlap rules while protecting personal data.
   A weak answer merges people by name without testing false matches.
5. **How would you choose a pre-positioning hub?**
   A strong answer compares coherent scenarios, lead times, capacity, costs, risk, and distributional effects.
   A weak answer selects the nearest hub without checking access or stock ownership.
6. **What does the Logistics Cluster do?**
   A strong answer describes context-dependent coordination, information management, and common services without assuming universal command.
   A weak answer assumes it controls all relief actors and beneficiary selection.
7. **Why can an optimal allocation still be inequitable?**
   A strong answer identifies omitted barriers, biased evidence, ethical weights, and differential unmet need.
   A weak answer assumes the optimizer's numerical objective is inherently fair.
8. **How would you choose between cash and in-kind assistance?**
   A strong answer considers market functionality, safe access, preferences, protection, and change triggers.
   A weak answer declares one modality always cheapest or most dignified.
9. **What would stop a planned convoy?**
   A strong answer names missing or expired security and administrative clearance and a clear escalation path.
   A weak answer treats route risk as only a cost penalty.
10. **How do you know assistance reached people safely?**
    A strong answer distinguishes shipment, receipt, distribution, and PDM, including people unable to attend.
    A weak answer treats a signed warehouse receipt as proof of impact.
11. **What data would you share with a coordination forum?**
    A strong answer starts with purpose and minimum necessary aggregate information and checks risks and permissions.
    A weak answer shares a full recipient list by default.
12. **How do you make accountability operational?**
    A strong answer includes accessible information, participation, safe feedback, response ownership, and closing the loop.
    A weak answer counts complaints without investigating or responding.

## 15. Key terms

- [Access](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — practical, safe, and lawful ability to reach assistance or a location.
- [Accountability to affected people](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — participation, information, feedback, and response practices.
- [Affected population](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — people exposed to a crisis in a specified scope.
- [Assessment coverage](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — portion represented by a collection method.
- [Available-to-promise](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — usable, unreserved, eligible inventory.
- [Cluster](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — context-dependent humanitarian sector coordination mechanism.
- [Commitment](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — authorized, resource-backed promise with a defined period.
- [Data minimization](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — limiting collection and sharing to a specified need.
- [Deduplication](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — identifying overlapping records or assistance under a defined rule.
- [Disaggregation](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — safe breakdown of a measure by meaningful groups.
- [Equity](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — fair attention to needs, barriers, and consequences.
- [Forecast](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — conditional estimate of a future outcome.
- [Gap](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — need less relevant, non-overlapping coverage under a stated rule.
- [Humanity](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — addressing suffering and protecting life, health, and dignity.
- [Impartiality](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — action based on need without adverse distinction.
- [Independence](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — autonomy of humanitarian objectives from other objectives.
- [Lead time](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — elapsed time between defined process events.
- [Logistics Cluster](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — logistics coordination and common-services mechanism where activated.
- [Market assessment](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — analysis of supply, access, prices, and market risks.
- [Modality](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — way assistance is provided.
- [Neutrality](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — not taking sides in hostilities or relevant controversies.
- [People in need](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — estimated people requiring assistance under stated criteria.
- [Pipeline](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — goods tracked by procurement and movement status.
- [Post-distribution monitoring](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — checking receipt, use, appropriateness, and safety after distribution.
- [Pre-positioning](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — placing stock ahead of potential demand or disruption.
- [Protection](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — reducing risks to safety, dignity, and rights.
- [Regret](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — difference from the best feasible outcome in a realized scenario.
- [Response plan](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — selected actions, responsibilities, resources, and timing.
- [Route capacity](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — quantity deliverable along a route in a defined period.
- [Scenario](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — coherent assumptions describing a possible future.
- [Severity](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — categorized intensity of need under defined indicators.
- [Target population](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — people the response intends to assist.
- [Unmet need](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — need not adequately addressed by available assistance.
