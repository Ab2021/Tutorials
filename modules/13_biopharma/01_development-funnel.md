## 1. Why this topic exists

A drug programme is not a research project that ends when the science is finished. It is a
sequence of irreversible financial commitments made under uncertainty, each one justified by
evidence that is weaker than the decision it supports. The industry's central operational fact is
that most programmes fail, and they fail after spending most of their money. Everything else in
this module — biomarkers, feasibility, standards, safety, real-world evidence, submission — is a
tool for making one of those commitments better.

The arithmetic of attrition is unforgiving. If the probability of surviving from first-in-human
dosing to approval is roughly 8%, then a portfolio of twelve Phase I programmes is expected to
produce one approval, and the cost of the eleven failures is carried by the one success. Under
that arithmetic, the cost of a late failure is not merely the money spent on the failed
programme; it is the number of other programmes that could have been funded with the same
capital. A Phase III failure that consumes USD 150 million destroys the option value of perhaps
three Phase I starts. This is why portfolio decisions, not scientific curiosity, set the agenda
at large sponsors.

The decision problem has a second layer. The evidence available at each gate is generated by the
stage that preceded it, and each stage is designed to answer a different question. A Phase I
programme answers "can humans tolerate exposures in the range where the animal models suggest
effect?" It does not answer "does the drug work?" A Phase II programme answers "is there a signal
of activity in a defined population?" It usually does not answer "is the effect large enough and
durable enough to justify a mortality or progression endpoint at scale?" When organisations
forget which question a dataset can answer, they promote a programme on evidence that cannot
support the next stage, and the failure surfaces two years and one hundred million dollars later.

Data science enters this topic in three places. First, benchmarking: what is the base rate of
success for this kind of asset in this kind of indication, and how should the base rate be
adjusted for what is known about this specific programme? Second, forecasting: how long will the
next stage take, how many patients will it need, and what will it cost, given site activation,
screen failure, and enrolment dynamics? Third, decision analysis: what is the expected value of
continuing versus stopping versus redesigning, and how sensitive is that answer to the
assumptions that are least well supported? None of these is a prediction of the future. Each is a
structured way of making the uncertainty explicit enough to argue about.

### 1.1 The failure pattern this topic prevents

1. A programme reports a positive Phase II result in 64 patients.
2. The effect size is attractive and statistically significant at p = 0.03.
3. The governance committee treats the result as evidence that the drug works.
4. No one asks what the confidence interval implies for the Phase III sample size.
5. No one asks whether the Phase II population matches the intended label population.
6. The Phase III is sized on the observed Phase II effect, without allowing for regression to
   the mean or for the winner's curse.
7. The Phase III is powered for an effect larger than the true one, enrols for 30 months, and
   reads out null.
8. The post-mortem attributes the failure to "biology" rather than to the decision rule.

The failure in step 8 is the expensive one, because it prevents learning. The decision in step 3
was made with the information available; the defect was that the information was not translated
into a distribution over effect sizes before the commitment was made.

### 1.2 Who is blocked when the funnel is managed badly

| Stakeholder | What they lose | Why they cannot fix it alone |
|---|---|---|
| Programme team | Two years and a career-defining readout | Cannot change the portfolio-level capital allocation |
| Portfolio committee | Capital that could have funded three other assets | Sees the programme in isolation, not the counterfactual |
| Clinical operations | A recruitment plan built on an unachievable rate | Receives a target, not the eligibility evidence |
| Biostatistics | Credibility when a trial is powered on an inflated effect | Receives the effect estimate after it is locked |
| Regulatory affairs | A submission that cannot support the proposed label | Receives data generated by a design chosen elsewhere |
| Patient | Exposure to a drug that was never likely to work | Has no access to the probability reasoning |
| Investor | A valuation built on a single-point probability of success | Receives the company's own, often optimistic, estimate |

### 1.3 The industry question

> Given what we know now, is the next commitment the best use of this capital, and what evidence
> would change the answer?

The question is deliberately two-part. The first part is about value; the second is about the
value of information. A programme can be worth continuing even when its expected value is
negative, if a cheap experiment would resolve the uncertainty. It can be worth stopping even when
its expected value is positive, if the same capital earns more elsewhere. Both judgements require
the funnel to be represented as a sequence of conditional probabilities and cash flows, not as a
narrative.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end of this topic you can:

1. **Draw** the development funnel with its decision gates, the question each stage answers, and
   the evidence each gate consumes, from memory.
2. **Distinguish** conditional transition probability, cumulative likelihood of approval, and the
   probability used in a valuation, and explain why confusing them changes a decision.
3. **Compute** a risk-adjusted net present value (rNPV) for a single asset from stage costs,
   transition probabilities, discount rate, and launch value, and state the units and timing
   conventions used.
4. **Adjust** a published base rate for programme-specific evidence using an explicit, auditable
   multiplier scheme, and state the cap and the rationale for each multiplier.
5. **Design** a gate decision rule with pre-specified kill criteria, the data that trigger them,
   and the decision options available at the gate.
6. **Forecast** enrolment completion month from site activation, per-site accrual rate, and
   target sample size, and identify the assumptions that dominate the forecast.
7. **Diagnose** a broken programme valuation by checking internal consistency: do the stage
   probabilities multiply to the stated overall probability, and does the enrolment model imply a
   per-site rate that matches historical site performance?
8. **Critique** a go/no-go recommendation that reports a single probability without a
   sensitivity analysis, a denominator, or an analysis population.

Each outcome maps to an exercise in Section 12 or a self-check item in Section 13.

## 3. The core mental model

### 3.1 The funnel as a chain of conditional bets

```text
 Discovery        Preclinical      Phase I        Phase II        Phase III      Filing     Launch
    |                 |               |               |               |             |          |
 target ID  -->  IND-enabling  -->  FIH/PK/PD  -->  signal      -->  confirm     --> review --> market
    |                 |               |               |               |             |          |
 Gate 0            Gate 1          Gate 2          Gate 3          Gate 4        Gate 5     Gate 6
 (target           (IND /          (RP2D /         (end of         (regist-      (approval) (access,
  validation)       first-in-       proof of        Phase II)       rational                    lifecycle)
                    human)          concept)                        readout)
```

Each arrow is a conditional probability: the probability of reaching the next stage given that
the programme reached this one. The product of the arrows is the cumulative probability from the
entry point to approval. The product is not the probability that the programme succeeds; it is
the probability that a programme *of this kind, entering at this point, with these
characteristics* succeeds, under the reference class used to estimate the arrows.

The distinction matters because the arrows are estimated from populations of programmes, and any
individual programme is not a random draw from that population. A programme with a validated
biomarker, a genetically defined subset, and a mechanism already validated by another approved
drug is not an average programme. Adjusting the base rate is legitimate; ignoring that the
adjustment is judgement rather than measurement is not.

### 3.2 The cost profile inverts the probability profile

```text
 Probability of reaching stage, entering at Phase I        Cost per stage
 100% |####                                          |     High |            ####
  80% |####                                          |          |          ##
  60% |####                                          |          |        ##
  40% |####      ####                                |          |      ##
  20% |####      ####      ####      ##              |     Low  |  ####
   0% +------------------------------------          |          +------------------
       Ph I     Ph II    Ph III   Filing                     Ph I  Ph II  Ph III  Filing
```

The left panel falls; the right panel rises. The product of the two — expected cost per approval
— is dominated by the stages where both are large: Phase III and filing. This is the structural
reason that late failures are the industry's principal economic problem, and the reason that
decision quality at Gate 3 (end of Phase II) is worth more than decision quality anywhere else.

### 3.3 Information accumulates but does not become certainty

At each gate the programme holds more information than at the previous one, but the *fraction* of
the total uncertainty resolved is modest. A Phase II trial of 80 patients estimates an effect size
with a standard error that is often 30-50% of the estimate itself. That is enough to reject a null
hypothesis and not enough to size a Phase III confidently. The mental model to carry is:

> A gate decision is a bet on a distribution, not on a point estimate. The width of the
> distribution, not just its mean, determines whether the next stage is worth funding.

### 3.4 Three different probabilities, three different uses

| Quantity | Definition | Used for | Typical error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conditional transition probability | P(reach stage k+1 \| reached stage k) | Stage-by-stage planning and budgeting | Quoted as if it were the overall probability |
| Cumulative likelihood of approval (LOA) | Product of conditional probabilities from an entry stage to approval | Portfolio-level expected approval counts | Applied to a single asset as if it were its own probability |
| Programme-specific probability of success (PoS) | LOA adjusted for asset-specific evidence | Valuation, governance, partnering | Adjusted without recording the multipliers |

A valuation that mixes these three produces a number that looks precise and cannot be defended.
The worked example in Section 6 shows the arithmetic and the consistency check.

### 3.5 The funnel is also an options portfolio

Stopping is a decision, not a failure of will. A programme that is stopped at Gate 3 preserves
capital for redeployment. A programme that is continued to Gate 4 because "we have already spent
so much" is a sunk-cost decision, and sunk costs are irrelevant to the forward-looking value. The
correct comparison at any gate is between the *remaining* cost and the *remaining* expected value,
not between total cost and total value. This is the single most common analytical error in
programme governance, and it is arithmetic, not psychology: the money already spent does not
appear in the forward NPV.

### 3.6 Where data science changes the answer

| Lever | What it changes | Typical effect on the decision |
|---|---|---|
| Better target validation | Raises Gate 0 and Gate 1 probabilities | Fewer programmes started, higher hit rate |
| Predictive biomarker | Raises Phase II and III transition probabilities and shrinks trial size | Smaller, faster, more expensive per patient |
| Feasibility modelling | Shortens enrolment and reduces the cost of delay | Changes timing and rNPV, rarely the biology |
| Adaptive design | Allows earlier stopping for futility or efficacy | Reduces expected cost, increases design complexity |
| Real-world evidence | Substitutes for or supplements a control arm | Changes the evidence standard, not the effect size |
| Operational data | Reduces protocol deviations and missing data | Protects the analysis population and the readout |

The table is a reminder that most data science in development does not change whether a drug
works. It changes the cost, speed, and credibility with which the question is answered. That is
still valuable, but it is a different claim, and conflating the two is how analytics teams
overpromise.

## 4. Definitions and vocabulary

| Term | Definition | Common misuse | Unit or coding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attrition | Loss of programmes between stages | Used as a synonym for failure rate at a single stage | Proportion per transition |
| Likelihood of approval (LOA) | Probability that a programme entering at a given stage reaches approval, estimated from a reference class | Treated as an asset-specific forecast | Probability, 0-1 |
| Probability of success (PoS) | Sponsor's or analyst's estimate for a specific asset, usually LOA adjusted | Used interchangeably with LOA without stating adjustments | Probability, 0-1 |
| Transition probability | Conditional probability of moving from one stage to the next | Multiplied as if unconditional | Probability, 0-1 |
| Gate | A formal decision point with defined evidence, criteria, and options | Treated as a reporting milestone | Identifier, e.g. Gate 3 |
| Kill criterion | A pre-specified result that ends the programme or the indication | Written after the data arrive | Threshold with units |
| Go/no-go | The binary decision at a gate | Presented without the third and fourth options (pause, redesign) | Decision category |
| Target product profile (TPP) | The label the programme intends to earn: indication, population, endpoint, safety, formulation | Written as a wish list without a minimum | Document with minimum and target attributes |
| Clinical development plan (CDP) | The sequence of studies, gates, and submissions intended to reach the TPP | Confused with the protocol | Document |
| Investigational New Drug (IND) | US authorisation to administer an investigational drug to humans | Treated as an approval | Regulatory status |
| Clinical Trial Application (CTA) | EU equivalent authorisation to conduct a trial | Confused with marketing authorisation | Regulatory status |
| New Drug Application (NDA) / Biologics License Application (BLA) | US marketing applications for small molecules / biologics | Used interchangeably | Regulatory submission |
| Marketing Authorisation Application (MAA) | EU application for marketing authorisation | Confused with FDA NDA | Regulatory submission |
| First-in-human (FIH) | First administration of an investigational drug to humans | Used as a synonym for Phase I | Study type |
| Single ascending dose (SAD) / multiple ascending dose (MAD) | Phase I designs to characterise safety and pharmacokinetics | Treated as efficacy studies | Study design |
| Maximum tolerated dose (MTD) | Highest dose with acceptable toxicity in a Phase I design | Assumed to be the optimal dose | Dose, with units |
| Recommended Phase II dose (RP2D) | Dose selected for later development, from safety, PK, and PD | Equated with MTD | Dose, with units |
| Pharmacokinetics (PK) | What the body does to the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion | Confused with pharmacodynamics | Concentration-time parameters |
| Pharmacodynamics (PD) | What the drug does to the body, including biomarker effects | Used loosely for any biomarker change | Effect measures |
| Proof of concept (PoC) | Evidence that the mechanism produces the intended effect in patients | Treated as evidence of clinical benefit | Study objective |
| Endpoint | Variable used to assess efficacy or safety | Confused with a biomarker | Defined variable |
| Surrogate endpoint | Endpoint expected to predict clinical benefit | Treated as validated without evidence | Defined variable |
| Interim analysis | Pre-planned analysis before final data | Conducted ad hoc and called interim | Analysis event |
| Data Monitoring Committee (DMC) | Independent group reviewing unblinded safety and efficacy | Confused with the steering committee | Governance body |
| Futility boundary | Pre-specified boundary at which continuing is futile | Applied after unblinding | Statistical threshold |
| Risk-adjusted NPV (rNPV) | NPV with each cash flow weighted by the probability of reaching it | Computed with a single probability applied to all flows | Currency, with discount rate and date |
| Discount rate | Rate used to convert future cash to present value | Chosen to match the sponsor's cost of capital without stating it | Annual proportion |
| Peak sales | Maximum annual revenue assumed for the asset | Used as if it were profit | Currency per year |
| Loss of exclusivity (LoE) | Date at which generic or biosimilar competition begins | Ignored in long-horizon valuations | Date |
| Reference class | Population of comparable programmes used to estimate base rates | Chosen after seeing the result | Defined set |
| Winner's curse | Tendency of selected estimates to overstate the true effect | Ignored when sizing the next trial | Statistical bias |
| Regression to the mean | Extreme observations tend to be followed by less extreme ones | Mistaken for treatment effect waning | Statistical phenomenon |
| Stage-gate process | Formal process of stage reviews with defined deliverables | Implemented as a paperwork exercise | Process |

### 4.1 Probability vocabulary in practice

Three conventions coexist and are routinely mixed in the same meeting.

- **Per-stage conditional rates** are the natural output of registry analyses: of programmes that
  entered Phase II, what fraction entered Phase III? These are the numbers in benchmark reports.
- **Cumulative LOA from Phase I** is the product of all downstream conditional rates. Benchmark
  reports usually quote it alongside the stage rates, and the two should be arithmetically
  consistent.
- **Asset-level PoS** is a judgement that begins from LOA and is adjusted. There is no standard
  method, no required reporting, and no regulatory definition. A number presented without its
  base rate and adjustment is not auditable.

> **Note:** A regulatory submission has no "probability of success" in the statistical sense used
> in a trial. The phrase is a commercial planning construct. When it appears in a regulatory
> document, it is a forecast, not an estimate with a confidence interval.

## 5. The mechanics, step by step

This section describes a gate review as a repeatable procedure. The procedure is written for a
sponsor's internal governance, but the same steps apply to an investment committee, a partner's
diligence team, or a public funding body. The steps are sequential, but steps 4 to 8 are usually
iterated once, because the first pass reveals which assumptions matter.

### Step 1. Define the asset, the indication, and the decision

**Input:** programme documents, the current development plan, and the question the committee is
being asked to answer.

**Output:** a one-page decision statement naming the asset, the indication and line of therapy,
the decision to be made, the date, the decision owner, and the options on the table.

**Rationale:** Most gate meetings fail at this step. "Should we continue the programme?" is not a
decision; it has no alternatives and no date. "Should we fund a 520-patient randomised Phase III
in biomarker-selected second-line disease, at USD 145 million over 42 months, starting in Q3, or
fund a 240-patient Phase IIb first?" is a decision. The statement also fixes the unit of analysis:
an asset in an indication, not a molecule in general, because success rates differ by indication
and line.

**Edge case:** the same molecule may be simultaneously under review for two indications with
different evidence. Treat them as separate decision units with separate probabilities, even when
they share a supply chain and a safety database.

### Step 2. Write or retrieve the target product profile

**Input:** the clinical strategy, competitor landscape, and payer research.

**Output:** a TPP with minimum acceptable and target attributes for indication, population,
efficacy endpoint and magnitude, safety, dosing, formulation, and label claims.

**Rationale:** The TPP is the definition of success. Without it, "positive" is undefined at every
gate, and each gate renegotiates the bar. A TPP with a minimum and a target also allows the
valuation to be run at both levels, which is how the sensitivity analysis in Step 6 is
constructed.

**Branch point:** if no TPP exists, the gate cannot proceed to valuation. A programme without a
defined label ambition can still be scientifically interesting, but it cannot be valued, and a
valuation performed without one will embed an implicit TPP that nobody has agreed to.

### Step 3. Map the evidence chain and the gates

**Input:** the TPP and the current development plan.

**Output:** a table with one row per stage: the question the stage answers, the study or studies,
the primary evidence, the gate criteria, and the cost and duration.

**Rationale:** The mapping exposes two failure patterns. The first is a stage that cannot answer
its question with the design proposed (a single-arm Phase II intended to support a mortality
claim). The second is a gate whose criteria depend on data the stage does not produce (a go
decision requiring an exposure-response relationship that the study never characterised).

**Edge case:** platform programmes (a cell therapy, an antibody-drug conjugate) may reuse
preclinical and manufacturing evidence across assets. The mapping should record which evidence is
platform-level and which is asset-specific, because the former is not repeated and the latter
cannot be borrowed.

### Step 4. Select the reference class and the base rates

**Input:** benchmark reports, internal historical performance, and the programme's characteristics.

**Output:** a documented reference class, a set of conditional transition probabilities, and a
statement of how the programme differs from the class.

**Rationale:** A base rate is only meaningful with its class. "Phase II success is 29%" is
uninterpretable without knowing the years, the therapeutic areas, the definition of a phase
transition, and whether the denominator is programmes or programme-indication pairs. Published
reports differ in all four, which is why their headline numbers differ by several percentage
points.

**Branch point — which reference class:**

| Situation | Reasonable reference class | Risk of the choice |
|---|---|---|
| Novel mechanism, no approved drug in class | All Phase II oncology programmes, 2011-2020 | Base rate may be too optimistic for a first-in-class asset |
| Mechanism validated by an approved drug | Approved-drug analogues in the same target class | Small class; selection on success |
| Biomarker-selected subset | Biomarker-defined programmes in the same area | Few observations; publication selection |
| Rare disease, single-arm registration path | Rare-disease programmes using the same pathway | Regulatory pathway changes over time |
| Platform reuse (same modality, new target) | Platform's own historical transitions | Platform effect may be real or may be era effect |
| In-licensing candidate | Sponsor's own transitions, not the licensor's | Licensor selection bias |

**Edge case:** internal historical rates are usually estimated on 20-60 programmes. At that size a
point estimate of 40% has a 95% confidence interval spanning roughly 26% to 56%. Using the point
estimate as if it were known is a false precision that propagates directly into the valuation.

### Step 5. Adjust the base rate explicitly

**Input:** base rates and the programme's differentiating evidence.

**Output:** a programme-specific PoS with a multiplier table, each multiplier justified by a
named piece of evidence, and a cap.

**Rationale:** Adjustments are judgements, and the purpose of the table is to make them
inspectable rather than to make them precise. A multiplier that cannot be tied to a specific
observation is a preference, and should be recorded as such. The cap exists because multiplicative
adjustment compounds: five multipliers of 1.2 produce a factor of 2.49, which is rarely defensible.

**Worked multiplier scheme (convention, not a standard):**

| Factor | Evidence required to apply | Multiplier range | Cap rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Validated predictive biomarker with a locked assay | Analytical validation plus a prospective or well-controlled retrospective association | 1.10-1.30 | Effect is real but assay and prevalence risk remain |
| Genetically defined subset with human genetic support | Mendelian randomisation or loss-of-function evidence | 1.10-1.25 | Human genetics is the strongest target evidence available |
| Phase II result with a confidence interval excluding the null by a wide margin | Pre-specified analysis, adequate sample | 1.05-1.20 | Reduces, but does not remove, winner's curse |
| Unmet need with a regulatory expedited pathway | Documented pathway eligibility | 1.05-1.15 | Speeds review; does not change efficacy |
| Single-arm design with external control | Pre-specified external control, comparability analysis | 0.80-0.95 | Design risk is real and rarely fully mitigated |
| First-in-class mechanism with no human validation | None beyond preclinical | 0.75-0.90 | Target risk is the dominant failure mode |
| High screen-failure or enrolment risk | Feasibility data showing constrained recruitment | 0.85-0.95 | Delay and selection effects |
| Prior class failure on the same mechanism | Documented failed programme | 0.60-0.85 | Strong evidence against the mechanism |

> **Note:** The ranges above are a teaching convention. They are not derived from a published
> meta-analysis and should not be cited as one. The defensible practice is to document the
> sponsor's own calibration: compare previous adjusted PoS with realised outcomes (Step 11).

### Step 6. Compute the risk-adjusted value

**Input:** stage costs and timing, transition probabilities, launch value, discount rate.

**Output:** an rNPV with its components shown, plus the probability-weighted cash flow table.

**Rationale:** The rNPV forces the analyst to state when money is spent and when it is received.
It also exposes the difference between a programme with a high probability of a modest return and
one with a low probability of a large return, which a single PoS number hides.

**Formula:**

```text
rNPV = sum over stages of [ -C_t * P(reach t) / (1+r)^t ]
       + sum over revenue years of [ R_t * P(launch) / (1+r)^t ]
```

where C_t is the cost in year t, R_t is the net revenue in year t, P(reach t) is the cumulative
probability of reaching year t, r is the annual discount rate, and P(launch) is the cumulative
probability of approval. Costs incurred before the decision are excluded, because they are sunk.

**Branch point — which value to use:**

| Value concept | Definition | When to use | Pitfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peak sales | Maximum annual revenue | Early screening only | Ignores time to peak, price erosion, and cost of goods |
| Risk-adjusted peak sales | Peak sales multiplied by PoS | Portfolio ranking | Applies one probability to all years |
| rNPV | Discounted, probability-weighted cash flows | Gate decisions and licensing | Sensitive to discount rate and terminal value |
| Expected net present value of information | Value of a trial that resolves uncertainty | Deciding whether to run another study | Requires explicit priors and likelihoods |
| Strategic option value | Value of platform, capability, or access | Portfolio strategy | Easily inflated; hard to falsify |

### Step 7. Pre-specify the kill criteria

**Input:** the TPP, the planned design, and the base rates.

**Output:** a written set of criteria, each with a threshold, an analysis population, a timing,
and the decision that follows.

**Rationale:** A kill criterion written before the data arrive is a commitment device. Written
afterwards, it is a rationalisation. The criterion must name the analysis population, because a
result in the per-protocol population and a result in the intention-to-treat population can point
in different directions, and choosing the favourable one after unblinding is a form of bias.

**Edge case:** a criterion that is certain to be met because the threshold is set below the
historical control rate is not a criterion. Check every threshold against the benchmark before
sign-off.

### Step 8. Run the gate review

**Input:** the evidence package, the valuation, the kill criteria, and an independent challenge.

**Output:** a decision record: the decision, the rationale, the dissent, the conditions, and the
date of the next review.

**Rationale:** The independent challenge is the mechanism that prevents groupthink. In practice it
is a named person or team with no ownership of the programme, whose role is to state the strongest
case against continuation. Programmes that skip this step tend to continue on momentum.

**Branch point — decision options (more than two):**

| Option | When appropriate | What must be true | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Continue as planned | Evidence meets criteria; value above hurdle | No material design flaw; budget available | Commit the next stage budget |
| Continue with conditions | Evidence adequate but a specific gap exists | Gap can be closed within the stage without re-design | Milestone-linked funding |
| Redesign and re-review | Design cannot answer the gate question | A feasible alternative design exists | Delay of 6-18 months |
| Pause and seek partner | Value positive but capital constrained | A partner or non-dilutive funding is plausible | Retains option; costs maintenance |
| Stop the indication, keep the asset | Indication-specific failure | Another indication has independent rationale | Reallocate the asset |
| Stop the asset | Mechanism or safety failure | Failure is not design-specific | Write off; capture the learning |

### Step 9. Handle the edge cases that break the arithmetic

**Screen failures.** Screen failure reduces the number of randomised patients per enrolled
patient. If 30% of screened patients fail, 520 randomised patients require 743 screened patients.
Enrolment forecasts that quote randomised patients without the screen-failure multiplier
understate site burden by 43%.

**Protocol deviations.** Deviations that affect the primary endpoint remove patients from the
per-protocol population but not from the intention-to-treat population. A programme whose
projected per-protocol population is below the power requirement is at risk even if enrolment
meets target.

**Missing visits.** Missing primary-endpoint assessments are handled by the estimand framework
(ICH E9(R1)), not by deletion. A gate that compares complete-case results with the primary
estimand is comparing two different questions.

**Partial dates.** Adverse event and concomitant medication dates are frequently partial
(month-year or year only). Imputation rules must be pre-specified, because they affect the
attribution of events to treatment periods and therefore the safety narrative at the gate.

**Concomitant medication coding.** A change in the coding dictionary version between stages can
change the counts of prior medications and therefore the baseline comparability. The gate package
should state the dictionary version and whether it changed.

**External control drift.** A single-arm programme with an external control inherits the risk that
standard of care changed between the control cohort's period and the trial's period. A drift of a
few months in median survival can erase the apparent effect.

**Unblinding.** An interim analysis that unblinds the sponsor's team creates a risk that
subsequent operational decisions are informed by the result. Firewalls and pre-specified
communication rules are the mitigation.

**Database lock.** A gate timed before database lock is a gate on incomplete data. State the data
cut, the number of patients with pending queries, and the expected change.

### Step 10. Allocate capital across the portfolio

**Input:** the set of funded and candidate programmes, each with an rNPV, a cost profile, and a
capacity constraint (cash, sites, manufacturing, people).

**Output:** a funded set, a deferred set, and a stopping set, with the constraint binding stated.

**Rationale:** Individually positive programmes can exceed the available capital. The portfolio
problem is a constrained selection, and the correct ranking is by value per unit of the binding
constraint, not by rNPV alone. A programme with a high rNPV that consumes the entire clinical
operations capacity for two years may be worse than three smaller programmes.

**Branch point:** if the binding constraint is cash, rank by rNPV per dollar of peak cumulative
funding requirement. If it is sites or patients, rank by rNPV per patient-year of enrolment. If
it is manufacturing, rank by rNPV per batch-year. The ranking changes with the constraint, and
stating the constraint is part of the decision.

### Step 11. Close the loop with calibration

**Input:** past gate predictions and realised outcomes.

**Output:** a calibration record: predicted PoS versus observed transition rate, by therapeutic
area and stage.

**Rationale:** An organisation that never checks its forecasts cannot improve them. Calibration is
simple to compute and rarely done: bin past predictions, compare the predicted rate with the
observed rate in each bin, and report the difference. A programme that predicted 40% and realised
25% is not a failure of the team; it is information about the adjustment scheme.

### Step 12. Communicate the decision and its uncertainty

**Input:** the decision record and the valuation.

**Output:** a one-page summary for governance and a fuller appendix for the file.

**Rationale:** The summary should state the decision, the value, the probability with its base
rate, the two assumptions that matter most, and what would change the answer. Governance bodies
that receive a single number make worse decisions than those that receive a range and the
conditions under which the range shifts.

|---|---|---|---|
| Primary endpoint met, CI excludes null, safety acceptable | rNPV above hurdle | Continue as planned | Confirm supply and enrolment capacity |
| Primary endpoint met, but CI lower bound near null | rNPV above hurdle only at optimistic value | Redesign with larger sample or enrichment | Re-power on the CI lower bound |
| Primary endpoint met in a subgroup only | rNPV positive in subgroup, negative overall | Continue in subgroup with a new protocol | Pre-specify subgroup in the next trial |
| Primary endpoint missed, secondary consistent | rNPV marginal | Independent re-analysis before stopping | Check estimand and missing-data handling |
| Primary endpoint missed, safety signal present | Negative | Stop the asset | Report to safety database and regulators |
| Endpoint unmet due to enrolment shortfall | Value unchanged, cost increased | Extend enrolment or reduce sample with re-powering | Re-run feasibility before committing |
| Endpoint uninterpretable due to deviations | Unknown | Pause; audit data quality | Fix root cause; re-review in one quarter |

### 5.2 What "the evidence supports the next stage" actually requires

A gate is not a vote on whether the team has worked hard. The evidence must satisfy four tests,
and all four are necessary:

1. **Direction.** The effect points the right way, in the pre-specified primary analysis.
2. **Magnitude.** The effect is large enough that a trial sized on a plausible true effect is
   feasible in the intended population.
3. **Durability.** The effect persists over a clinically relevant period, not only at the first
   assessment.
4. **Generalisation.** The population studied resembles the population in the intended label, or
   the difference is understood and can be addressed by enrichment.

A programme that passes direction but fails magnitude should not proceed to a Phase III sized on
the observed effect; it should proceed, if at all, to a study designed to estimate the effect more
precisely. A programme that passes magnitude but fails generalisation should narrow the label.

### 5.3 Timeline arithmetic and its traps

Development timelines are usually built bottom-up: activation, enrolment, follow-up, analysis,
lock, submission, review. Three traps recur.

- **Activation is not instantaneous.** A site that signs a contract in January may randomise its
  first patient in April. The gap is not uniform across sites and is longer in countries with
  separate regulatory and ethics approvals.
- **Enrolment does not stop at the target.** Sites continue to screen while closure
  documentation is processed, producing over-enrolment that can affect the analysis if not
  handled by a pre-specified rule.
- **Follow-up is event-driven, not calendar-driven.** A progression-free survival analysis
  requires a number of events, not a number of months. If the event rate is lower than assumed,
  the analysis date moves later, and the cost of the study increases without any change in
  enrolment.

The worked example in Section 6 quantifies the first and third traps.

## 6. Worked example

> **Note:** The asset, sponsor, and results in this example are fictional and constructed for
> teaching. The benchmark rates are real published figures and are cited in Section 16; the
> arithmetic is reproducible with the code in Section 7.

### 6.1 The programme

A mid-sized oncology sponsor is developing **ravutinib** (fictional), an oral kinase inhibitor for
the approximately 8% of advanced non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLC) carrying an activating
alteration. Phase I (58 patients) selected a recommended Phase II dose of 160 mg once daily.
Phase II was single-arm in 84 biomarker-positive patients, second line after platinum
chemotherapy: 32 of 84 responded, ORR 38.1% (95% CI 27.7% to 49.3%), median duration of response
9.1 months. Historical control ORR for second-line chemotherapy in this setting is 22%.

The programme now faces Gate 3: fund a randomised Phase III (A), fund a Phase IIb first (B), or
stop and out-license (C).

### 6.2 Step 1: the decision statement

Decision: at the Q1 portfolio committee on 15 March, approve one of three exclusive options for
ravutinib in biomarker-selected second-line NSCLC:

- **Option A:** randomised Phase III, 520 patients, 1:1 ravutinib versus docetaxel, primary
  endpoint progression-free survival (PFS), 45 sites, out-of-pocket cost USD 145 million over
  42 months.
- **Option B:** Phase IIb randomised, 240 patients, same comparison, primary endpoint ORR,
  cost USD 48 million over 24 months, followed by a Phase III decision.
- **Option C:** stop and out-license the asset.


### 6.3 Step 2: the TPP

| Attribute | Minimum | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Population | 2L EGFR-alteration NSCLC, ≥1 prior platinum regimen | Same, with CNS activity |
| Endpoint for approval | PFS hazard ratio ≤ 0.80 | PFS HR ≤ 0.70 with OS trend |
| ORR | ≥ 30% | ≥ 40% |
| Safety | No increase in grade ≥3 interstitial lung disease above 5% | Grade ≥3 ILD below 2% |
| Label | Biomarker-selected 2L | 2L and 1L combination |

### 6.4 Step 4: base rate

Reference class: all Phase II oncology programmes in the 2011-2020 BIO/Citeline/QLS cohort. The
reported Phase II transition probability is 28.9% and the cumulative LOA from Phase I is 7.9%.
The Phase III transition in the same cohort is 57.8% and the filing-to-approval rate is 90.6%.

Check the arithmetic:

```text
0.520 (Phase I -> II) x 0.289 (II -> III) x 0.578 (III -> filing) x 0.906 (filing -> approval)
= 0.0788, i.e. 7.9%
```

The product reproduces the reported LOA. If stage rates and the LOA do not reconcile, one of
them is being quoted on a different definition.

### 6.5 Step 5: adjustment

The decision is whether to enter Phase III, so the base rate must be the probability of approval
*given entry to Phase III*, not the probability of entering Phase III. In the same cohort:

```text
Phase III -> filing      = 0.578
filing -> approval       = 0.906
Phase III -> approval    = 0.578 x 0.906 = 0.5237, i.e. 52.4%
```

Using the 28.9% Phase II transition here would be a category error: 28.9% is the probability that
a Phase II programme reaches Phase III, and ravutinib has already reached Phase III entry. This is
the most common arithmetic error in gate valuations, and Section 10 lists it as a failure mode.

| Factor | Evidence | Multiplier |
|---|---|---|
| Validated predictive biomarker, locked assay | Analytical validation study; prospective-retrospective association in 2 cohorts | 1.25 |
| Phase II ORR 38.1% with 95% CI lower bound 27.7%, above the 22% historical control | Pre-specified primary analysis, 84 patients | 1.15 |
| Genetically defined subset with pathway-level human evidence | Activating alteration is a known driver in this tumour type | 1.10 |
| Single-arm Phase II design with historical control | No randomised concurrent control; historical control may not represent current practice | 0.85 |
| PFS-only benefit, overall survival immature at the gate | 9.1-month median duration of response; no survival data | 0.80 |

Combined multiplier: 1.25 × 1.15 × 1.10 × 0.85 × 0.80 = 1.0753.

Adjusted PoS: 0.5237 × 1.0753 = 0.5631, i.e. **56.3%**, below the 60% policy cap, so retained.

The adjustment is modest: the positive factors roughly cancel the design discounts, which is the
intended behaviour of a disciplined scheme. A table producing a large net uplift is usually doing
advocacy rather than estimation. The unadjusted base (52.4%) is carried as a sensitivity.

### 6.6 Step 6: rNPV for Option A

Assumptions, all stated in the decision record:

- Phase III cost: USD 145 million, spent as 40 / 50 / 40 / 15 million at the end of years 1-4.
- Launch at the end of year 4 if successful.
- Net present value at launch of all post-launch cash flows: USD 1,100 million (a commercial
  assumption, not a clinical one; it embeds price, uptake, cost of goods, and loss of exclusivity
  at year 12).
- Discount rate: 9% per year.
- Probability of approval from Phase III entry: 0.563.
- No value on failure.

Present value of costs:

```text
40/1.09^1 = 36.70
50/1.09^2 = 42.08
40/1.09^3 = 30.89
15/1.09^4 = 10.63
total     = 120.29 USD million
```

Present value of success:

```text
1,100 / 1.09^4 = 1,100 / 1.4116 = 779.3 USD million
probability-weighted = 0.563 x 779.3 = 438.7 USD million
```

Risk-adjusted NPV:

```text
438.7 - 120.3 = 318.4 USD million
```

Option A is positive at the 9% discount rate and above the committee's USD 150 million hurdle.

### 6.7 Step 6b: rNPV for Option B

Option B costs USD 48 million over 2 years (24 + 24), then proceeds to Phase III only if the
Phase IIb is positive (assumed probability 0.62, from the same adjustment logic applied to a
randomised ORR comparison). The conditional approval probability from Phase III entry is 0.563,
and launch is at the end of year 6 (2 years Phase IIb plus 4 years Phase III), so 1.09^6 = 1.6771.

```text
Phase IIb cost PV      = 24/1.09 + 24/1.09^2 = 22.02 + 20.20 = 42.22
Phase III cost PV      = 40/1.09^3 + 50/1.09^4 + 40/1.09^5 + 15/1.09^6
                       = 30.89 + 35.42 + 26.00 + 8.94 = 101.25
weighted by 0.62       = 62.78
launch value at year 6 = 1,100 / 1.6771 = 655.9
probability of success = 0.62 x 0.563 = 0.3491
weighted success value = 228.9
rNPV                   = 228.9 - 62.8 - 42.2 = 123.9 USD million
```

Option A has the higher rNPV (318.4 versus 123.9) and is the only option above the USD 150 million
hurdle. Option B has the higher rNPV per dollar of peak funding: A is 318.4/145 = 2.20; B is
123.9/48 = 2.58. If capital were binding, B would be the better use of a dollar, but it does not
clear the absolute hurdle. An absolute rNPV hurdle systematically favours large late-stage
commitments over smaller information-generating ones; that is a property of the hurdle.

### 6.8 Step 3 revisited: enrolment and timeline for Option A

The plan assumes 45 sites, first activation in month 4, five new sites per month, and 0.55
randomised patients per site per month, a rate reflecting the 8% prevalence and Phase II screen failures.

Monthly enrolment is 0.55 × active sites, so month 4 (5 sites) yields 2.75 patients, month 8
(25 sites) yields 13.75, and month 12 (45 sites) yields 24.75.

Cumulative by month 12: 0.55 × 5 × (1 + 2 + ... + 9) = 0.55 × 5 × 45 = 123.75 patients.

Remaining to 520: 396.25 patients. At 24.75 per month: 16.01 months. Completion during month 29
(month 12 + 16.01 = 28.01, so the target is crossed early in month 29). Total enrolment duration
is about 25 months after first activation, or 29 months from the decision.

Six months of follow-up after the last patient plus two months for database lock gives the
primary analysis at month 37, about 3.1 years. Launch at the end of year 4 allows 11 months for
readout, filing, and the start of review, which is optimistic but not impossible.

> **Note:** If the true per-site rate is 0.40 rather than 0.55, enrolment requires 520/(45×0.40) =
> 28.9 months at full activation, plus the 12-month ramp, or about 38 months to complete. The
> launch date slips by nine months and the rNPV falls by roughly 1,100 × (1/1.09^4.75 − 1/1.09^4)
> × 0.563 ≈ 27 USD million, before any additional fixed costs. Enrolment assumptions are value
> drivers, not operational details.

### 6.9 Two diagnostics that reveal a broken analysis

**Diagnostic 1 — the probability does not reconcile.** Reproduce the stage arithmetic from the
benchmark table. If the product of the stated conditional rates does not equal the stated
cumulative LOA, one number is on a different definition. A model quoting 7.9% as the Phase III
success probability is off by a factor of 6.6, because 7.9% is the probability from Phase I. The
check takes one line and catches an error that survives review because the number looks like a
published figure.

**Diagnostic 2 — the enrolment model implies an implausible per-site rate.** Divide cumulative
enrolment each month by the number of active sites. If the implied rate rises steadily, the model
is absorbing activation delay into the accrual rate and the completion date is too early. A naive
model assuming 45 active sites from month 1 predicts 520/24.75 = 21 months, eight months shorter
than the ramp-aware forecast. Plot observed cumulative enrolment against plan and compute the
implied quarterly rate; a rising rate means the model is wrong, not that sites are improving.

A third check is the screen-failure multiplier: if 30% of screened patients fail, 520 randomised
patients require 743 screened. A plan that quotes 520 as the screening target is internally
inconsistent with its own eligibility criteria.

### 6.10 Vary one assumption: the commercial value

The portfolio committee applies a hurdle of USD 150 million rNPV for a Phase III commitment of
this size. That hurdle is a convention, not a rule, but it is the one in force.

A competitor reports a positive Phase III in the same line six weeks before the gate. Commercial
review cuts the net present value at launch from USD 1,100 million to USD 550 million, a 50%
reduction, assuming the competitor takes second-line share, pricing pressure increases, and loss
of exclusivity arrives sooner.

```text
550 / 1.4116 = 389.6
0.563 x 389.6 = 219.4
219.4 - 120.3 = 99.1 USD million
```

The decision changes from "continue as planned" (318.4, above the 150 hurdle) to "continue with
conditions or seek a partner" (99.1, positive but below the hurdle). The clinical evidence has not
changed at all. A single commercial assumption moved the recommendation across the governance
threshold, which is why the valuation must be presented with its sensitivities rather than as a
point estimate.

For contrast, the unadjusted base rate of 52.4% gives 0.524 × 779.3 = 408.4, less 120.3 =
288.1, still above the hurdle. The adjustment from 52.4% to 56.3% does not drive the decision; the
launch value does.

## 7. Code, query, or calculation

The script below reproduces every number in Section 6. It has no dependencies beyond the standard
library, so it runs in any Python 3.9 or later environment. The conventions are stated in the
docstring: amounts are in USD million, costs occur at the end of each year, the discount rate is
9%, and the probability applies to the success path only.

```python
"""Gate-3 decision arithmetic: rNPV, enrolment timeline, sensitivity.
Amounts in USD million; costs at year end; discount rate 9%."""

def pv(cashflows, rate=0.09):
    """cashflows: list of (year, amount). Present value at year 0."""
    return sum(cf / (1 + rate) ** t for t, cf in cashflows)

def rnpv(costs, launch_year, launch_value, pos, rate=0.09):
    """Costs start in year 1. Success value realised at launch_year."""
    cost_pv = pv(list(enumerate(costs, start=1)), rate)
    success_pv = pos * launch_value / (1 + rate) ** launch_year
    return cost_pv, success_pv, success_pv - cost_pv

# Option A: Phase III directly
A_costs = [40, 50, 40, 15]
A_cost_pv, A_succ_pv, A_npv = rnpv(A_costs, 4, 1100, 0.563)

# Option B: Phase IIb, then Phase III only if positive
B2_pv = pv([(1, 24), (2, 24)])
B3_pv = pv([(3, 40), (4, 50), (5, 40), (6, 15)])
p_go = 0.62
B_succ_pv = p_go * 0.563 * 1100 / 1.09 ** 6
B_npv = B_succ_pv - p_go * B3_pv - B2_pv

print(f"A: cost PV {A_cost_pv:6.1f}  success PV {A_succ_pv:6.1f}  rNPV {A_npv:6.1f}")
print(f"B: cost PV {B2_pv + p_go*B3_pv:6.1f}  success PV {B_succ_pv:6.1f}  rNPV {B_npv:6.1f}")
print(f"A rNPV per USD peak funding {A_npv/145:.2f}; B {B_npv/48:.2f}")

# Enrolment: 45 sites, 5 activations/month from month 4, 0.55 patients/site/month
def months_to_enrol(target, n_sites=45, first_month=4, per_month=5, rate=0.55):
    enrolled, month, log = 0, 0, []
    while enrolled < target:
        month += 1
        active = min(n_sites, max(0, per_month * (month - first_month + 1)))
        enrolled += active * rate
        if active:
            log.append((month, active, active * rate, enrolled))
    return month, enrolled, log

month, total, log = months_to_enrol(520)
at12 = next(r[3] for r in log if r[0] == 12)
print(f"\nEnrolment complete in month {month} with {total:.1f} randomised")
print(f"Cumulative at month 12: {at12:.1f}")
print(f"Naive model, 45 sites from month 1: {520 / (45 * 0.55):.1f} months")

# Sensitivity to launch value
print("\nlaunch value   Option A rNPV")
for v in (1200, 1100, 900, 700, 550):
    print(f"{v:>8}      {rnpv(A_costs, 4, v, 0.563)[2]:8.1f}")

# Consistency check
print(f"\nStage product {0.520*0.289*0.578*0.906:.4f};"
      f" Phase III entry to approval {0.578*0.906:.4f}")
```

Expected output (Python 3.12, executed):

```text
A: cost PV  120.3  success PV  438.7  rNPV  318.4
B: cost PV  105.0  success PV  228.9  rNPV  124.0
A rNPV per USD peak funding 2.20; B 2.58

Enrolment complete in month 29 with 544.5 randomised
Cumulative at month 12: 123.8
Naive model, 45 sites from month 1: 21.0 months

launch value   Option A rNPV
    1200         358.3
    1100         318.4
     900         238.7
     700         158.9
     550          99.1

Stage product 0.0787; Phase III entry to approval 0.5237
```

Three features of the output are worth reading carefully.

- **Over-enrolment.** The simulation stops after the month in which the cumulative total crosses
  520, producing 544.5 randomised patients. Real trials behave the same way, because sites continue
  to screen until close-out. A pre-specified rule is needed for which patients enter the analysis
  population.
- **B's rNPV is 124.0, not 123.9.** The difference is rounding in the hand calculation; the script's
  full-precision arithmetic is authoritative. Report the script's value in the decision record.
- **The consistency check prints 0.0787 rather than 0.079.** The published cumulative LOA is
  rounded to one decimal place. A difference at the fourth decimal is rounding, not an error.

What the script does not do: it does not model probability distributions, correlate the Phase IIb
and Phase III outcomes (they are positively correlated, so the product 0.62 × 0.563 overstates the
independent-path probability), or apply tax or financing. It is a decision aid, not a valuation of
the company.

## 8. Data, standards, and regulatory context

Development gates sit inside a framework of guidelines and law that constrains what evidence can
be used for a regulatory decision. The framework does not prescribe how a sponsor decides to
continue or stop; that is a commercial decision. It does prescribe what a regulator will accept as
evidence when the sponsor files.

| Instrument | What it covers | Status and bindingness | What it does not constrain |
|---|---|---|---|
| International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) E8(R1), General Considerations for Clinical Studies | Study quality by design, critical-to-quality factors, patient input | ICH guideline adopted 2021; implemented through regional guidance, not directly as law | The sponsor's internal go/no-go thresholds |
| ICH E9(R1), Addendum on Estimands and Sensitivity Analysis | Defining the treatment effect to be estimated, including intercurrent events | Adopted 2019; applies to submissions per regional implementation dates | The choice of endpoint for a gate decision |
| ICH E6(R3), Good Clinical Practice | Quality management, data governance, computerised systems | Adopted 2025, with implementation phased by region | Commercial portfolio decisions |
| ICH M11, Clinical Electronic Structured Harmonised Protocol | Structured protocol content and format | Developed to replace free-text protocols; check current regional status | The evidence a gate consumes |
| Food and Drug Administration Amendments Act (FDAAA) 2007, Section 801 and 42 CFR Part 11 | Registration and results reporting for applicable clinical trials | US law; civil penalties for non-compliance | Internal decision records |
| 21 CFR 314.500-314.520, accelerated approval | Approval on a surrogate endpoint with post-marketing confirmatory obligations | US regulation | Whether a surrogate is adequate for a gate decision |
| PDUFA (Prescription Drug User Fee Act) | Review timelines and commitments | US law, reauthorised periodically | Development timelines before filing |
| EMA PRIME | Enhanced scientific advice and accelerated assessment for priority medicines | EU programme, voluntary | FDA review timelines |
| EU Clinical Trials Regulation 536/2014 and CTIS | Trial authorisation and transparency in the EU | EU law, applicable since 2022; transition period ended | US filing strategy |
| Health technology assessment (HTA) methods (for example the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, NICE, and IQWiG) | Cost-effectiveness and added-benefit assessment | National methods, not regulatory | The clinical evidence package itself |

Two distinctions matter for a gate review.

**A regulatory requirement versus a convention.** Registering an applicable clinical trial and
posting results is a legal requirement in the US. Submitting Study Data Tabulation Model (SDTM) datasets in a marketing
application is a regulatory expectation expressed through guidance and the Data Standards Catalog;
the statute does not name a standard. A stage-gate process, an rNPV hurdle, and a 60% probability
cap are conventions with no regulatory standing. When a document says "required", the first
question is "required by whom, and under what instrument".

**A guideline versus a law.** ICH guidelines are consensus documents developed by regulators and
industry. They become enforceable through regional implementation: in the US through FDA guidance
and regulations, in the EU through the Clinical Trials Regulation and EMA guidelines. A programme
that cites ICH E9(R1) as "the law" overstates the instrument; a programme that ignores it because
it is "only a guideline" understates the review risk.

### 8.1 Data sources for benchmarking and gates

| Source | Content | Granularity | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| ClinicalTrials.gov (API v2) | Registrations, status, sponsors, phase, enrolment | Trial-level | Registered trials only; status updates lag |
| EU Clinical Trials Information System | EU trial authorisations and summaries | Trial-level | EU-only; results quality varies |
| FDA Drugs@FDA and annual novel approvals reports | Approvals, review documents, pathways | Product-level | Approved products only; survivorship |
| Company annual reports and pipeline disclosures | Stage, indication, expected milestones | Company-defined | Selective disclosure; definitions vary |
| Benchmark reports (BIO/Citeline/QLS; IQVIA Institute) | Transition rates by phase and area | Aggregated | Differ in definitions, years, and inclusion |
| Internal trial operations data | Site activation, enrolment, screen failure | Site-level | Small samples; not comparable across eras |
| Claims and EHR data | Feasibility, standard of care, population size | Patient-level | Coding lag; not a trial denominator |

### 8.2 What the standards do not tell you

No standard defines a probability of success, a gate criterion, or a kill threshold. No regulator
requires an rNPV. The numbers in a gate package are therefore sponsor constructs, and they are
auditable only against the sponsor's own stated method. That is why the decision record — base
rate, multipliers, discount rate, launch value, hurdle — matters as much as the result. A number
without its method cannot be reviewed, and a number that cannot be reviewed cannot be defended
after the programme fails.

> **Note:** Regulatory expedited programmes (breakthrough therapy, PRIME, accelerated approval)
> change the timing and pathway to a decision, not the underlying probability that the drug works.
> Treating a breakthrough designation as evidence of efficacy is a category error that appears
> regularly in investment materials.

## 9. Industry practice and case evidence

### 9.1 What sponsors actually do

Large sponsors run formal stage-gate processes with governance committees, independent challenge,
and portfolio-level capital allocation. Mid-size sponsors often run a lighter version with a
single investment committee. Small biotechs typically run no formal gate at all, because the
decision is constrained by cash runway rather than by a comparison of alternatives. In that
setting the gate is the financing event, and the probability that matters is the probability of
raising the next round.

Documented practice across these settings is thinner than the process literature suggests. What is
publicly observable is the output: transitions between phases, disclosed discontinuations, and
licensing deals. Internal decision rules are rarely published. Claims about "best practice" gate
design are therefore mostly inference from outcomes, not observation of process.

### 9.2 Benchmarks and how they differ

| Source | Cohort | Headline figure | Definitional caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| DiMasi, Grabowski and Hansen (2016) | 106 drugs from 10 firms | Capitalised cost per approval USD 2,558 million (2013 dollars); clinical approval success rate 11.83% | Self-selected firms; cost includes cost of capital at 10.5% real |
| BIO/Citeline/QLS (2021) | Programme-indication pairs, 2011-2020 | Cumulative LOA from Phase I 7.9%; Phase II transition 28.9% | Programme-indication pairs; company-submitted data |
| IQVIA Institute (2024) | Phase transitions 2010-2023 | Reported composite success rate 10.8% for 2023 | Restated prior-year figures; different transition definitions |
| FDA (2025 report) | Novel approvals in calendar 2024 | 50 novel drugs; 24 first-in-class; 26 orphan designation | Approved products only; excludes Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER) products |

The difference between 7.9% and 10.8% is not a contradiction. These are different cohorts, years,
and definitions. A gate package that quotes one number without its cohort is not citing evidence;
it is citing a slogan.

### 9.3 Case: EXPEDITION3 and the cost of a subgroup signal

Solanezumab is a monoclonal antibody against soluble amyloid beta. Two Phase III trials in mild to
moderate Alzheimer's disease, EXPEDITION1 and EXPEDITION2, missed their primary endpoints. A
pre-specified pooled analysis of patients with mild disease suggested a smaller decline on the
cognitive subscale, and that subgroup signal supported a third trial in mild dementia.

EXPEDITION3 randomised 2,129 patients with mild dementia and biomarker-confirmed amyloid across
210 sites in 11 countries. The primary endpoint was change in ADAS-cog14 at week 80. The result was
6.65 with solanezumab versus 7.44 with placebo, a difference of −0.80 (95% CI −1.73 to 0.14),
P = 0.10. The trial did not meet its primary endpoint, the hierarchical testing sequence failed,
and the sponsor did not pursue a regulatory submission.

**Documented:** the design, sample size, primary result, and the sponsor's decision not to file.
**Inference:** that the mild-disease subgroup signal was substantially a selection effect, and that
a gate modelling the Phase III effect size as a distribution centred near zero, rather than on the
subgroup point estimate, would have priced the programme very differently. The trial remains a
defensible scientific question; the inference concerns the decision arithmetic, not the biology.

The case illustrates the failure this topic prevents: a subgroup result from trials that failed
overall was treated as a sufficient basis for a large confirmatory programme without an explicit
adjustment for the selection that produced it.

### 9.4 Case: the 2024 approval cohort and the funnel's tail

The FDA's 2024 novel approvals report records 50 novel drugs, of which 24 (48%) were first-in-class
and 26 (52%) had orphan designation; 33 (66%) used at least one expedited pathway, and 37 (74%)
were approved in the first review cycle. These are approvals, not applications, and the report does
not state the denominator of submissions. The figures are consistent with a funnel in which the
final transition is high-probability and the earlier transitions carry the attrition, but they
cannot be used to estimate that attrition. Reading them as a success rate is a denominator error.

### 9.5 What is documented and what is inferred

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Most programmes entering Phase I do not reach approval | Documented across multiple benchmark reports |
| Phase II has the lowest transition probability of the clinical stages | Documented in the 2011-2020 cohort; direction varies by therapeutic area |
| Late-stage failure is economically dominant | Inference from cost per stage and probability of reaching it; consistent with reported cost figures |
| Formal stage-gate processes improve outcomes | Inference; the process literature reports adoption, not controlled outcome comparisons |
| Biomarker selection raises transition probabilities | Documented for specific programmes; the average effect across programmes is not established |

## 10. Failure modes and red flags

| Failure | Early warning sign | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base rate applied to the wrong stage | Stage rates do not multiply to the stated LOA | Confusing conditional transition with cumulative probability | PoS overstated by a factor of two or more | Reproduce the arithmetic before the meeting |
| Sunk-cost continuation | "We have already spent X" appears in the rationale | Prior spend treated as relevant to forward value | Capital committed to a negative-value programme | State that only remaining cost and value enter the decision |
| Effect size taken at face value | Phase III power calculation uses the Phase II point estimate | Winner's curse and regression to the mean | Underpowered trial, null result | Power on the CI lower bound or a shrunk estimate |
| Single-arm result treated as randomised evidence | Historical control used without comparability analysis | Secular trends and patient selection | Effect confounded by era and case mix | Pre-specify external control and run sensitivity analyses |
| Enrolment model ignores activation | Implied per-site rate rises over time | Activation delay absorbed into accrual rate | Timeline slip and higher fixed costs | Model activation explicitly; track implied rate |
| Screen failure omitted | Randomised target equals screening target | Eligibility applied to a screened population | Site burden understated; enrolment misses target | Apply a screen-failure multiplier with a source |
| Probability presented without a denominator | "70% chance of success" with no reference class | Advocacy framing | Governance approves an unjustified number | Require base rate, multipliers, and cap in the record |
| Gate criteria written after the data | Criteria document postdates the data cut | Rationalisation | Programme continues on a moved goalpost | Lock criteria before unblinding; version-control them |
| Unblinded interim leaks to the team | Operational decisions correlate with interim timing | Loss of blinding | Bias in conduct and analysis | Firewall and pre-specified communication rules |
| Analysis population chosen after unblinding | Intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) results diverge and the favourable one is reported | Selection on results | Inflated effect, regulatory query | Pre-specify the estimand and the primary population |
| Timeline built without follow-up lag | Launch date equals enrolment end date | Event-driven endpoints need follow-up | Value overstated by 6-18 months of discounting | Build the timeline from events, not last patient in |
| Indication switching without a new gate | Asset moved to a new indication after a failed readout | Prior evidence does not transfer | Repeat failure in a new population | Treat each indication as a separate decision unit |
| Portfolio ranking by rNPV alone | Funded set exceeds capital or capacity | Ignores the binding constraint | Overcommitment and delayed programmes | Rank by value per unit of the binding constraint |

Two red flags deserve emphasis because they are cheap to detect and expensive to miss. The first
is an internal inconsistency in the probability arithmetic: if the stage rates do not multiply to
the overall figure, the package contains at least one definitional error. The second is a timeline
that ends at the last patient in rather than at the primary analysis: a PFS or survival endpoint
requires follow-up, and a valuation that ignores it overstates value by the discounted period
between enrolment completion and readout.

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why it is needed | What breaks it | How to test it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The reference class resembles the asset | Base rates are meaningful only within a class | A first-in-class mechanism or novel modality | Compare asset characteristics with the class definition |
| Transition probabilities are stable over the programme's life | They are estimated from historical cohorts | Standard of care or regulatory pathway changes | Re-estimate at each gate; track era effects |
| Adjustment multipliers are independent | The scheme multiplies them | Correlated factors (biomarker and genetics often co-occur) | Cap the combined multiplier; test sensitivity |
| Costs are known to within 20% | rNPV requires a cost profile | Protocol amendments, site changes, exchange rates | Range the costs; report the rNPV range |
| Launch value is a commercial estimate | It dominates the rNPV | Pricing reform, competition, payer decisions | Sensitivity analysis and scenario planning |
| The discount rate reflects the cost of capital | It converts future to present value | Financing conditions change | Report rNPV at two rates |
| Enrolment rate is constant per active site | It simplifies forecasting | Learning curve, seasonality, competing trials | Compare actual with forecast monthly |
| Follow-up duration is sufficient for the endpoint | The analysis date depends on it | Lower-than-expected event rate | Event-driven planning, not calendar planning |
| Patients enrolled resemble the intended label population | Generalisation of the result | Eligibility drift during enrolment | Monitor baseline characteristics against the protocol |
| The gate decision is reversible or staged | It limits downside | All-or-nothing commitments | Build stage funding into the decision |

The largest limit is structural: the rNPV is a single number built from at least five uncertain
inputs, and its precision is far lower than its presentation suggests. A useful discipline is to
report the rNPV as a range from the sensitivity analysis and to state which input moves the
decision. If no single input moves the decision, the decision is robust; if several do, the
programme needs more information before commitment, not a more elaborate model.

## 12. Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Reconstruct the funnel (recall, 25 minutes).**
Deliverable: a one-page diagram of the development funnel with, for each stage, the question it
answers, the primary evidence it produces, and the gate decision it informs.
Acceptance criteria: seven stages; each stage names a decision, not a document; no stage labelled
only with a phase number; the diagram can be redrawn from memory.
Time: 25 minutes.

**Exercise 2 — Probability arithmetic (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a calculation of the cumulative LOA from Phase II for a programme with transition
probabilities of 0.35 (II to III), 0.60 (III to filing), and 0.92 (filing to approval), and of the
probability of approval given entry to Phase III.
Acceptance criteria: both numbers correct to three decimal places; the distinction between them
stated in one sentence; denominators shown.
Time: 30 minutes.

**Exercise 3 — Base rate selection (critique, 40 minutes).**
Deliverable: a 400-word critique of a gate memo that applies a 7.9% LOA to a Phase III entry
decision, including the correct figure and the consequence for the decision.
Acceptance criteria: identifies the category error; computes 52.4% or a justified alternative;
states how the error changes the rNPV sign or magnitude.
Time: 40 minutes.

**Exercise 4 — rNPV build (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a spreadsheet or script computing rNPV for a Phase II asset with costs of USD 30m,
35m, and 20m over three years, a 45% probability of approval, a launch value of USD 600m at the
end of year 3, and a 10% discount rate.
Acceptance criteria: cost PV, success PV, and rNPV shown separately; formula readable; answer
within 2% of the reference calculation.
Time: 45 minutes.

**Exercise 5 — Enrolment forecast (application, 40 minutes).**
Deliverable: a month-by-month enrolment table for 30 sites activating at 4 per month from month 3,
at 0.8 patients per site per month, targeting 300 patients; state the completion month and the
cumulative total in the final month.
Acceptance criteria: activation ramp modelled; over-enrolment reported; the naive forecast shown
for comparison.
Time: 40 minutes.

**Exercise 6 — Kill criteria design (design, 50 minutes).**
Deliverable: five kill criteria for a Phase II oncology trial, each with threshold, analysis
population, timing, and the decision that follows.
Acceptance criteria: each criterion falsifiable; at least one addresses safety; at least one
addresses the lower bound of the effect; no criterion requires data the trial does not collect.
Time: 50 minutes.

**Exercise 7 — Red-team a gate package (critique, 60 minutes).**
Deliverable: a red-team memo identifying at least six defects in a supplied gate package (use the
worked example with the errors reintroduced: 28.9% applied to Phase III entry, launch at enrolment
end, no screen-failure multiplier, single point estimate, missing analysis population).
Acceptance criteria: each defect linked to its consequence; at least two defects quantified;
prioritised by impact on the decision.
Time: 60 minutes.

**Exercise 8 — Portfolio allocation (design, 60 minutes).**
Deliverable: a funded set of four programmes from six candidates under a USD 300m two-year budget,
with the ranking rule, the binding constraint, and the two programmes not funded.
Acceptance criteria: ranking rule stated before the selection; arithmetic shown; rejected
programmes have a stated reason and a re-entry condition.
Time: 60 minutes.

## 13. Self-check answers

**Exercise 1.** The seven stages are discovery and target validation, preclinical and
IND-enabling, Phase I, Phase II, Phase III, filing and review, launch and lifecycle. Each stage's
question: is the target causal; is the molecule safe enough and manufacturable; is it tolerable at
exposures with a biological effect; is there a signal in the intended population; is the effect
durable and large enough at scale; is the evidence package sufficient; who should receive it and
at what price. A diagram that lists only documents has not answered the question.

**Exercise 2.** Cumulative LOA from Phase II = 0.35 × 0.60 × 0.92 = 0.1932, or 19.3%. Probability
of approval given Phase III entry = 0.60 × 0.92 = 0.552, or 55.2%. The first is the probability
that a Phase II programme reaches approval; the second is the probability that a programme already
entering Phase III reaches approval. Applying 19.3% to a Phase III decision understates the
probability by a factor of 2.9.

**Exercise 3.** The memo's error is using a cumulative LOA from Phase I (7.9%) for a decision at
Phase III entry. The correct unadjusted figure is 0.578 × 0.906 = 52.4%. At a launch value of USD
800m discounted four years at 9% (USD 566.7m) and Phase III costs with a PV of USD 120m, the
memo's 7.9% gives 44.8 − 120.3 = −75.5m (stop), while 52.4% gives 296.9 − 120.3 = +176.6m
(continue). The error reverses the recommendation.

**Exercise 4.** Cost PV = 30/1.10 + 35/1.10^2 + 20/1.10^3 = 27.27 + 28.93 + 15.03 = 71.23.
Success PV = 0.45 × 600/1.10^3 = 0.45 × 450.79 = 202.86. rNPV = 131.63 USD million. A result
between 129 and 134 is acceptable given rounding.

**Exercise 5.** Cumulative by month 3: 4 × 0.8 = 3.2. Month 4: 8 × 0.8 = 6.4 (cumulative 9.6).
Month 5: 12 × 0.8 = 9.6 (19.2). Month 6: 16 × 0.8 = 12.8 (32.0). Month 7: 20 × 0.8 = 16.0 (48.0).
Month 8: 24 × 0.8 = 19.2 (67.2). Month 9: 28 × 0.8 = 22.4 (89.6). Month 10: 30 × 0.8 = 24.0
(113.6). Thereafter 24.0 per month: month 18 cumulative 305.6, so completion in month 18 with
305.6 patients. The naive forecast is 300/(30 × 0.8) = 12.5 months, a 5.5-month understatement.

**Exercise 6 rubric.** Full marks require five criteria that are falsifiable and pre-specified. A
typical set: ORR lower bound of the 95% CI below 15% at the interim; any grade 5 event related to
study drug; progression-free survival hazard ratio above 1.0 at the interim; more than 20% of the
randomised population unable to receive the planned dose; a safety signal in a pre-specified organ
class exceeding the historical rate by a stated margin. Deduct marks for criteria that require
uncollected data or restate the primary endpoint without a threshold.

**Exercise 7.** The six intended defects are: (1) 28.9% applied to Phase III entry, overstating
risk by a factor of about 1.8 relative to 52.4%; (2) launch dated at enrolment completion, removing
follow-up and lock and overstating value; (3) no screen-failure multiplier, understating site
burden; (4) a single point estimate with no range; (5) the analysis population unnamed, so the
effect estimate cannot be interpreted; (6) the historical control assumed to represent current
practice with no comparability analysis. Credit is given for additional defects that are correctly
quantified.

**Exercise 8 rubric.** Full marks require the ranking rule to be stated before the selection, the
binding constraint to be named, and the arithmetic to be shown. Acceptable ranking rules include
rNPV per dollar of peak funding and rNPV per patient-year, provided the choice is justified by the
constraint. Deduct marks for selecting by rNPV alone when the budget is the stated constraint, and
for rejected programmes without a re-entry condition.

## 14. Interview questions

**1. What is the difference between a Phase II transition rate and a likelihood of approval?**
A strong answer: the transition rate is conditional on reaching Phase II and measures movement to
Phase III; the LOA is the product of all downstream transitions and measures reaching approval from
a stated entry point. The candidate should give an example of how mixing them changes a valuation,
and should mention that both are reference-class estimates, not asset forecasts.

**2. How would you estimate the probability of success for a specific asset?**
Strong: start from the appropriate reference class and stage, adjust with named evidence, cap the
adjustment, record the multipliers, and calibrate against past predictions. Weak: quote a single
published number, or say "it depends on the data" without a method.

**3. Why is a late-stage failure more costly than an early-stage failure, beyond the money spent?**
Strong: the opportunity cost of capital and capacity, the loss of option value on other
programmes, and the information value that a well-designed early stop would have preserved. Weak:
"because it costs more", with no portfolio reasoning.

**4. What would make you stop a programme that has met its primary endpoint?**
Strong: a safety signal, an effect below the clinically meaningful threshold, a population that
does not match the intended label, a data integrity problem, or a commercial value that no longer
clears the hurdle. Weak: "if the p-value is not significant", which confuses statistical and
programme significance.

**5. How do you handle a subgroup result that is positive when the overall trial is negative?**
Strong: treat it as a hypothesis, require a pre-specified analysis or independent replication,
model the effect as a distribution that includes the overall result, and consider the prior
probability that a subgroup effect is real. Weak: assume the subgroup is the true effect and design
the next trial on it.

**6. What is the role of independent challenge in a gate review?**
Strong: a named person without ownership whose task is to state the strongest case against
continuation, with access to the same data. Weak: "to make sure the team did the work", which is
audit, not challenge.

**7. How would you forecast enrolment for a trial in a biomarker-defined population?**
Strong: prevalence times eligible patients per site times activation ramp times willingness, with
screen failure applied, validated against historical site performance, and presented as a range.
Weak: divide target sample size by the number of sites.

**8. What is the difference between a regulatory requirement and a company convention in a gate
package?**
Strong: distinguishes law (registration, GCP implementation), guidance (data standards), and
internal policy (rNPV hurdle, probability cap), and gives an example of each. Weak: treats all
documents as equally binding.

**9. How do you communicate a probability of success to a committee that wants one number?**
Strong: give the number with its base rate, a range, the two assumptions that matter, and what
evidence would change it. Weak: give a single number to avoid confusion, which transfers the
uncertainty to the decision without disclosure.

**10. What would you check first in a gate package you inherited?**
Strong: the probability arithmetic (do the stage rates reconcile), the analysis population, the
enrolment model's implied per-site rate, and the timeline's follow-up assumptions. Weak: start with
the executive summary and accept its numbers.

## 15. Key terms

- **Attrition** — loss of programmes between stages; a rate per transition, with a denominator.
- **Base rate** — transition probability observed in a defined reference class.
- **Conditional transition probability** — probability of reaching the next stage given the current
  stage; the building block of LOA.
- **Cumulative LOA** — product of conditional transitions from an entry stage to approval.
- **Critical-to-quality factor** — an attribute of a study that materially affects the reliability
  of its results, per ICH E8(R1).
- **Decision record** — the document stating the decision, rationale, dissent, conditions, and next
  review date.
- **Estimand** — the precise definition of the treatment effect to be estimated, per ICH E9(R1).
- **Futility boundary** — a pre-specified threshold at which continuing is not worthwhile.
- **Gate** — a formal decision point with defined evidence and options.
- **Kill criterion** — a pre-specified result that ends the programme or indication.
- **Likelihood of approval (LOA)** — reference-class probability of reaching approval.
- **Opportunity cost** — value forgone by committing capital to one programme rather than another.
- **Peak sales** — maximum annual revenue assumed for an asset; not profit.
- **Probability of success (PoS)** — asset-specific estimate, usually an adjusted LOA.
- **Reference class** — the population of comparable programmes used to estimate base rates.
- **Regression to the mean** — the tendency of extreme estimates to be followed by less extreme
  ones.
- **Risk-adjusted NPV (rNPV)** — NPV with cash flows weighted by the probability of reaching them.
- **Screen failure** — a screened patient who does not meet eligibility; reduces randomised yield.
- **Sunk cost** — cost already incurred and irrelevant to forward-looking decisions.
- **Target product profile (TPP)** — the label the programme intends to earn, with minimum and
  target attributes.
- **Winner's curse** — upward bias in the effect estimate selected for having crossed a significance
  threshold.
