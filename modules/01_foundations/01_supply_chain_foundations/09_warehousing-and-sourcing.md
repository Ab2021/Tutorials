## 1. Why this topic exists

A distribution centre (DC) is where an order stops being a message and becomes a parcel. Everything
upstream of it — forecast, plan, purchase order, inbound transport — produces intent. Everything
downstream of it produces a delivery. The building in between is the only place in the chain where
the information flow and the physical flow are reconciled at unit level, by a person, several
thousand times a day.

That reconciliation is expensive. Order picking is commonly cited at around 55% of warehouse
operating cost, and labour is typically half to two thirds of a DC's operating cost. Within picking,
travel is the largest single component of a picker's time — roughly half, in the most widely cited
decomposition. Those two facts explain most of what follows: the building's economics are dominated
by people walking, and the design question is how to make them walk less.

The same topic contains a second, apparently unrelated subject: sourcing. It is in the same file
because the two decisions are made by the same organisation about the same flow, and because both
are exercises in segmenting a large population of small decisions so that management attention goes
where it changes something. A warehouse has 12,000 stock-keeping units (SKUs) and cannot slot all of
them optimally at once. A procurement function has 4,000 suppliers and cannot manage all of them
relationship by relationship. Both need a portfolio rule.

**Why this matters to a data scientist.** Warehouse data is not generated by a neutral instrument.
It is generated by people working to a standard, under a supervisor, against a rate, on a layout
that someone designed five years ago for a different product mix. Every timestamp in a warehouse
management system (WMS) is a record of a physical action performed under those conditions. If you
model pick rates without knowing what the standard is, what the allowances are, and what the
incentive pays for, you will build a model of the measurement system rather than a model of the work.

Three failures show the shape of the problem:

1. **A pick-rate benchmark applied across sites.** An analyst ranks 14 DCs by units per hour (UPH).
   The lowest-scoring site handles small parcels at 4.8 lines per order; the best handles pallets at
   1.1. The ranking measures the mix, not the management.
2. **An automation case built on the vendor's rate.** The case uses 500 lines per hour, a mechanical
   rate measured on an ideal-mix run. Demonstrated rate at a comparable site is 320. Payback moves
   from 2.4 years to 3.9, and at a lower wage it fails inside the asset life.
3. **A supplier scorecard that rewards the wrong supplier.** Unit price carries 40% of the weight.
   The winner is also the only qualified source, holds the tooling, and has no alternate. A leverage
   metric has been optimised on a bottleneck item.

None is a modelling error in the narrow sense; each is a failure to know what the number measures.

## 3. The core mental model

Hold two pictures. The first is a building as a sequence of queues with one shared labour pool. The
second is a two-by-two grid that decides where management effort goes. They are the same idea at
different scales: scarce attention allocated against heterogeneous objects.

### 3.1 The building is a process, not a warehouse

```text
   INBOUND                                                              OUTBOUND
   -------                                                              --------
   truck --> DOCK --> RECEIVE --> PUTAWAY --> STORAGE (reserve)
                                                |
                                                | replenishment
                                                v
                                             PICK FACE --> PICK --> PACK --> STAGE --> DOCK --> truck
                                                ^
                                                |
                                        slotting decides what sits here,
                                        and it decays as the mix changes

   RETURNS: customer --> DOCK --> RECEIVE (returns) --> DISPOSITION --> restock | scrap | repair
```

Six properties of this picture explain most warehouse behaviour.

**Property 1 — Every order crosses every stage, but not every stage costs the same.** Receiving and
putaway amortise over pallets, picking is incurred per line, packing per order, shipping per parcel.
A change in the ratio of orders to lines to units changes the cost structure without changing volume.

**Property 2 — Every stage is a queue, and throughput is the minimum across stages.** A DC with a
4,000-line-per-hour pick operation and a 2,800-parcel-per-hour pack bench ships 2,800. Improving
picking is then worth nothing, which is why projects that mechanise the visible stage rather than
the binding one fail.

**Property 3 — Labour is the flexible resource and absorbs all variability.** Dock doors, rack bays,
and conveyor are fixed within a shift; people are not. Every demand spike, late inbound, and system
outage is resolved by moving or extending labour, so the labour data contains the history of every
other problem in the building.

**Property 4 — The information system both enables and records the work.** A pick confirmation is an
instruction fulfilled and a data point created; there is no independent measurement. This is why
warehouse analytics is unusually vulnerable to the observer effect: change the standard, and
recorded productivity changes even if the work does not.

**Property 5 — Storage is a decision, not a space.** A pallet position commits a cubic volume for a
duration at a cost. A building at 95% occupancy is inflexible rather than efficient, because the
last 5% absorbs inbound variance, and honeycombing means nominal occupancy overstates usable space.

**Property 6 — The returns loop shares the dock and none of the assumptions.** Returns arrive without
appointment, in unknown condition and mixed units, requiring a disposition decision before putaway.
In high-return channels the returns dock is the true constraint and is usually designed last.

### 3.2 Where the time actually goes

The standard decomposition of picker time, as reported in the order-picking literature, is:

| Activity | Share of picker time | What it means | Lever that changes it |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Travel | 50% | Walking between locations, often empty-handed | Layout, slotting, batching, goods-to-person |
| Search | 20% | Finding the item at the location | Labelling, location discipline, pick-to-light |
| Pick | 15% | Grasping and confirming | Packaging design, ergonomics, piece count |
| Setup | 10% | Reading the next task, handling the tote | Screen design, task interleaving |
| Other | 5% | Awaited instructions, interruptions | System performance, supervision |

> **Note:** These shares are a typical case, not a law. The source reports them as a typical
> distribution drawn from an earlier textbook, and notes that case studies show non-travel activities
> can also dominate in specific operations. Use the decomposition to ask which activity you would
> have to change, not to assert that travel is exactly half in your building.

The consequence is counter-intuitive if you have not worked in a DC. Making a picker faster at the
pick — the 15% — improves throughput by at most 15%. Making the picker walk less improves it by up
to 50%. That is why slotting, batching, and goods-to-person are the levers that matter, and why
"pick faster" programmes plateau.

It also explains the shape of the automation market. Every mainstream category attacks travel or
search: goods-to-person brings inventory to a stationary picker, pick-to-light removes search, batch
and cluster picking consolidate trips, and voice frees the eyes and hands without removing walking.

### 3.3 The sourcing lens: the Kraljic portfolio

The Kraljic matrix classifies purchased items on two axes: the profit impact of the purchase, and
the supply risk of the market that supplies it.

```text
                        HIGH
                         ^
                         |   BOTTLENECK          |   STRATEGIC
     supply risk         |   diversify supply    |   partnership
     (scarcity, number   |   secure volume       |   long-term agreement
      of sources,        |   qualify alternates  |   joint development
      substitution,      |   hold buffer stock   |   total cost focus
      logistics risk)    |                       |
                         |   NON-CRITICAL        |   LEVERAGE
                         |   simplify, automate  |   competitive bidding
                         |   catalogue buying    |   volume consolidation
                         |   consolidate vendors |   price benchmarking
                         +-------------------------------------------------> HIGH
                      LOW          profit impact (spend, value, effect on margin)

     Management effort per euro of spend rises upward and to the right.
```

The four strategies are not four adjectives. They are four different operating models, with
different data requirements, different metrics, and different people:

| Segment | Objective | Metric that matters | Data you need | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Non-critical | Minimise transaction cost | Cost per purchase order | Catalogue, spend by buyer | Over-managed; buyers negotiate €400 items |
| Leverage | Extract price | Price versus benchmark index | Spend cube, market indices, bid history | Volume leakage defeats the deal |
| Bottleneck | Secure availability | Days of cover, recovery time | Supplier capacity, lead-time distribution | Price focus destroys the only source |
| Strategic | Create joint value | TCO, innovation, resilience | Cost breakdowns, multi-tier map, joint roadmap | Partnership language, transactional behaviour |

Two properties of the grid matter more than the four labels.

**The axes are judgements, not measurements.** "High" and "low" have no standard thresholds. Profit
impact is usually proxied by annual spend, which conflates a high-volume commodity with a
high-margin-critical component. Supply risk is usually proxied by the number of qualified sources,
which ignores capacity, geography, and substitution. Two competent teams given the same spend data
will place several items in different quadrants.

**Position is a decision, not an observation.** An item is in the bottleneck quadrant because nobody
has qualified a second source, not because the market forbids it. Moving an item from bottleneck to
leverage is a sourcing project with a cost and a lead time. The matrix describes the current state;
the strategy is the move.

### 3.4 Design capacity and demonstrated capacity

Four capacity numbers exist for any automated or manual system, and confusing them is the most common
arithmetic error in warehouse business cases.

| Measure | Definition | Typical relationship | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|---|
| Design (mechanical) capacity | Rate under ideal conditions, ideal mix, no interference | Highest | Vendor specification sheet |
| Rated capacity | Design adjusted for the contractual mix and availability | 70–85% of design | Contract, acceptance test |
| Demonstrated capacity | Best sustained rate achieved in a live comparable operation | 80–95% of rated | Reference site, pilot |
| Effective capacity | Demonstrated × planned availability × planned staffing | 70–90% of demonstrated | Operating budget |

A business case built on design capacity is a case built on a number nobody will ever observe.
Section 6 uses demonstrated capacity and states why.

### 3.5 Where analytics enters

Every analytic task in this topic attaches to one of these objects: pick hours and the shift schedule,
slot locations and the slotting rule, stage capacity and the constraint, spend lines and the category
structure, supplier–item pairs and the management model. The failure is the same in each case. The
model is built on the process as described in the system rather than the process as performed, and
sections 9 and 10 catalogue the specific forms.

## 4. Definitions and vocabulary

| Term | Definition | Common misuse | Unit or coding system |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distribution centre (DC) | A facility that stores and redistributes goods without transforming them | Used interchangeably with warehouse | Facility identifier |
| Warehouse | The physical building and its storage function | Confused with the operation inside it | Facility identifier |
| Fulfilment centre (FC) | A DC whose unit of work is the individual customer order | Used for any e-commerce site | Facility identifier |
| Receiving | The process of accepting inbound goods and recording them | Treated as unloading only | Pallets or lines per hour |
| Putaway | Moving received goods to a storage location | Confused with storage itself | Pallets per hour |
| Cross-docking | Moving goods from inbound to outbound with no putaway | Used for any fast turnaround | Percentage of throughput |
| Flow-through | Case-picked replenishment for stores with minimal reserve storage | Confused with cross-docking | Percentage of throughput |
| Slotting | Assigning SKUs to pick-face locations by expected velocity and affinity | Treated as a one-off project | Assignment, revisited on a cadence |
| Pick face | The location from which a picker takes stock for an order | Confused with the reserve location | Location identifier |
| Reserve storage | Bulk storage that replenishes the pick face | Ignored in picking analysis | Pallet positions |
| Replenishment | Moving stock from reserve to pick face | Treated as free | Replenishment tasks per day |
| Honeycombing | Unusable space created by partial pallets in racking | Counted as occupancy | Percentage of positions |
| Cubiscan / cube | The measured three-dimensional size of an item or case | Assumed from weight | Cubic metres |
| Pallet position | One storage slot for one pallet | Equated with a pallet | Count |
| Stock-keeping unit (SKU) | The finest level at which inventory is managed | Equated with a product | Item–location pair |
| Handling unit | The physical unit moved: each, inner pack, case, pallet | Treated as a SKU | GTIN plus pack level |
| Discrete picking | One order picked at a time, start to finish | Used as the default without comparison | Orders per hour |
| Batch picking | Several orders picked together, sorted later | Confused with wave picking | Lines per hour |
| Zone picking | Each picker owns an area; orders pass between zones | Confused with batch | Lines per hour |
| Wave picking | Orders released in scheduled groups tied to cutoffs | Used for any release logic | Orders per wave |
| Cluster picking | One picker carries several containers and picks for all of them per stop | Confused with batch | Lines per hour |
| Put wall | A sorting wall that distributes batch-picked items to order containers | Called a packing wall | Orders per hour |
| Goods-to-person (G2P) | System brings inventory to a stationary picker | Used for any automation | Lines per station hour |
| Automated storage and retrieval system (AS/RS) | Automated rack or shuttle system that stores and retrieves containers or pallets | Used for conveyors | Container moves per hour |
| Autonomous mobile robot (AMR) | A robot that navigates without fixed infrastructure | Confused with automated guided vehicle (AGV) | Robot hours, moves per hour |
| Automated guided vehicle (AGV) | A robot that follows fixed wires, tapes, or markers | Confused with AMR | Moves per hour |
| Pick-to-light | Lights at the pick location indicate quantity and location | Assumed to remove all errors | Errors per 1,000 lines |
| Voice picking | Speech-directed picking with spoken confirmation | Assumed to be hands-free throughout | Errors per 1,000 lines |
| Radio frequency (RF) scanning | Handheld barcode scanning for pick confirmation | Treated as equivalent to the above | Errors per 1,000 lines |
| Engineered labour standard | A time value for a defined task, derived from time and motion study | Treated as a physical constant | Minutes per task |
| Allowance | Added time for personal needs, fatigue, and delay | Omitted, producing unreachable standards | Percentage of task time |
| Learning curve | The rate at which a new worker approaches standard | Ignored in staffing plans | Percentage improvement per doubling |
| Units per hour (UPH) | Units handled per paid or productive hour | Compared across order profiles | Units per hour |
| Lines per hour | Order lines picked per hour | Confused with UPH | Lines per hour |
| Dock-to-stock | Elapsed time from unload to available-to-promise | Measured from appointment, not arrival | Hours |
| Cycle count | Periodic count of a subset of locations to verify inventory | Confused with a full physical count | Counts per day, accuracy percentage |
| Inventory accuracy | Recorded quantity divided by physical quantity, at location level | Reported at aggregate level | Percentage |
| Shrink | Inventory loss from error, damage, or theft | Reported without cause split | Percentage of value |
| Perfect order | An order that is complete, on time, undamaged, and correctly documented | Equated with on-time in-full (OTIF) | Percentage |
| Spend under management | Share of spend covered by a category strategy or contract | Confused with contracted spend | Percentage of spend |
| Category | A group of purchases managed as one commercial unit | Confused with a commodity code | Category identifier |
| Should-cost | An independent estimate of what an item ought to cost, built from its cost drivers | Confused with the supplier's price | Currency per unit |
| Total cost of ownership (TCO) | All costs of acquiring, using, and disposing of an item | Used as a synonym for landed cost | Currency per unit or per year |
| Supplier rationalisation | Reducing the number of suppliers in a category | Treated as inherently good | Supplier count |
| Dual sourcing | Qualifying and allocating volume to two sources | Confused with splitting every order | Percentage volume split |
| Supplier relationship management (SRM) | The structured management of supplier performance and value | Reduced to a scorecard | Governance cadence |
| Supplier preferencing | The supplier's ranking of its customers, which affects allocation in scarcity | Ignored in buyer-side strategy | Qualitative rank |

Four of these terms carry the most confusion and deserve expansion.

**Design capacity versus demonstrated capacity.** A shuttle system specified at 1,500 tote moves per
hour per aisle will not move 1,500 in your building: interference, operator pace, replenishment, and
exceptions consume the difference. The vendor's number is a mechanical limit; the number for a
business case is the one observed at a reference site with a comparable profile, minus a haircut for
your own mix.

**Engineered standard versus observed average.** A standard is the time a qualified worker should
take at a defined pace with defined allowances. An observed average includes the learning curve,
system downtime, and the incentive effect. Comparing a standard with an average from another
building is not a comparison.

**Landed cost versus TCO.** Landed cost adds freight, duty, and fees to the invoice. TCO adds quality,
inventory carried because of lead time, supplier management, and expected disruption. A 6-week ocean
supplier on 30-day terms is cheaper landed and more expensive in TCO than a 2-week domestic supplier.

**Category versus commodity code.** A category is a commercial construct that can be sourced and
negotiated together; a commodity code is an accounting construct. A spend cube built on commodity
codes fragments categories that belong together and merges ones that do not.

## 5. The mechanics, step by step

This section traces a unit through the building and a category through the sourcing process. Each
step names the physical event, the data artifact, and the branch that changes the answer.

### Phase A — Inbound

**Step 1. The inbound is announced.** Physical: nothing yet. Data: an advance ship notice (ASN) or
booking, ideally with handling-unit identifiers. Branch: **is the ASN accurate?** Receiving without a
trustworthy ASN means counting and identifying at the door, which turns a 20-minute unload into a
90-minute one and destroys dock scheduling.

**Step 2. The vehicle is received.** Physical: trailer docked, seal checked, pallets unloaded. Data:
arrival time, door, carrier, seal number, pallet count. Branch: **appointment or turn-up?**
Appointment-driven receiving smooths labour; turn-up receiving moves the variance to the yard and to
detention cost.

**Step 3. Goods are received against the order.** Physical: counts taken, damage noted, labels applied
if absent. Data: receipt record with quantity, condition, lot, and expiry. Branch: **blind receipt or
against the ASN?** Blind receipt improves count accuracy and slows the process; receiving against the
ASN propagates the supplier's errors.

**Step 4. Quality and compliance checks run.** Physical: sampling, temperature, documentation. Data:
accept, reject, or quarantine with a reason code. Branch: **is the reason code a taxonomy or free
text?** Free text makes supplier quality unanalysable, and this is a design decision paid for
annually.

**Step 5. Goods are put away.** Physical: move to a reserve location, scan location and handling unit.
Data: putaway task with source, destination, and timestamp. Branch: **directed or free putaway?**
Directed putaway preserves slotting logic and cube utilisation; free putaway is faster per task and
destroys both.

### Phase B — Storage and picking

**Step 6. Storage is allocated and decays.** Physical: pallets occupy positions and leave partial
ones. Data: location master, occupancy, cube, weight limits. Branch: **when was the last re-slot?**
Slotting is an optimisation against a demand profile that moves with promotions, seasonality, new
items, and discontinuations.

**Step 7. Orders are released.** Data: order pool, priority rules, cutoffs, carrier schedules. Branch:
**wave or continuous release?** Waves batch work and create end-of-wave idleness; continuous release
smooths flow and needs enough open orders to batch.

**Step 8. Replenishment is triggered.** Physical: stock moved from reserve to pick face. Data:
replenishment task and trigger rule (min/max, demand-based, opportunistic). Branch: **is
replenishment counted in pick productivity?** Often not, so buildings where pickers replenish badly
look more productive.

**Step 9. Picking occurs.** Physical: travel, search, grasp, confirm. Data: task timestamps, in
theory at pick level and in practice often at tote or order level. Branch: **which picking method?**
Discrete for large orders with few lines, batch for many small orders, zone for very high volume,
cluster for one picker carrying several containers.

**Step 10. Packing and value-added work.** Physical: consolidation, packing, labelling, kitting. Data:
pack confirmation, carton type, weight, dimensions. Branch: **is pack time inside order cycle time?**
It usually is, and it is usually the largest non-pick component for small-parcel channels.

**Step 11. Shipping.** Physical: staged by carrier and route, loaded, manifested. Data: parcel or
pallet record, carrier, service level, manifest, bill of lading (BOL). Branch: **is the cutoff real or
nominal?** A 16:00 cutoff that carriers routinely miss by 40 minutes shows up as late orders
attributed to picking.

### Phase C — Returns and recovery

**Step 12. Returns are received and dispositioned.** Physical: unloaded, inspected, graded. Data:
return authorisation, reason code, disposition (restock, repair, liquidate, scrap). Branch: **is the
return reason trustworthy?** Default coding ("customer changed mind") hides fit, damage, and mis-pick
causes that are operationally fixable.

**Step 13. Restocked returns re-enter inventory.** Physical: putaway to a returns location or to the
pick face. Data: inventory adjustment with condition code. Branch: **are returns counted as available
inventory?** If yes, service looks better and quality looks worse; the choice must be explicit and
consistent with the promise made to customers.

### Phase D — The sourcing process

Sourcing is a cycle, not an event. These steps assume a category that has been through one full cycle.

**Step 14. Spend is analysed and classified.** Data: purchase line items normalised to supplier, item,
business unit, and period. Branch: **what is the taxonomy?** A tree built from the chart of accounts
produces categories that cannot be sourced; one built from supplier and item descriptions requires
cleaning and judgement.

**Step 15. The supply market is analysed.** Data: qualified sources, capacity, cost drivers,
geographic concentration, tariff exposure. Branch: **item level or technology level?** A component
with three suppliers may have one supplier of its underlying sub-component, which makes the three an
illusion.

**Step 16. Items and suppliers are segmented.** Data: profit impact proxy, supply risk proxy, scored
and reviewed. Branch: **who sets the thresholds?** Set by the analyst alone, the segmentation will not
survive contact with the business.

**Step 17. A strategy is chosen per segment.** Data: a strategy statement with a target, a lever, and
an owner. Branch: **is the strategy a verb or a noun?** "Strategic partnership" is a noun.
"Consolidate to two sources and co-develop the next generation with one" is a strategy.

**Step 18. Should-cost and TCO are built.** Data: cost breakdown (material, conversion, tooling,
logistics, overhead, margin) and volume assumptions. Branch: **bottom-up or benchmarked?** Bottom-up
from process and material is defensible in negotiation; benchmarked from other quotes is a price
estimate with a different name.

**Step 19. The sourcing event runs and the contract is set.** Data: request for information (RFI),
request for quotation (RFQ), bids, award, terms. Branch: **what is awarded: price or TCO?** An award
on unit price with a quality clause that is never enforced is an award on unit price.

**Step 20. Performance is managed.** Data: delivery, quality, cost, responsiveness, and innovation
measures at a stated cadence. Branch: **does the review change anything?** A quarterly business review
with no consequence is a meeting, not supplier relationship management.

## 6. Worked example

A single-channel retailer operates one DC. All figures are illustrative but internally consistent,
and every assumption is stated so that you can substitute your own.

### 6.1 The operation

| Parameter | Value | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| Orders shipped per day | 8,000 | Average day, 300 operating days per year |
| Lines per order | 2.4 | Measured over the last 12 months |
| Lines per day | 19,200 | 8,000 × 2.4 |
| Productive pick rate | 55 lines per hour | Discrete picking with RF scanning, mixed each and case |
| Paid-to-productive ratio | 1.18 | Breaks, briefings, waiting, system downtime |
| Paid hours per shift | 7.5 | After breaks; shifts are 8 hours |
| Absence and turnover cover | 12% | Scheduled headcount above required |
| Loaded labour rate | €18.50 per paid hour | Wage, payroll cost, agency margin |
| Operating days per year | 300 | |

As a sanity check against the market: 8,000 orders over 61.5 scheduled heads and 7.5 hours is
17.3 orders per scheduled head-hour. A 2025 vendor benchmark of WMS customer data reports a mean of
23.5 orders picked per hour per person, with a range from 5.6 (third-party logistics operators) to
41.7 (one sector). A mixed-profile retailer at 17.3 is plausible and not a red flag. This is exactly
the kind of comparison that is useful for orientation and dangerous for judgement.

### 6.2 Pick labour arithmetic

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---:|
| Productive pick hours per day | 19,200 ÷ 55 | 349.1 h |
| Paid hours per day | 349.1 × 1.18 | 411.9 h |
| Required full-time equivalents (FTEs) | 411.9 ÷ 7.5 | 54.9 |
| Scheduled headcount | 54.9 × 1.12 | 61.5 |
| Paid hours per year | 411.9 × 300 | 123,570 h |
| Annual pick labour cost | 123,570 × €18.50 | €2,286,045 |

Call it €2.29m per year of pick labour, before supervision, before packing, before receiving, and
before any of the indirect labour that supports it. The building's total labour bill is larger; this
is the pool that goods-to-person would address.

### 6.3 The automation proposal

A goods-to-person shuttle system: totes stored in a grid, robots delivering totes to stationary pick
stations.

| Capital item | Cost (€m) |
|---|---:|
| Shuttle grid, lifts, and racking | 2.40 |
| Five pick stations | 0.55 |
| Totes, conveyors, and station fit-out | 0.75 |
| Warehouse execution system (WES) and WMS integration | 0.60 |
| Building works and charging | 0.30 |
| Contingency at 5% | 0.23 |
| **Total capital** | **4.83** |

| Recurring item | Annual cost (€k) | Basis |
|---|---:|---|
| Maintenance and spares | 320 | 8% of €4.0m hardware |
| Software licence and support | 90 | Contract |
| Energy | 55 | Measured comparable site |
| **Total recurring** | **465** | |

Operating assumptions after automation:

| Parameter | Value | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| Stations | 5 | |
| Demonstrated station rate | 320 lines per hour | Reference site, comparable mix |
| Station capacity per day | 24,000 lines | 5 × 320 × 15 operating hours |
| Utilisation at current demand | 80% | 19,200 ÷ 24,000 |
| Picking hours per day | 60.0 | 19,200 ÷ 320 |
| Decant and replenishment hours | 21.0 | 35% of picking hours |
| Control room and maintenance | 15.0 | 2 FTE × 7.5 h |
| Exception and returns handling | 12.0 | Residual manual work |
| Total labour hours per day | 108.0 | |

The vendor's specification for this class of station is 500 lines per hour. The 320 used here is a
demonstrated rate from a similar site, already below the 400–425 that a 15–20% haircut on the
mechanical rate implies. The further gap is order mix: a 2.4-line average with variable item sizes
produces more station-side handling than the single-line runs used for acceptance testing.

### 6.4 Payback and NPV at the base case

```text
manual paid hours per day      = 411.9
automated labour hours per day = 108.0
hours saved per day            = 303.9
hours saved per year           = 303.9 x 300 = 91,170
gross annual saving at EUR 18.50 = 91,170 x 18.50 = EUR 1,686,645

net annual benefit = 1,686,645 - 465,000 = EUR 1,221,645
simple payback     = 4,830,000 / 1,221,645 = 3.95 years

NPV at 10% over 8 years, annuity factor 5.335:
NPV = 1,221,645 x 5.335 - 4,830,000 = EUR 1,687,000
```

So the base case passes: a 3.95-year simple payback, a positive NPV of about €1.69m over eight
years, and a capital asset with a ten-year mechanical life. This is a real but unspectacular case,
and it is roughly half as attractive as the vendor's illustrative 2.4-year payback, mostly because
of the demonstrated-rate haircut and the recurring costs the vendor quotation excluded.

### 6.5 The same calculation at a lower labour cost

Now move the DC to a region where the loaded rate is €12.00 rather than €18.50. Nothing else
changes.

```text
gross annual saving = 91,170 x 12.00 = EUR 1,094,040
net annual benefit  = 1,094,040 - 465,000 = EUR 629,040
simple payback      = 4,830,000 / 629,040 = 7.68 years
NPV at 10%, 8 years = 629,040 x 5.335 - 4,830,000 = EUR -1,474,000
```

The project is now value-destroying on an eight-year horizon. The equipment is identical, the
throughput is identical, the service improvement is identical. Only the price of the labour it
replaces has changed, and that single number flips the decision.

### 6.6 Sensitivity

| Scenario | Gross saving (€k) | Net benefit (€k) | Payback (years) | NPV 8 yr at 10% (€k) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Base: €18.50, 320 lines/h | 1,687 | 1,222 | 3.95 | +1,687 |
| Low wage: €12.00, 320 lines/h | 1,094 | 629 | 7.68 | −1,474 |
| Low rate: €18.50, 260 lines/h | 1,583 | 1,118 | 4.32 | +1,134 |
| Both: €12.00, 260 lines/h | 1,027 | 562 | 8.60 | −1,833 |

The breakeven loaded wage, holding everything else at base, is the wage at which NPV is zero:

```text
required net benefit = 4,830,000 / 5.335 = EUR 905,300
required gross saving = 905,300 + 465,000 = EUR 1,370,300
breakeven wage = 1,370,300 / 91,170 = EUR 15.03 per hour
```

Below roughly €15 per loaded hour, this system does not clear a 10% hurdle over eight years. That
is the single most important number in the business case, and it is usually absent from the deck.

> **Note:** The conclusion is not "automation is bad in low-wage regions." It is that the case is a
> function of labour cost, labour availability, and turnover, and that the same machine is a good
> investment in one labour market and a poor one in another. Where turnover is high enough that the
> wage understates the true cost of a filled position, the case improves; where the operation cannot
> staff the manual alternative at any wage, the case becomes one of feasibility rather than return.

### 6.7 Applying the same discipline to a sourcing decision

A component is bought at 1.2 million units per year. Two suppliers are qualified. The question is
whether to move from 100% single-source to a 70/30 split.

| Cost element | Supplier A (offshore) | Supplier B (nearshore) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Unit price (€) | 4.10 | 4.38 |
| Inbound freight and duty (€/unit) | 0.12 | 0.05 |
| Quality cost: inspection, scrap, rework (€/unit) | 0.09 | 0.02 |
| Inventory carrying cost from lead time (€/unit) | 0.07 | 0.03 |
| Expected disruption cost (€/unit) | 0.05 | 0.01 |
| Supplier management cost (€/unit) | 0.01 | 0.02 |
| **TCO per unit (€)** | **4.44** | **4.51** |

On unit price, Supplier A wins by 28 cents. On TCO, Supplier A still wins, but by 7 cents. The
70/30 split has a blended TCO of about €4.46 plus roughly €0.02 of added complexity cost, or €4.48:
about 4 cents per unit, or €48,000 per year, as the price of not having a single point of failure on
a component worth €5.3m of annual spend.

That is the whole argument, and it is a judgement rather than a calculation. The calculation gives the
price of the insurance; whether it is worth paying depends on what a stoppage would cost, which is a
separate model with much wider error bars. Presenting the premium and the exposure separately is
honest. Netting them into one "risk-adjusted TCO" hides the judgement inside an assumption.

### 6.8 What an analyst should have asked

1. Is 55 lines per hour a standard, an average, or a best-shift figure? They differ by 20–30%.
2. Does the manual baseline include replenishment and exceptions, or only pure picking? Crediting
   automation with work the baseline never counted overstates the saving.
3. Is €18.50 fully loaded, including agency margin and supervision, or is it the base wage?
4. What happens to the 61.5 people, and at what cost?
5. Does the released space have a cash value? In a leased building that cannot be sublet, it is zero.
6. Is the reference-site rate from a matched profile, or from a showcase site?
7. What is the ramp curve? Months one to six will be below demonstrated rate, and the case usually
   assumes full rate from month one.

## 7. Code, query, or calculation

The arithmetic in section 6 is deliberately hand-computable. This section gives one small function
so that the sensitivity can be run without a spreadsheet, then the shortcut rules that matter more
than the code.

### 7.1 One function for the payback and the breakeven wage

```python
def automation_case(capex, hours_saved_year, wage, annual_cost,
                    life=8, rate=0.10):
    """Simple payback, NPV, and breakeven wage for a labour-substitution case."""
    af = (1 - (1 + rate) ** -life) / rate          # annuity factor
    gross = hours_saved_year * wage
    net = gross - annual_cost
    payback = capex / net if net > 0 else float("inf")
    npv = net * af - capex
    breakeven_wage = (capex / af + annual_cost) / hours_saved_year
    return {"payback_years": round(payback, 2),
            "npv": round(npv),
            "breakeven_wage": round(breakeven_wage, 2)}

automation_case(4_830_000, 91_170, 18.50, 465_000)
# {'payback_years': 3.95, 'npv': 1687000, 'breakeven_wage': 15.03}
```

The only subtlety is the annuity factor: it converts a level annual benefit into a present value, so
that a long-lived asset is not judged on simple payback alone. Simple payback ignores everything
after the payback date, which is why a 7.7-year payback on a 10-year asset can still be a poor
investment once discounting is applied.

### 7.2 The conversion chain

Orders → lines → productive hours → paid hours → headcount → annual cost. Each factor is a place
where an assumption enters. The two that matter most are lines per order and the demonstrated pick
rate; state both before showing a headcount.

### 7.3 The number to argue about

Capital cost, maintenance, and rate can all be bounded by evidence. The loaded wage is the number
that differs between sites, and it is usually taken from a single HR figure that excludes agency
margin, overtime, and supervision. Run the sensitivity on the wage and the demonstrated rate before
arguing about the discount rate.

There is no standard for supplier segmentation, and this is not an oversight. The axes are

### 8.5 What is convention rather than requirement

- A 10–15% personal, fatigue, and delay allowance is a convention from time study, not a regulatory
  minimum.
- ABC thresholds of 80/15/5, a quarterly re-slot, and "one dock door per 10,000 square feet" are
  inherited rules of thumb, not optima.
- "Three quotes" is a procurement convention, not evidence that the third quote is competitive.

## 9. Industry practice and case evidence

### 9.1 What the warehouse labour market actually looks like

Four 2025 sources, all with different sampling frames, converge on a consistent picture.

| Source | Sample | Finding | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| BLS Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey, June 2025 | US establishment survey | Transportation, warehousing, and utilities separation rate 4.1% per month, roughly 49% annualised | B |
| Modern Materials Handling / Peerless Research Group, 2025 | 101 warehouse and DC operations | 55% report annualised turnover at or below 15%; 29% report 16–30%; temporary labour is 16.6% of peak workforce | C |
| UKG, September 2025 | 400 HR and operations leaders at firms above $1bn revenue | 22% report turnover above 45%; 79% say labour is not aligned to demand; real hourly earnings up 4.8% year on year | C |
| CleoHR Frontline Hiring Index, 2025 | Frontline hiring data | Warehouse and logistics turnover reported at 73%; 43% of frontline workers leave within 90 days | C |

The spread between "55% below 15%" and "22% above 45%" is a sampling difference, not a contradiction:
the trade survey samples operations that answer trade surveys, and the HR survey samples large firms
with high-churn urban sites. Assuming one turnover rate for "the industry" assumes away the variance
that drives the decision.

Replacement cost estimates vary even more widely: about 16% of annual wages for hourly workers in one
staffing source, roughly 30% in a Department of Labor figure, 30–150% in a vendor analysis, and a
loaded $15,000–25,000 per frontline hire in the CleoHR data. The disagreement is definitional. Build
the cost from components rather than importing a percentage.

### 9.2 Productivity benchmarks and why they mislead

A 2025 benchmark drawn from one WMS vendor's customer base reports a mean of 23.5 orders picked per
hour per person, with sector means ranging from 5.6 for third-party logistics providers to 41.7 in
one sector, and a top performer at 128. This is Grade D evidence: it is customer data from a
particular software product, self-selected, and measured on whatever each customer calls an order.

It is still useful as an order-of-magnitude check, and as a demonstration of the point: a 23-fold
spread between sector means is not a spread in effort but in what an "order" is. Normalising requires
lines per order, units per line, and storage type, and even then the comparison stays rough.

### 9.3 Automation: claims, counter-claims, and what is actually measured

Amazon reported deploying its one-millionth robot in mid-2025 and launching a generative model for
fleet coordination, claiming about a 10% reduction in fleet travel time. These are company claims
(Grade C) and they are specific enough to be meaningful, which is unusual in this category.

Independent evidence is more mixed and more interesting.

- **Safety.** A 2025 study in *ILR Review* using OSHA establishment panels and BLS state panels
  found that robotic fulfilment centres had roughly 40% fewer severe injuries but roughly 77% more
  non-severe injuries, attributed to reduced task variety and a faster pace. That is a real
  trade-off, not a marketing position. (Grade A)
- **Employment.** A 2025 meta-analysis in the *Journal of Economic Surveys*, covering 33 studies and
  644 employment estimates, found a mean partial correlation between robotisation and employment of
  −0.02 and with wages of −0.03, both close to zero after correcting for publication bias, with
  negative effects concentrated in US manufacturing, medium-skilled occupations, and long-run
  specifications. (Grade A)
- **Warehouse-specific displacement.** A 2025 press analysis argues that, after adjusting for
  productivity growth, fulfilment centre employment fell by roughly 25% over a two-year period while
  package volumes rose. This is an opinion piece using public data (Grade E) and should be treated as
  a hypothesis, not a measurement.

The honest summary: at the level of the economy, robotisation does not reliably destroy net
employment; at the level of a specific building, it reliably changes the composition of work, the
injury profile, and the skill mix. Both statements are true and they answer different questions.

Automation project outcomes are equally contested. A 2026 industry analysis citing EY reports that
30–50% of robotics and automation projects fail, and that 20–40% in consumer goods, retail, and
e-commerce deliver materially lower returns than planned. (Grade D; the underlying EY figure is not
independently verifiable from the summary.) The stated causes are process and master-data readiness,
slotting, and inbound data quality, not the equipment. A vendor engineering note adds a related and
more useful point: mechanical and operational rates differ by 15–20%, and peak acceptance testing is
the only way to close the gap. (Grade D, but consistent with section 6.)

### 9.4 Sourcing practice: the matrix is universal, the execution is not

The Kraljic portfolio remains the dominant segmentation framework. A 2025 study in the *Journal of
Purchasing and Supply Management* benchmarking 17 leading firms found continued dominance of the
matrix alongside growing interest in sourcing-lever frameworks, and reviewed more than 40 portfolio
models. (Grade A)

Adoption of the artifact is much higher than adoption of the behaviour it implies:

| Finding | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 94% of organisations have segmented suppliers | State of Flux, 2024 Global SRM Research | C |
| Only 42% have a defined treatment strategy for all segments (leaders 85%, followers 29%) | State of Flux, 2024 | C |
| Segmentation criteria are spend (83%) and business criticality (83%); risk is third (51%) | State of Flux, 2024 | C |
| Top-quartile firms implement 10 or more of 22 SRM practices and realise about 50% more supplier value | Vantage Partners SRM benchmarking, 900+ respondents | C |
| Firms rated a "customer of choice" are 29 times more likely to receive suppliers' best people, pricing, and ideas | Vantage Partners | C |
| 41% of procurement leaders are renegotiating with existing suppliers; 74% are actively developing alternate sources | Deloitte 2025 Global CPO Survey, 250+ CPOs | C |
| Strategic supplier relationship management is the largest internal capability gap, cited by 32% | ProcureCon CPO Report 2025 | C |

The gap between 94% segmentation and 42% treatment strategies is the most useful number in the table:
most organisations can produce the two-by-two, and few can act on it. That is the academic critique
in one line — the matrix classifies, and classification is the easy part.

### 9.5 The critique of the portfolio model

The standard critiques are old, well documented, and still unanswered in practice.

| Critique | Substance | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Measurement subjectivity | No standard rule for high versus low on either axis; positioning is judgement | Gelderman and van Weele (2003) |
| Static | Two dimensions and four boxes; categories move and the model does not | Later portfolio literature, reviewed in Fontes et al. (2025) |
| Supplier-blind | Ignores the supplier's own strategy and the network around it | Portfolio critique literature |
| Prescription-light | "Partner" and "exploit" do not say how, or when to move an item | Gelderman and van Weele; practitioner commentary |
| Missing sustainability | Two axes omit environmental and social risk, now material in regulated categories | Majidi et al. (2025) |

Two practical extensions are worth knowing because they address the supplier-blind critique.

**Supplier preferencing** reverses the lens: suppliers also rank their customers, and in a tight
market that ranking determines who gets volume, technical support, and early access to new products.
A buyer who classifies an item as strategic while being a low-priority customer has mis-specified the
relationship. The Vantage "customer of choice" finding is the quantified version, and it is Grade C
evidence from a benchmark study rather than an audit.

**The relationship spectrum** replaces the binary partnership-or-transaction choice with a range:
adversarial, arm's length, single-sourced, framework agreement, strategic alliance, partnership, joint
venture, vertical integration. Each point has a different governance cost and, importantly, a
different exit cost, which the two-by-two does not make visible.

### 9.6 Dual sourcing: what the evidence supports

Dual sourcing is often presented as free resilience. The research is more conditional.

- A 2025 lot-sizing model for critical raw materials found that disruption from a critical
  high-volume supplier can raise total cost by up to 23%, reduced to around 10% with a higher
  spot-market share. That is quantification of the exposure, not of the benefit. (Grade A)
- A 2025 game-theoretic paper in the *International Journal of Production Research* found that
  dual sourcing is optimal for a monopolist but that competing retailers can be jointly better off
  single-sourcing — dual sourcing becomes a prisoner's dilemma, and minimum order quantities can
  sustain a more profitable single-source equilibrium. (Grade A)
- A 2025 comparison of dual sourcing and AI-enabled sourcing in *Risk Analysis* found dual sourcing
  advantageous when disruption probability is high and local production cost is moderate, but noted
  that supplier pricing behaviour erodes the advantage. (Grade A)

The practical reading: dual sourcing buys a reduction in outcome variance at a known and usually
small unit-cost premium, and it costs qualification effort, lost volume leverage, and management
overhead. It is worth it for bottleneck and strategic items with real disruption exposure, and
usually not for leverage items where the second source is a negotiation tactic, not a capability.

## 10. Failure modes and red flags

| Failure | Early warning sign | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Benchmarking rates across order profiles | One site is an outlier on UPH | Lines per order and units per line differ | Wrong management action, damaged trust | Normalise by lines, units, and storage type before comparing |
| Using design capacity in a business case | Payback under two years for a mature technology | Vendor rate measured under ideal conditions | Capital committed to a case that cannot be met | Use demonstrated rate from a comparable site, then haircut |
| Omitting recurring costs | Business case lists capex and labour only | Maintenance, licences, energy, and support excluded | Payback understated by 20–40% | Build a full TCO with a recurring line for each contract |
| Ignoring the wage level | Same case proposed for sites in different labour markets | Saving scales linearly with wage | Value destroyed in low-wage sites | Compute the breakeven wage and compare it to each site |
| Slotting treated as a one-off | Pick productivity drifts down 2–3% per year | Velocity changes, new items, discontinuations | Travel creeps back; no one owns it | Re-slot on a cadence; monitor travel per line |
| Replenishment excluded from pick hours | Pick rate improves after automation, total labour does not | Work moved to a different task code | Saving double-counted | Measure at process level, not task level |
| Incentive gaming | Rate rises while cost per unit is flat | Time shifted to indirect codes, cherry-picking | Productivity gain is an artifact | Triangulate with cost per unit and effectiveness |
| Learning curve ignored in ramp | Month-one throughput below plan | New hires and new system | Service failure in the transition | Model a ramp curve and staff for it |
| Supplier scorecard weighted to price | Bottleneck item awarded to cheapest bid | Price is measurable, risk is not | Single point of failure created | Segment first, then choose metrics per segment |
| Segmentation without treatment strategy | A four-box slide with no owners | Classification mistaken for strategy | No behaviour changes | Require a strategy, a target, and an owner per segment |
| Category taxonomy from the chart of accounts | Categories that cannot be sourced | Accounting logic, not commercial logic | Fragmented spend, lost leverage | Build the taxonomy from supplier and item, then reconcile |
| Should-cost equal to the last price | "Target" is a 5% reduction on current | Anchor on incumbent | Negotiation without information | Build bottom-up from cost drivers |
| Dual sourcing without qualification | Second source named but never ordered from | No volume, no learning, no capability | Illusion of resilience | Allocate a minimum volume and audit it |
| Returns excluded from capacity | Peak planning misses the returns dock | Returns share labour and dock doors | Inbound and outbound both late | Model returns as a separate demand stream |

Two of these deserve a note.

**Incentive gaming** is the most analytically seductive failure because the data looks clean. A
workforce paid on a performance percentage will optimise the denominator, typically by shifting time
to indirect codes or manipulating scan sequences to earn location-change credits. The defence is not
tighter policing but measuring at the level the business cares about — cost per unit shipped, cost per
order, perfect order rate — and comparing the two views. If the standard says productivity rose 12%
and cost per unit is flat, it is measuring something other than work.

**Segmentation without treatment strategy** is the most common sourcing failure, and it is invisible
because the deliverable exists. A matrix with four colours is not a strategy. The test is whether
anyone can name, for each strategic supplier, the joint initiative, the executive sponsor, and the
metric that would show the relationship delivering. If not, a classification has been called
management.

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why it is needed | What breaks it | How to test it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pick rate is stable within a shift | Converting lines to hours requires a single rate | Fatigue, shift pattern, temperature, mix | Compare first-hour and last-hour rates |
| Lines per order is stable | Volume converts to workload | Channel mix, promotions, seasonality | Plot lines per order by week and channel |
| Paid-to-productive ratio is constant | Headcount derives from it | Briefings, system outages, absence | Measure from WMS login to task time |
| Labour is the binding constraint | Justifies the automation saving | Dock, pack, or transport capacity binds first | Capacity check at every stage |
| Wage is fully loaded | Saving per hour is a cash saving | Agency margin, supervision, overtime excluded | Reconcile to payroll and agency invoices |
| Displaced labour is actually removed | Saving is real, not theoretical | Redeployment, attrition-only reduction, union agreement | Agree the mechanism before approving capital |
| Space released has a cash value | Justifies part of the case | Leased building that cannot be sublet | Check lease terms and local market |
| Demonstrated rate transfers to your mix | Rate is the core input | Different order profile, item sizes, seasonality | Pilot, or reference site with matched profile |
| Supplier risk is proxied by supplier count | Makes risk scorable | Capacity concentration, single site, single tier-2 | Map tier-2 and site concentration |
| Spend is a proxy for profit impact | Makes the axis computable | Low-spend, margin-critical components | Test with margin sensitivity per item |
| Category boundaries are stable | Allows year-on-year comparison | New products, divestments, outsourcing | Review the taxonomy annually |
| Supplier performance data is comparable | Enables scorecards | Different definitions, different sites | Audit one supplier's data end to end |

Two limits of the framing are worth stating plainly.

**The Kraljic portfolio is a prioritisation device, not an explanation.** It tells you where to spend
management attention, not whether a supplier will fail, whether a price is fair, or whether a market
will tighten. Treating the quadrant as a prediction is a category error.

**The warehouse cost model in this topic is a labour model.** Labour dominates variable cost, which
makes this the right first model. It is the wrong only model because it ignores capital productivity,
service, and safety, and a building that hits its labour budget while injuring more people and
shipping later is not well run. Module 13 develops the fuller model.

```text
lines per day        = 12,000 x 1.8 = 21,600
productive hours     = 21,600 / 48 = 450.0
paid hours           = 450.0 x 1.22 = 549.0
FTEs                 = 549.0 / 7.5 = 73.2
scheduled headcount  = 73.2 x 1.10 = 80.5
annual paid hours    = 549.0 x 320 = 175,680
annual cost          = 175,680 x 17.00 = EUR 2,986,560
```

The assumption to test first is 48 lines per productive hour: it drives the answer and is the most
likely to have been measured on a different method or profile. A 10% error moves annual cost by about
€299,000, more than the entire effect of the absence factor.

A common error is applying absence to hours rather than headcount. That gives 499.1 paid hours and
66.5 FTEs, understating headcount by about 14 people and cost by roughly €500,000. Absence is a
property of the schedule.

**Exercise 3 arithmetic.**

```text
annuity factor, 8 years at 10% = (1 - 1.1^-8) / 0.10 = 5.335

At EUR 16.00:
gross saving = 60,000 x 16.00 = 960,000
net benefit  = 960,000 - 310,000 = 650,000
payback      = 3,200,000 / 650,000 = 4.92 years
NPV          = 650,000 x 5.335 - 3,200,000 = EUR 268,000

At EUR 13.00:
gross saving = 780,000
net benefit  = 470,000
payback      = 6.81 years
NPV          = 470,000 x 5.335 - 3,200,000 = EUR -693,000

breakeven wage = (3,200,000 / 5.335 + 310,000) / 60,000
               = (599,813 + 310,000) / 60,000
               = EUR 15.16 per hour
```

At €16 the project clears the hurdle by a modest margin; at €13 it destroys about €0.69m. The
breakeven wage of €15.16 means the same equipment is a good decision in one labour market and a bad
one 200 kilometres away. Where the network can choose where the DC sits, the automation case and the
network decision should be evaluated together.

**Exercise 4 rubric.** A strong answer states the scoring rule before showing the result. Profit impact
is scored on something defensible — annual spend, spend as a share of item cost, or margin at risk —
and the choice is argued. Supply risk uses at least three inputs: qualified sources, capacity
concentration, and lead-time or logistics exposure. It identifies one item a spend-only rule would
misfile, typically a low-spend single-source component that belongs in bottleneck. Each strategy
statement is an action with an owner. A weak answer uses spend as both axes, gives no scoring rule, or
writes strategies as adjectives without a verb, target, or owner.

**Exercise 5 rubric.** A strong answer has six or more elements, each with a stated basis, and
separates measured from assumed. Disruption cost is an assumption with a range, and the recommendation
separates the premium (about 4 cents per unit in the worked example) from the exposure avoided. It
notes what the TCO omits: supplier financial health, capacity, and whether the second source can
scale. A weak answer compares landed cost and calls it TCO.

**Exercise 6 rubric.** A strong policy slots on velocity (picks per location per week) plus affinity,
sets a re-slot cadence of one to two times per year, and names three decay metrics: share of picks
from the top 20% of locations, travel per line, and replenishment tasks per pick. The trigger is a
threshold, for example travel per line up 8% from the post-slot baseline. It acknowledges the labour
cost, disruption, and error risk of re-slotting. A weak answer slots on total units, sets no cadence,
and gives no measurable trigger.

**Exercise 7 rubric.** A strong critique reconstructs the vendor's payback, then changes at least four
assumptions: demonstrated rate, paid-to-productive ratio, recurring cost, and ramp curve. It reports
the payback under each change and identifies the two that dominate, labels vendor and company claims,
and states what evidence — a pilot, a reference-site visit, a contractual rate — would change the
conclusion. A weak critique disputes numbers without recomputing them.

**Exercise 8 model answer.** Components: recruiting, onboarding and training, vacancy overtime, ramp
productivity loss, and separation administration. For 120 associates at 40% separations, that is 48
separations per year.

```text
recruiting and advertising      48 x EUR 1,200  =  57,600
onboarding and training         48 x EUR 2,400  = 115,200
vacancy overtime (6 weeks avg)  48 x EUR 1,900  =  91,200
ramp productivity loss          48 x EUR 2,200  = 105,600
separation administration       48 x EUR   500  =  24,000
total                                          = 393,600
cost per separation                            =   8,200
```

Present as a range of roughly €0.32m to €0.55m, because the ramp and overtime components are least
certain. The largest effect per euro is usually in the first 90 days: if 43% of leavers go inside 90
days, the interventions that matter are scheduling predictability, first-week onboarding, and
supervisor quality, not pay alone.

**Exercise 9 rubric.** A strong memo names the constraint explicitly: one qualified source, long
qualification lead time, supplier-owned tooling, or a tier-2 concentration that makes apparent
competition illusory. The strategy is availability-first: qualify a second source, hold strategic
buffer, or design out the dependency. Make-versus-buy includes capability — whether the firm can
manufacture, whether the process is core, and what the learning curve would cost — not only unit
cost. The metric is days of cover or recovery time. A weak memo treats the item as strategic because
it is expensive and recommends "partnership" without saying what changes.

## 14. Interview questions

**1. "What is a warehouse for?"**

A strong answer describes it as a set of processes that decouples supply from demand at a cost:
receiving, storage, picking, packing, shipping, returns. It names the trade-off between service,
cost, and inventory, and notes that the building's economics are dominated by labour and travel. A
weak answer says "to store things", which describes the smallest part of the cost.

**2. "Why is picking the most expensive activity in a DC?"**

A strong answer gives the two-part reason: picking is incurred per line, and lines are numerous
relative to pallets; and within picking, travel is roughly half of the time and adds no value. It
connects this to slotting, batching, and goods-to-person as the levers. A weak answer says "because
it takes the most people" without explaining why.

**3. "How would you estimate the labour required to ship 8,000 orders a day?"**

A strong answer walks the chain: orders × lines per order ÷ pick rate = productive hours; × paid
ratio = paid hours; ÷ shift length × absence factor = headcount. It states the assumptions and says
which one it would test first. A weak answer quotes an industry ratio without asking about order
profile.

**4. "You are given a vendor's throughput number. What do you do with it?"**

A strong answer distinguishes mechanical, rated, demonstrated, and effective capacity, asks for the
acceptance-test conditions and the order mix, and applies a haircut validated against a reference
site or pilot. A weak answer uses the number, or dismisses it entirely.

**5. "Why might the same automation be a good investment in one country and a bad one in another?"**

A strong answer explains that the benefit is a wage multiplied by hours saved, while the capital
cost is fixed, so the payback moves with the loaded wage. It gives the breakeven wage as the decision
variable and mentions labour availability and turnover as secondary drivers. A weak answer says
"labour is cheaper there", which is the premise, not the analysis.

**6. "What is the productivity paradox in warehouse automation?"**

A strong answer notes that measured productivity can rise while cost per unit does not, because work
moves to another task code, support labour is excluded, or the constraint was elsewhere. It adds that
aggregate evidence is near zero with wide heterogeneity; a weak answer calls the paradox a myth.

**7. "Walk me through the Kraljic matrix."**

A strong answer names the axes, the quadrants, and the strategy for each, then adds the three
critiques: subjective axes, a static model for moving categories, and no execution guidance. A weak
answer draws the boxes and stops.

**8. "An item is single-sourced and expensive. What quadrant is it in, and what do you do?"**

A strong answer separates spend from risk: expensive alone does not make an item strategic; single
source with no qualified alternate makes it bottleneck, or strategic if value is also high.
Qualifying an alternate or designing out the dependency follows, and a price negotiation is the wrong
first move. A weak answer says "strategic, so partner".

**9. "How do you decide between single and dual sourcing?"**

A strong answer frames it as the price of insurance: the unit-cost premium against the exposure
avoided, with qualification cost and lost volume leverage included. It notes that dual sourcing can be
a prisoner's dilemma and that a nominal second source without volume is not a second source. A weak
answer calls dual sourcing free resilience.

**10. "How would you detect that a warehouse incentive scheme is being gamed?"**

A strong answer triangulates: compare the engineered-standard measure against cost per unit shipped
and against perfect order rate. If the standard improves while cost per unit is flat, work has moved
rather than disappeared. It names the mechanisms — time shifting, cherry-picking, scan manipulation —
and the response: team measures, quality gates, transparent standards. A weak answer says "audit the
data".

## 15. Key terms

- **Distribution centre (DC)** — a facility that stores and redistributes without transforming.
- **Receiving** — accepting and recording inbound goods; **putaway** — moving them to storage.
- **Cross-docking** — inbound to outbound with no putaway; **flow-through** — case picking for stores.
- **Slotting** — assigning SKUs to pick-face locations by velocity and affinity.
- **Pick face** — the location picked from; **reserve** — bulk storage that replenishes it.
- **Discrete picking** — one order at a time; **batch** — several orders, sorted later.
- **Zone picking** — one picker per area; **wave** — orders released in scheduled groups.
- **Goods-to-person (G2P)** — inventory travels to a stationary picker.
- **AS/RS** — automated storage and retrieval, usually shuttle or crane based.
- **AMR** — autonomous mobile robot; **AGV** — guided vehicle on fixed paths.
- **Pick-to-light / voice / RF** — pick confirmation technologies with different error profiles.
- **Engineered labour standard** — time for a defined task at a defined pace, with allowances.
- **UPH / lines per hour** — productivity units; not comparable across order profiles.
- **Inventory accuracy** — recorded versus physical quantity at location level.
- **Design capacity** — ideal rate; **demonstrated** — best sustained rate in a live operation.
- **Kraljic portfolio** — segmentation of purchases by profit impact and supply risk.
- **Leverage / strategic / bottleneck / non-critical** — the four sourcing segments.
- **Should-cost** — independent estimate of what an item ought to cost.
- **TCO** — total cost of ownership across the life of the item.
- **Supplier preferencing** — the supplier's ranking of its customers.
- **Dual sourcing** — two qualified sources with allocated volume.
