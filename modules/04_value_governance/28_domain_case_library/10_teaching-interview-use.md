> Module 28 — Domain Case Library and Sector Playbooks  
> Topic 10 of 10  
> Estimated study time: 4 hours

> Version 2026.5

## 1. Why this topic exists

A memorable company story is not yet a decision exercise.
A classroom can accurately repeat a headline while never deciding what the manager should do.
An interview candidate can describe a famous company while leaving their own role, evidence, and trade-offs unclear.
Both failures conceal the reasoning that a supply-chain role requires.

A case exercise makes a decision under limited information observable.
It states who can act, when the choice must be made, what options are feasible, and what outcomes matter.
It also makes uncertainty visible: a good decision can have a bad realized outcome, and a lucky outcome can follow weak reasoning.
Evaluation should therefore score the decision process, not agreement with a facilitator's preferred answer.

The instructor's problem is to create a bounded, fair opportunity to demonstrate judgment.
The hiring manager's problem is to compare job-relevant evidence across candidates, not to reward fluency or familiarity with a particular company.
The learner's problem is to move from a reported result to an appropriately qualified decision in a different setting.

This unit uses a synthetic replenishment case throughout.
All case numbers, people, and results are invented for instruction; they are not claims about a real organization.
Public sources later in the unit support specific methodological points, not the synthetic case's numerical outcomes.

## 2. Learning outcomes

After this unit you can:

1. Convert a case narrative into a decision prompt with an owner, deadline, choices, constraints, and deliverable; exercise 1 assesses this.
2. Separate source evidence, interpretation, missing information, and assumptions; exercises 2 and 5 assess this.
3. Calculate and compare expected operational contribution and downside under explicit scenarios; exercise 3 assesses this.
4. Design a bounded pilot and specify a decision gate; exercises 4 and 6 assess this.
5. Facilitate participation and handle evidence updates without revealing an answer; exercise 7 assesses this.
6. Apply an anchored rubric to reasoning rather than the selected option; exercise 8 assesses this.
7. Produce a structured interview answer that distinguishes personal contribution from team results; exercise 9 assesses this.
8. Identify transfer conditions and limits before treating a published case as a recommendation; exercises 2 and 5 assess this.

## 3. The core mental model

```text
Source + provenance -> case facts -> decision at a specified time
                         |                |
                     uncertainty       feasible options
                         |                |
                         +--> learner reasoning <-- constraints
                                    |
                          decision + evidence + limits
                                    |
                          observable behavior and rubric
                                    |
                      feedback -> revised decision -> learning
```

A case is a selective account, not a complete system model.
Its evidence may establish an event without establishing why it happened.
The facilitator chooses which information the learner receives and when.
That choice should reproduce the decision-time information boundary, not smuggle hindsight into the prompt.

There are three distinct perspectives.
The decision owner chooses an action using the information then available.
The facilitator designs the environment and moderates discussion.
The assessor records observable reasoning against pre-established criteria.
One person may fill several roles, but should make those roles explicit.

A decision exercise has no obligation to make every option equally attractive.
It does need a credible alternative and enough disclosed information to justify comparison.
If a critical assumption changes the ranking, learners should see or be asked to identify that sensitivity.
A case may support more than one defensible recommendation when risk preferences or unobserved constraints differ.

For an interview answer, the corresponding flow is context, decision, personal action, evidence, result, and reflection.
The speaker must not turn a team result into a personal achievement or imply that correlation proves causation.

## 4. Definitions and vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Frequent misuse | Unit or boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Case | Bounded account of a decision and its context | Treating an anecdote as a causal estimate | Named place and period |
| Decision owner | Role authorized to select an action | Confusing analyst with approver | Person or role |
| Decision point | Time at which action must be selected | Using later results as known facts | Date and time |
| Option | Feasible action the owner can choose | Comparing a plan to an impossible ideal | Operational scope |
| Constraint | Limit that an option must respect | Calling a preference a legal requirement | Currency, time, or capacity |
| Evidence | Information bearing on a claim | Treating assertion as independent verification | Source and observation period |
| Claim | Proposition offered as true | Assuming a quoted statement is established fact | Claim and claimant |
| Inference | Interpretation derived from evidence and assumptions | Presenting interpretation as directly observed | Reasoning chain |
| Synthetic case | Invented example for teaching | Attributing invented results to an actual company | Explicitly invented |
| Counterfactual | Outcome under an alternative not observed for the same unit and time | Calling prior-year performance a controlled comparison | Defined unit and interval |
| Baseline | Reference condition for comparison | Assuming baseline establishes causal effect | Scope and period |
| Scenario | Internally consistent possible future | Treating it as a prediction | Probability, if justified |
| Expected value | Probability-weighted average across scenarios | Assuming average is a guaranteed outcome | Currency per decision horizon |
| Downside | Loss or unfavorable performance in an adverse condition | Omitting safety or service effects | Currency and service units |
| Pilot | Bounded test designed to inform a larger decision | Calling an uncontrolled rollout an experiment | Sites, duration, and gate |
| Guardrail | Limit on harm or exposure during an intervention | Treating an aspirational target as an enforceable stop | Metric and threshold |
| Transfer | Application of a lesson to another context | Assuming a result travels unchanged | Source and destination settings |
| Rubric | Criteria and performance anchors for scoring | Scoring the answer's style alone | Criteria and levels |
| Calibration | Shared review of anchors and sample responses | Altering ratings to create artificial agreement | Assessor and exercise |
| Behavioral evidence | Observable statement or action relevant to a criterion | Inference from confidence or accent | Recorded observation |
| Structured interview | Consistent job-related questions and scoring | Treating identical wording as sufficient validation | Role and assessment process |
| Forecast | Estimate of an uncertain future | Confusing it with a promise | Quantity and horizon |
| Plan | Chosen intended action | Calling an unapproved proposal a commitment | Scope and period |
| Target | Desired outcome | Assuming a target is an achieved result | Metric and threshold |
| Commitment | Authorized obligation to act or deliver | Confusing an aspiration with an approved commitment | Owner and deadline |

## 5. The mechanics, step by step

The following procedure converts source material into an assessable decision.
It is also a repeatable protocol for a case discussion or a structured interview.

### Step 1: Specify the job to be done

Input: a role description, business problem, or learning objective.
Write the decision verb, object, and horizon: for example, choose a six-week replenishment pilot for a regional distribution center.
State who owns the decision and who is affected by it.
Exclude objectives that cannot be observed in the allotted session.
Output: one sentence defining the decision and another defining the assessed behavior.
Branch: if the job requires negotiation rather than quantitative prioritization, include a role interaction instead of an unrelated arithmetic test.

### Step 2: Assemble an evidence packet

Input: primary reports, operational extracts, case accounts, and expert statements.
Record source, author, publication date, observation period, measures, and incentives for every material claim.
Separate direct observations from attributed claims, interpretation, and synthetic assumptions.
Check whether an apparently independent report merely repeats a vendor announcement.
Use the module's A–E grades as a provenance aid, not a guarantee that every claim from an A source is unbiased or transferable.
Output: a claim–source ledger and an explicit list of unknowns.
Branch: if decision-critical evidence is missing, either disclose the gap or supply a clearly labeled synthetic assumption; never invent verification.

### Step 3: Fix the information boundary

Input: the case chronology and decision deadline.
Build a timeline showing what was knowable before the choice and what became known later.
Put post-decision results in a sealed reveal, not the initial prompt.
State the observation window, units, and whether numbers are actual, estimates, forecasts, or targets.
Output: an initial packet and a facilitator-only later-information packet.
Branch: if the case has no decision-time information, ask learners to reconstruct plausible alternatives without claiming historical accuracy.

### Step 4: Define feasible options

Input: authority, capacity, budget, operating rules, and stakeholder requirements.
Include the status quo and at least one realistic alternative.
State what each option changes, what it costs, and who must authorize it.
Identify irreversible consequences and whether a smaller test is possible.
Output: a comparable option table, with a common horizon and metric definitions.
Branch: if an option violates a hard constraint, reject it before ranking expected value; do not hide the violation inside a weighted score.

### Step 5: Choose evidence and decision criteria

Input: business objectives, operational exposure, and stakeholder needs.
Separate minimum safety, legality, and service requirements from preferences among feasible options.
Define contribution, service, risk, and learning metrics, including denominators and measurement cadence.
Choose whether and how to value financial outcomes; never add unlike units without an explicit conversion.
Output: criteria with owners, units, and non-negotiable thresholds.
Branch: if stakeholders disagree on risk tolerance, show how a different threshold changes the recommendation.

### Step 6: Construct scenarios

Input: a baseline, uncertain drivers, and defensible bounds.
List plausible states and the outcome of each option in each state.
Use probabilities only when supplied or explicitly assumed; they must total 1.
Compute expected value and inspect adverse states separately.
State that scenario-weighted value is conditional on assumptions, not a prediction of realized value.
Output: a scenario matrix, weighted calculations, and at least one sensitivity or break-even calculation.
Branch: if probabilities cannot be defended, present ranges or a threshold decision rather than a false point estimate.

### Step 7: Design the learner's deliverable

Input: the decision, evidence, constraints, and available time.
Request a recommendation, alternative, reasoning, uncertainty, and implementation or stopping rule.
Make the length appropriate to the exercise, for example a one-page memo or a three-minute verbal answer.
Supply a calculation sheet or accessible equivalent when numerical computation is not itself the skill being measured.
Output: a prompt that another facilitator can administer without inventing hidden requirements.
Branch: if an interview requires proprietary experience, offer a hypothetical alternative and grade the same competencies.

### Step 8: Prepare facilitation and reveals

Input: a timed agenda and evidence packet.
Give independent thinking time before group discussion to reduce immediate anchoring.
Invite each participant to state an option and a reason before debating the group consensus.
Reveal material new information at a predetermined time and ask whether it changes the decision.
Output: a run sheet with timing, prompts, and contingency for accessibility or time loss.
Branch: if discussion becomes dominated, use a round-robin or written input, not public criticism of the dominant person.

### Step 9: Design and calibrate scoring

Input: the learning objectives or job analysis and the expected deliverable.
Write observable criteria and performance anchors before reading responses.
Have assessors independently score the same sample, then discuss evidence for disagreements.
Revise ambiguous anchors before live scoring; do not move the goalposts after seeing a preferred candidate.
Output: a rubric, scoring guide, and documented calibration decisions.
Branch: if assessors disagree because the response is ambiguous, record the uncertainty and use an agreed follow-up; do not infer missing behavior.

### Step 10: Debrief and transfer

Input: the decisions, actual reveal if available, and rubric observations.
Ask what was known at the time, which assumptions drove the choice, and which new fact would reverse it.
Separate assessment of decision quality from the outcome that happened later.
Apply the transfer test to a new setting: decision, process, data, constraints, and economics must be comparable enough for the proposed use.
Output: a short feedback note and a revised decision or test plan.
Branch: if evidence does not transfer, retain the case as an analogy and identify the local test needed before adoption.

### Decision-exercise design checks

### Authority

**Prompt:** Who can authorize the proposed action?

**Action:** Name the accountable role and any approval gate.

**Reason:** A recommendation outside the owner's authority is not an executable decision.

### Timing

**Prompt:** When must the decision be made?

**Action:** State the deadline and cadence of review.

**Reason:** Late evidence may not have been available when the decision was made.

### Baseline

**Prompt:** What happens without intervention?

**Action:** Describe current practice and its costs.

**Reason:** A comparison requires an actionable reference, not an imaginary zero-cost option.

### Scope

**Prompt:** What units are affected?

**Action:** Name sites, products, customer segments, and time window.

**Reason:** Benefits and harms cannot be compared if their populations differ.

### Option symmetry

**Prompt:** Are options described at similar detail?

**Action:** Disclose implementation effort and risks for each alternative.

**Reason:** Asymmetric detail can steer learners toward the instructor's favorite choice.

### Data quality

**Prompt:** Can the metric be trusted?

**Action:** Specify missingness, latency, and definition changes.

**Reason:** The precision of arithmetic cannot repair unreliable inputs.

### Trade-offs

**Prompt:** What must be traded against value?

**Action:** Name service, workload, cash, and exposure as applicable.

**Reason:** Maximizing a single measure can hide operational harm.

### Reversibility

**Prompt:** Can the action be stopped?

**Action:** Describe rollback, owner, and deadline.

**Reason:** Reversible pilots and irreversible commitments need different evidence thresholds.

### Stakeholders

**Prompt:** Who experiences the consequences?

**Action:** Map affected operators, customers, suppliers, and decision makers.

**Reason:** The financial owner may not observe all service or workload effects.

### Uncertainty

**Prompt:** Which unknown could change the choice?

**Action:** Ask for a threshold or sensitivity analysis.

**Reason:** A decision can be robust even when a point estimate is uncertain.

### Fair access

**Prompt:** Can people demonstrate the competency without specialist background?

**Action:** Define acronyms and give all candidates the same relevant case facts.

**Reason:** Unnecessary domain trivia can obscure the skill under assessment.

### Evidence updates

**Prompt:** How is new information handled?

**Action:** Set a predetermined reveal and ask for a revision.

**Reason:** Changing one's mind in response to reliable evidence can be strong judgment.

### Outcome separation

**Prompt:** What is evaluated before knowing the result?

**Action:** Assess the ex ante decision and its rationale separately from realized outcomes.

**Reason:** Luck and external shocks can dominate outcomes.

### Transfer

**Prompt:** What differs in the destination setting?

**Action:** Compare process, data, economics, and constraints.

**Reason:** Similarity of company names or technology labels does not establish transfer.

### Feedback

**Prompt:** What will a learner do differently?

**Action:** Give one observed strength and one specific change tied to the rubric.

**Reason:** General praise or criticism does not support improvement.

### Practice probes for case designers

Use these compact situations as facilitator prompts or to rehearse an interview answer.
Each response should state what is known, what is assumed, and what would change the action.
They are instructional scenarios, not reports of actual company behavior.

**Decision ownership.**
- Situation: The analyst recommends a pilot but lacks budget authority.
- Ask or do: Ask who can approve and which expense ceiling applies.
- Why it matters: A good analysis must reach the person empowered to act.
- Boundary: Do not infer approval from an analyst's plan.

**Decision horizon.**
- Situation: A six-week benefit is compared with an annual cost.
- Ask or do: Put every cash figure on a common horizon before ranking options.
- Why it matters: Mismatched periods create misleading ratios.
- Boundary: Do not extrapolate six weeks to a year without seasonality and cost assumptions.

**Status quo.**
- Situation: The proposed tool is compared with zero operational effort.
- Ask or do: Estimate costs and service under the actual manual process.
- Why it matters: The relevant counterfactual is an actionable alternative.
- Boundary: The status quo can change too, so describe the assumed baseline.

**Decision rights.**
- Situation: A planner can override recommendations but cannot cancel supplier orders.
- Ask or do: Map which actions are reversible and by whom.
- Why it matters: An override is not a universal rollback mechanism.
- Boundary: Check actual supplier terms before promising reversibility.

**Budget cap.**
- Situation: A pilot costs USD 10,000 against a USD 12,000 cap.
- Ask or do: Confirm whether staff time and integration expense are included.
- Why it matters: A stated cap applies to the agreed cost definition.
- Boundary: Do not infer spare budget without reconciling cost categories.

**Service constraint.**
- Situation: A scenario projects 92% service against a 94% threshold.
- Ask or do: Clarify whether the threshold is a hard precondition or a weekly stop.
- Why it matters: These policy interpretations lead to different feasible choices.
- Boundary: A monitored stop cannot undo service failures already experienced.

**Threshold arithmetic.**
- Situation: The stable-state advantage is USD 16,000 and volatile loss is USD 8,000.
- Ask or do: Solve p × 16,000 − (1 − p) × 8,000 = 0.
- Why it matters: The stable-state break-even probability is one third.
- Boundary: The threshold changes if either state payoff changes.

**Probability provenance.**
- Situation: The prompt supplies a 60% stable-state probability without a source.
- Ask or do: Label the number as an assumption and explore a range.
- Why it matters: Conditional arithmetic should not be mistaken for empirical certainty.
- Boundary: No sensitivity range proves that the assumed probability is calibrated.

**Unmodeled state.**
- Situation: A supplier outage is not in the two-state matrix.
- Ask or do: Add a separate plausible state or explain why it is excluded.
- Why it matters: Omitted states can reverse expected value and alter feasibility.
- Boundary: Additional states require defensible probabilities or a nonprobabilistic treatment.

**Correlated uncertainty.**
- Situation: A promotion affects demand and supplier lead time simultaneously.
- Ask or do: Model the joint scenario instead of assuming independent changes.
- Why it matters: Correlated shocks can increase stockout exposure.
- Boundary: Do not assign a correlation coefficient without relevant evidence.

**Cost inclusion.**
- Situation: The pilot matrix already includes USD 10,000 of implementation cost.
- Ask or do: Avoid subtracting the same amount again from the pilot value.
- Why it matters: Double counting distorts the comparison.
- Boundary: Verify the matrix's cost definition before using it.

**Service denominator.**
- Situation: A weekly service figure is reported without order-line counts.
- Ask or do: Request eligible lines and on-time fulfilled lines.
- Why it matters: A percentage without its denominator obscures volume and precision.
- Boundary: Define cancellations, backorders, and split shipments consistently.

**Service aggregation.**
- Situation: Overall service looks healthy but one customer group deteriorates.
- Ask or do: Inspect prespecified relevant segments alongside the aggregate.
- Why it matters: An aggregate can mask concentrated harm.
- Boundary: Avoid identifying individuals in shared teaching materials.

**Small samples.**
- Situation: One missed order out of ten changes service by 10 percentage points.
- Ask or do: Report numerator, denominator, and the small-sample limitation.
- Why it matters: A volatile rate may not reflect a stable process change.
- Boundary: The operational stop may still apply even when statistical certainty is low.

**Data latency.**
- Situation: Service data arrive nine days after each weekly review.
- Ask or do: Redesign the monitoring cadence or use a reliable leading signal.
- Why it matters: A late indicator cannot support a genuinely weekly stop.
- Boundary: A leading signal needs a clear relationship to the actual guardrail.

**Missing data.**
- Situation: Stockout timestamps are absent for a subset of SKUs.
- Ask or do: Quantify missingness and check whether it differs by option.
- Why it matters: Differential missingness can bias the comparison.
- Boundary: Imputation does not automatically remove systematic error.

**Metric gaming.**
- Situation: Planners delay entering difficult orders to improve reported service.
- Ask or do: Audit data timestamps and define the start of the service clock.
- Why it matters: Operational measures influence behavior as well as record it.
- Boundary: A single metric rarely captures every undesirable response.

**Comparison group.**
- Situation: Pilot SKUs are high-volume products and controls are slow movers.
- Ask or do: Match or stratify on relevant pre-intervention characteristics.
- Why it matters: Different demand patterns can explain apparent pilot effects.
- Boundary: Matching observed factors cannot eliminate unobserved confounding.

**Random allocation.**
- Situation: The site can allocate eligible SKUs to pilot or control before launch.
- Ask or do: Randomize where feasible and document assignment and exceptions.
- Why it matters: Randomization can reduce systematic allocation differences.
- Boundary: Small samples, spillovers, and noncompliance still limit interpretation.

**Contamination.**
- Situation: A planner applies pilot insights to control SKUs.
- Ask or do: Record cross-use and choose a design that minimizes spillovers.
- Why it matters: The observed difference may understate or distort the intervention contrast.
- Boundary: Do not claim clean separation if behavior crossed the boundary.

**Seasonality.**
- Situation: The pilot runs in a promotion while the baseline covers a quiet month.
- Ask or do: Use a contemporaneous comparison and record promotion exposure.
- Why it matters: A time-only comparison confounds intervention and demand changes.
- Boundary: A matched comparison still depends on comparable exposure.

**Attribution.**
- Situation: Sales rise after the recommendation tool is introduced.
- Ask or do: Separate the measured change from the estimated causal effect.
- Why it matters: Price, demand, availability, and promotions can change simultaneously.
- Boundary: A before-and-after increase alone does not identify the tool's contribution.

**Source independence.**
- Situation: Three news articles quote the same vendor press release.
- Ask or do: Trace each article to its underlying source.
- Why it matters: Repeated reporting of one claim is not three independent observations.
- Boundary: Independent reporting may still share data or incentives.

**Source grade.**
- Situation: A peer-reviewed paper studies a different product and geography.
- Ask or do: Evaluate measurement and contextual fit in addition to provenance.
- Why it matters: A high-grade source can be irrelevant to the local decision.
- Boundary: A source grade is not a numeric probability that a claim is true.

**Disclosure.**
- Situation: A company reports a percentage improvement but no baseline.
- Ask or do: Ask for the original level, denominator, period, and costs.
- Why it matters: A relative percentage cannot reveal absolute business value alone.
- Boundary: Do not fill missing inputs with invented company facts.

**Decision-time evidence.**
- Situation: A learner uses a result published after the manager acted.
- Ask or do: Separate the initial packet from the later reveal.
- Why it matters: Decision quality depends on what could reasonably be known at the time.
- Boundary: Later evidence is useful for learning, not retroactive clairvoyance.

**Reveal design.**
- Situation: The facilitator introduces a supplier promotion halfway through discussion.
- Ask or do: Announce the update at the same point for all comparable groups.
- Why it matters: A common reveal makes revisions comparable and prevents accidental coaching.
- Boundary: The promotion does not establish a particular demand probability.

**Facilitator neutrality.**
- Situation: An instructor says the pilot is clearly the modern choice.
- Ask or do: Replace the value judgment with a request for evidence and trade-offs.
- Why it matters: Authority cues can anchor learners before they reason independently.
- Boundary: Neutrality does not mean concealing a genuine safety constraint.

**Participation.**
- Situation: Two people speak while four remain silent.
- Ask or do: Use individual writing followed by a timed round-robin.
- Why it matters: Structured turn-taking can surface alternatives without public confrontation.
- Boundary: Do not force disclosure of personal experiences or disabilities.

**Disagreement.**
- Situation: One learner favors manual planning and another favors a conditional pilot.
- Ask or do: Ask each to state the relevant service-policy interpretation and evidence.
- Why it matters: Different assumptions can produce different defensible decisions.
- Boundary: Check that each interpretation is actually permitted by the prompt.

**Time pressure.**
- Situation: A 45-minute discussion falls ten minutes behind.
- Ask or do: Protect individual reasoning and the debrief; shorten optional discussion.
- Why it matters: These stages make reasoning observable and produce learning.
- Boundary: Do not skip an accommodation or alter one candidate's scoring criteria.

**Rubric alignment.**
- Situation: A criterion rewards confidence but the role requires analytical judgment.
- Ask or do: Replace it with observable evidence use and clear reasoning anchors.
- Why it matters: Fluency is not the same as decision quality.
- Boundary: Do not infer competence from accent, speed, or disability.

**Assessor calibration.**
- Situation: Two raters award different scores to the same answer.
- Ask or do: Have each cite the exact observation linked to the anchor.
- Why it matters: Disagreement can reveal ambiguous criteria or missed evidence.
- Boundary: Agreement after discussion does not by itself establish predictive validity.

**Follow-up probe.**
- Situation: A candidate says 'we improved service' without explaining their role.
- Ask or do: Ask what they personally did and what evidence they observed.
- Why it matters: A neutral probe separates individual contribution from team achievement.
- Boundary: Use consistent job-relevant probes and do not demand confidential detail.

**Hypothetical response.**
- Situation: A candidate lacks permission to discuss a prior employer's case.
- Ask or do: Offer a clearly labeled hypothetical using the same competencies.
- Why it matters: The candidate can demonstrate reasoning without breaching confidentiality.
- Boundary: Do not represent a hypothetical outcome as achieved experience.

**Interview outcome.**
- Situation: A candidate chose a sound action but an external shock caused failure.
- Ask or do: Assess the decision-time rationale and the observed learning separately.
- Why it matters: A bad result can follow a reasonable choice under uncertainty.
- Boundary: Do not ignore preventable execution errors merely because uncertainty existed.

**Score interpretation.**
- Situation: A learner earns eight of ten on the exercise rubric.
- Ask or do: Explain the criterion-level evidence and the scale's limited purpose.
- Why it matters: The total summarizes this exercise, not overall job performance.
- Boundary: Do not use an unvalidated score as an automatic hiring cutoff.

**Privacy.**
- Situation: A case packet includes a rare customer shipment with exact dates.
- Ask or do: Minimize fields or replace the case with synthetic values.
- Why it matters: Combined operational attributes can identify a person or organization.
- Boundary: Removing a name does not guarantee anonymization.

**Accessibility.**
- Situation: The task uses a timed spreadsheet although spreadsheet speed is not the competency.
- Ask or do: Provide an equivalent accessible format and appropriate accommodation.
- Why it matters: The assessment should isolate the job-relevant skill.
- Boundary: Confirm the chosen adaptation with applicable policy and the individual's needs.

**Automation.**
- Situation: A language model is proposed to score candidate narratives.
- Ask or do: Specify human oversight, job relevance, testing, and applicable legal review before use.
- Why it matters: Automated scoring can reproduce bias or obscure why a score was assigned.
- Boundary: A model-generated explanation is not proof that the rating is valid.

**Transfer economics.**
- Situation: A public case claims lower inventory at a large national retailer.
- Ask or do: Compare the local cost of capital, margins, lead times, and service penalties.
- Why it matters: An operational improvement can have different value in another network.
- Boundary: Do not copy reported savings into the local business case.

**Transfer process.**
- Situation: The source company has centralized planning but the destination is decentralized.
- Ask or do: Map who decides, how overrides work, and where information arrives.
- Why it matters: A method may rely on authority or workflow absent in the destination.
- Boundary: Technology similarity alone does not establish process equivalence.

**Transfer data.**
- Situation: The source has reliable point-of-sale data but the destination has delayed orders.
- Ask or do: Test coverage, latency, definitions, and missingness before adopting the method.
- Why it matters: Different data-generating processes can change model performance.
- Boundary: A data dictionary alone does not establish data quality.

**Transfer constraints.**
- Situation: The source operates with unrestricted supplier flexibility but the destination has fixed order windows.
- Ask or do: Include the local ordering and contractual constraints in the exercise.
- Why it matters: A locally infeasible action cannot realize the source case's benefits.
- Boundary: Consult the responsible commercial and legal owners on actual contracts.

**Debrief.**
- Situation: The historical outcome is disclosed after learners commit to a choice.
- Ask or do: Ask which facts were available, what changed, and what would reverse the choice.
- Why it matters: Separating ex ante reasoning from ex post results reduces hindsight bias.
- Boundary: Do not rewrite the original decision to match the outcome.

**Feedback.**
- Situation: A learner is told only that their answer was unconvincing.
- Ask or do: Point to one observed strength and one specific improvement tied to a criterion.
- Why it matters: Actionable feedback supports a repeatable change in behavior.
- Boundary: Avoid diagnosing motivation or personality from one exercise.

**Documentation.**
- Situation: A facilitator changes the prompt between groups without recording it.
- Ask or do: Version packets, answer keys, timing, and material changes.
- Why it matters: Comparisons require knowing whether participants faced the same task.
- Boundary: Document approved accommodations without penalizing the participant.

**Post-pilot decision.**
- Situation: The pilot improves service but the cost estimate is incomplete.
- Ask or do: Hold the scale decision until costs and service are reconciled against criteria.
- Why it matters: A favorable single metric does not establish net operational value.
- Boundary: A delay is not evidence that the intervention failed.

**Reversal trigger.**
- Situation: A recommendation is described as robust without a threshold.
- Ask or do: State the probability, cost, service, or capacity level that would change it.
- Why it matters: A testable reversal condition exposes the decision's dependence on assumptions.
- Boundary: The trigger is conditional on the remaining assumptions staying valid.

**Portfolio learning.**
- Situation: A class studies only celebrated successful deployments.
- Ask or do: Add failed, inconclusive, and non-adopted examples where evidence exists.
- Why it matters: Success-only samples can create survivorship and publication bias.
- Boundary: An unsuccessful outcome alone does not prove that the initial choice was irrational.

## 6. Worked example

### Case and decision

**Synthetic teaching case:** a regional distributor must choose whether to keep manual replenishment or run a bounded decision-support pilot for 40 fast-moving stock-keeping units (SKUs).
The horizon is six weeks.
The distribution director owns the choice and can authorize up to USD 12,000 of pilot expense.
The analyst can recommend but cannot commit funds.
An existing warehouse labor agreement limits overtime, so the pilot must use normal staffing.
The service guardrail is at least 94% of order lines fulfilled on time in each weekly review.
The example assumes comparable order-line definitions and reliable timestamp data.
No real distributor, customer, or measured result is implied.

The manual option is the status quo.
The pilot recommends reorder quantities; a planner reviews each recommendation before execution.
The pilot's USD 10,000 implementation and review cost is included in its net contribution below.
No supplier order is automatically released.
The pilot can be stopped weekly, with the planner reverting to the manual process.

| State over six weeks | Assumed probability | Manual net contribution | Pilot net contribution | Pilot minus manual |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Stable demand | 0.60 | USD 92,000 | USD 108,000 | USD 16,000 |
| Volatile demand | 0.40 | USD 82,000 | USD 74,000 | −USD 8,000 |

These are assumptions for a classroom decision, not a forecast or empirical effect estimate.
The contribution figures include the pilot cost where applicable; they are incremental operational contribution for the defined scope and horizon, not company profit.
The probabilities sum to 1.00.

Manual expected contribution = 0.60 × USD 92,000 + 0.40 × USD 82,000 = USD 88,000.
Pilot expected contribution = 0.60 × USD 108,000 + 0.40 × USD 74,000 = USD 94,400.
Expected pilot advantage = USD 94,400 − USD 88,000 = USD 6,400 over six weeks.
The same result follows from 0.60 × USD 16,000 + 0.40 × (−USD 8,000) = USD 6,400.
In the volatile state the pilot is USD 8,000 worse than manual; its positive average does not remove that exposure.

Let p be the probability of stable demand.
The pilot's relative value is p × USD 16,000 + (1 − p) × (−USD 8,000).
At break-even, USD 24,000 × p − USD 8,000 = 0, so p = 1/3.
If the stable-state probability is below one third, the manual option has higher expected contribution under the stated matrix.
This threshold does not account for other scenarios, uncertainty in the state values, or service violations.

### Service and guardrails

For the exercise, assume the pilot's projected weekly service is 96% in the stable state and 92% in the volatile state; manual is projected at 95% in both states.
These projections are not measured results.
If the 94% guardrail is a strict pre-launch requirement that must be met in every plausible state, the pilot as specified is infeasible despite its positive expected contribution.
If instead the director authorizes a monitored test with a weekly stop when observed service falls below 94%, a conditional pilot may be acceptable, subject to approval and a credible recovery plan.
The two interpretations are different policies, not contradictory arithmetic.
A stop rule does not guarantee that no harm occurs before detection.

A defensible conditional recommendation is to seek explicit authorization for the monitored pilot, retain planner approval, review service weekly, and stop if the threshold is breached.
Also precommit to review workload, data quality, and lost-sales estimates.
If the director treats the guardrail as an ex ante hard constraint, recommend manual operation until the pilot is redesigned.
Either answer can earn a high score when the learner identifies the policy interpretation and justifies it.

### Pilot measurement

Record the number of eligible order lines and on-time fulfilled lines each week.
Compute service as on-time lines divided by eligible lines; report both numerator and denominator.
Compare pilot SKUs with a pre-specified, suitably matched set of manual SKUs if feasible.
Document changes in promotions, supplier lead times, and stock availability.
A matched comparison can reduce some differences but does not by itself establish causality.
If SKUs are randomized and implementation supports it, a randomized comparison can improve causal interpretation; contamination, small samples, and noncompliance still matter.

The USD 10,000 is a planned cost, not an authorized commitment.
The 96% and 92% numbers are scenario projections, not observed service.
The 94% threshold is a target and, if explicitly adopted, a stop rule; it is not an achieved result.
The plan is to seek authorization for a bounded pilot, not an assertion that one has been approved.

### Discussion reveal

At minute 25, reveal a synthetic update: the supplier announces a promotion affecting the pilot SKUs, making the stable-state probability less credible.
No revised probability is supplied.
The facilitator asks whether the learner would continue, pause, or change scope and which evidence would determine the choice.
A strong answer notes that the expected USD 6,400 advantage is now less reliable, asks for relevant promotion and lead-time information, and considers pausing or narrowing exposure.
The update alone does not prove that the volatile state will occur.

## 7. Code, query, or calculation

The following Python 3 program uses only the standard library.
It calculates scenario-weighted contributions, the pilot's downside by state, and the break-even stable-state probability.
The program treats the input probabilities and outcome values as assumed and does not determine whether the service guardrail allows a pilot.
A negative contribution difference indicates that the pilot is worse than manual in that state.
Run the block as a script; USD values are per six-week decision horizon.

```python
from decimal import Decimal

states = [
    {
        "name": "stable",
        "probability": Decimal("0.60"),
        "manual": Decimal("92000"),
        "pilot": Decimal("108000"),
    },
    {
        "name": "volatile",
        "probability": Decimal("0.40"),
        "manual": Decimal("82000"),
        "pilot": Decimal("74000"),
    },
]

assert sum(row["probability"] for row in states) == Decimal("1")
manual_ev = sum(row["probability"] * row["manual"] for row in states)
pilot_ev = sum(row["probability"] * row["pilot"] for row in states)
stable_delta = states[0]["pilot"] - states[0]["manual"]
volatile_delta = states[1]["pilot"] - states[1]["manual"]
break_even = -volatile_delta / (stable_delta - volatile_delta)

print(f"Manual expected contribution: USD {manual_ev:,.0f}")
print(f"Pilot expected contribution: USD {pilot_ev:,.0f}")
print(f"Expected pilot advantage: USD {pilot_ev - manual_ev:,.0f}")
print(f"Volatile-state pilot advantage: USD {volatile_delta:,.0f}")
print(f"Break-even stable probability: {break_even:.4f}")
```

Expected output:

```text
Manual expected contribution: USD 88,000
Pilot expected contribution: USD 94,400
Expected pilot advantage: USD 6,400
Volatile-state pilot advantage: USD -8,000
Break-even stable probability: 0.3333
```

For another exercise, replace both outcome pairs and probabilities together.
Keep the same currency, time horizon, cost inclusion, and population for all rows.
If the two relative state outcomes are equal, the break-even calculation has a zero denominator and requires a different interpretation.
The script is an arithmetic aid, not validation of the inputs, the probabilities, or the decision owner's risk tolerance.

## 8. Data, standards, and regulatory context

Teaching and hiring exercises may use operational data, assessment records, and individual responses.
The relevant rules depend on jurisdiction, employer, and the data used; this unit is educational and does not give legal advice.

For a teaching packet, use invented values where a real extract is unnecessary.
If real data is required, document authorization, purpose, access, retention, and deletion.
Removing names alone may not prevent re-identification when dates, locations, and rare events remain.
Instructors should avoid including candidate or employee information in a shared case packet without a legitimate basis and appropriate safeguards.

The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) explains that employment selection procedures must comply with federal equal employment opportunity law, including where selection procedures have an unjustified disparate impact.
Job relevance, consistent administration, validation, and reasonable accommodation should be reviewed for the applicable context; a rubric alone is not proof of compliance.
The EEOC and U.S. Department of Justice also provide guidance on disability discrimination and algorithmic assessment tools.
An organization considering automated scoring should assess accessibility, validity, and the applicable legal requirements with qualified advisers.

The Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures describe validity and adverse-impact concepts in U.S. employment selection.
They are not a universal scoring recipe and do not authorize collecting sensitive attributes without an appropriate lawful process.
In an educational setting, institutional assessment and privacy rules may differ from employment rules.

The European Union General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) addresses personal-data processing within its territorial and material scope.
Article 5 sets out principles including data minimization and storage limitation; Article 22 addresses certain decisions based solely on automated processing with legal or similarly significant effects, subject to its conditions and exceptions.
Do not infer from a case being scored by software alone that Article 22 necessarily applies; obtain context-specific legal review.

For all settings, define the data owner, source period, transformations, denominators, permitted purpose, access controls, and correction mechanism.
Give assessors the minimum necessary information.
Retain raw notes and scores only according to the organization's applicable policy and legal obligations.
Never infer an applicant's protected characteristic from their answer or use a supplied accommodation as a negative signal.

## 9. Industry practice and case evidence

### Evidence-based interview design

The U.S. Office of Personnel Management's structured interview guidance describes standardized questions, job-related competencies, anchored rating scales, and trained interviewers.
That is an official practice source, not proof that any particular classroom rubric predicts performance in every role.
The appropriate inference is that structured, job-related observation provides a more auditable process than improvising a different interview for each person.
The local employer still needs to assess fit, fairness, and validity for its own job and jurisdiction.

Campion, Palmer, and Campion's review of interview structure describes multiple components of structure rather than treating an interview as simply structured or unstructured.
Their published review is evidence about the research literature, not evidence that a specific candidate is good or bad.
A teaching application is to specify common questions, consistent probes, and anchored ratings in advance.
A practical limit is that over-standardized administration must still accommodate disability and allow a candidate to clarify relevant facts.

### Evidence-based case discussion

The Case Centre describes case teaching as discussion of situations requiring analysis and decisions.
This source supports use of decision-centered discussion as a teaching method; it does not establish that any particular case's outcome can be transferred to a different supply chain.
A facilitator can use a decision-forcing question before disclosing the historical outcome.
A class should then evaluate what was knowable at the decision time rather than rewarding retrospective certainty.

### Public case versus a teaching scenario

The M5 forecasting competition provides a public dataset and a documented forecasting setting.
It is evidence of the competition's defined task, data, and evaluation, not proof that its winning models would improve a particular retailer's profit.
For an interview or class, an instructor may construct an inventory decision using a subset, but must identify any service costs, supplier rules, or business outcomes they add as assumptions rather than dataset facts.
A forecast accuracy leaderboard and a replenishment decision exercise answer different questions.

For a vendor announcement, quote the vendor's actual claim and date, then ask whether the outcome has an independent comparison, what the denominator is, and which costs are omitted.
If no reliable counterfactual is available, describe the reported before-and-after result as an attributed claim, not a causal effect.
If the source is a secondary press report, check whether it rests on the same company statement before calling it independent corroboration.

## 10. Failure modes and red flags

| Failure | Early warning | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hindsight | Learners cite a later outcome as a known fact | Timeline not separated | Lucky or unlucky outcomes dominate scoring | Seal the reveal and score decision-time reasoning |
| Hidden constraints | Facilitator announces a rule after answers | Packet omits authority or service limit | Arbitrary scoring | Publish constraints and version the packet |
| Fake precision | Probabilities have unexplained decimals | Guesses presented as measurements | Overconfidence | Label assumptions and show thresholds |
| Missing baseline | Recommendation is compared with doing nothing | Current operation is undescribed | Benefits overstated | Specify the feasible status quo |
| Unequal options | One choice has richer evidence | Prompt favors an option | Biased discussion | Supply symmetric material or disclose gaps |
| Claim laundering | Multiple articles repeat one announcement | Repetition mistaken for independent sources | False corroboration | Trace citations to original evidence |
| Causal leap | Before-and-after is called an effect | Confounding ignored | Misleading transfer | Describe comparison and limitations |
| Consensus pressure | First speaker dictates the answer | No individual reflection | Suppressed alternatives | Write first, then round-robin |
| Facilitation leading | Instructor praises one choice early | Authority shapes participation | Conformity rather than reasoning | Ask neutral evidence questions |
| Style bias | Fluent response scores highly despite weak substance | Impression replaces anchors | Unfair evaluation | Record behavioral evidence per criterion |
| Personal-credit inflation | Candidate says 'I' for a team result | Role not probed | Misleading attribution | Ask what the candidate personally did |
| Proprietary disclosure | Candidate names confidential client metrics | Pressure for specificity | Privacy or contractual harm | Invite anonymized or hypothetical examples |
| Inaccessible task | Timed spreadsheet is essential to an unrelated skill | Format excludes some participants | Invalid comparison | Offer reasonable accommodations and equivalent formats |
| Data leakage | Answer key is in learner packet | Materials not separated | Measurement compromised | Control packet versions and access |
| Bad gate design | Stop threshold lacks cadence or owner | Metric is not operationalized | Harm continues unnoticed | Specify numerator, denominator, owner, and action |
| Rigid single answer | Alternative rejected without reasoning | Rubric scores choice rather than logic | Valid judgment penalized | Anchor criteria to evidence and constraints |
| Uncontrolled rollout | Pilot has no comparison or scope limit | Test confused with deployment | Weak learning and wider exposure | Bound scope and predefine the decision gate |
| Outcome inflation | Target is described as achieved benefit | Plan and realization conflated | Misstated results | Label forecast, plan, target, commitment, and actual |

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why needed | What breaks it | How to test or respond |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contribution values cover the same scope | Makes options comparable | Different SKUs or periods | Reconcile scope and included costs |
| State probabilities total 1 | Enables expected value | Overlapping or missing states | Check exhaustiveness and sum |
| State outcomes are representative | Makes scenario calculation meaningful | Structural demand change | Stress-test alternative values |
| Pilot costs are included once | Avoids misstatement | Setup omitted or double-counted | Trace each cost to a row |
| Weekly service is measurable | Enables stop rule | Delayed or inconsistent data | Verify timestamps and denominator |
| Planner review is feasible | Limits automated action | Workload exceeds capacity | Trial on representative volumes |
| Rollback is operationally possible | Bounds exposure | Irreversible supplier commitments | Map lead times and cancellation rights |
| Authority is correctly assigned | Makes recommendation actionable | Budget or policy approval absent | Verify decision rights |
| Learner has relevant case facts | Supports fair comparison | Hidden data or unexplained jargon | Pilot the prompt and clarify terms |
| Rubric covers job-relevant behaviors | Supports meaningful scoring | Criteria measure fluency alone | Map criteria to job analysis |
| Assessors can distinguish observations from impressions | Reduces halo effects | No calibration or contemporaneous notes | Independently score sample answers |
| Accommodation preserves the competency | Supports equitable opportunity | Format changes the skill being measured | Review equivalent accessible formats |
| Comparison units are comparable | Supports pilot learning | Promotions or supplier shocks differ | Prespecify matching and report differences |
| The case can transfer | Supports local application | Different economics or constraints | Run the transfer test and local validation |

A scenario calculation is not a statistical confidence interval.
Expected contribution is not a cash-flow forecast and does not price all tail risks.
An exercise score is evidence about behavior in one artificial setting, not a complete measure of job performance.
No case, however well designed, eliminates the need for local operational, legal, and human review.

## 12. Exercises

Each exercise uses the synthetic distributor case unless stated otherwise.
Show units, assumptions, and the distinction between an observed fact and a scenario input.

### Exercise 1 — decision prompt

**Prompt:** Who must choose what and when? (12 minutes.)

**Action:** Deliver a four-sentence prompt naming owner, horizon, options, and decision deliverable.

**Reason:** Acceptance: the prompt excludes the later supplier reveal and includes the authorization boundary.

### Exercise 2 — evidence ledger

**Prompt:** Which case statements are facts, assumptions, or forecasts? (15 minutes.)

**Action:** Deliver a six-row ledger with statement, category, source or synthetic status, and decision implication.

**Reason:** Acceptance: identify probabilities and service values as synthetic assumptions or projections, not measured outcomes.

### Exercise 3 — expected value

**Prompt:** Which option has greater expected contribution? (15 minutes.)

**Action:** Deliver both expected values, the expected difference, adverse-state difference, and break-even probability.

**Reason:** Acceptance: arithmetic is correct, USD and six-week horizon are named, and guardrail feasibility is addressed separately.

### Exercise 4 — pilot and stop

**Prompt:** What test could a director authorize? (20 minutes.)

**Action:** Deliver a one-page pilot charter with scope, owner, comparator, metrics, stop rule, and rollback.

**Reason:** Acceptance: service numerator and denominator, weekly review, and authorization are explicit; no claim that a stop prevents all harm.

### Exercise 5 — transfer critique

**Prompt:** A retailer cites a different company's successful replenishment tool. (15 minutes.)

**Action:** Deliver five questions covering decision, process, data, constraints, and economics plus a source ledger.

**Reason:** Acceptance: distinguish vendor assertion from observed effect and name one local test before scaling.

### Exercise 6 — new information

**Prompt:** The supplier promotion reveal arrives at minute 25. (12 minutes.)

**Action:** Deliver a revised recommendation and the information that would change it again.

**Reason:** Acceptance: do not assign an unsupported probability or assume that volatility is certain; explain exposure and next action.

### Exercise 7 — facilitation

**Prompt:** Design a 45-minute discussion for six learners. (20 minutes.)

**Action:** Deliver a timed run sheet with individual work, participation method, reveal, debrief, and accommodation path.

**Reason:** Acceptance: times sum to 45 minutes and the facilitator avoids revealing a preferred answer before individual reasoning.

### Exercise 8 — scoring

**Prompt:** Two learners choose different options. (15 minutes.)

**Action:** Deliver criterion-level ratings using the section 13 rubric and cite observable evidence for each rating.

**Reason:** Acceptance: do not award points solely for the option chosen; identify where evidence is insufficient.

### Exercise 9 — interview answer

**Prompt:** Describe a decision you supported, or use a clearly labeled hypothetical. (15 minutes.)

**Action:** Deliver a two-minute structured answer with context, decision, personal action, result or expected result, and reflection.

**Reason:** Acceptance: separate team from personal contribution, include one limitation, and disclose if the case is hypothetical.

### Exercises 4–9: assessment rubric

Use the following five criteria, each scored from 0 to 2.
Total possible score is 10; do not treat the total as a validated hiring cutoff.
A 0 means the criterion is absent or materially incorrect; a 1 means partly demonstrated; a 2 means demonstrated clearly and correctly.
Record the observation supporting each score before discussing an overall judgment.

| Criterion | 0 points | 1 point | 2 points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decision and constraints | No feasible decision identified | Decision stated but owner, timing, or constraint missing | Decision, owner, horizon, and material constraints explicit |
| Evidence and arithmetic | Materially wrong or unsupported | Some correct evidence but important uncertainty omitted | Correct reasoning, units, provenance, and uncertainty |
| Alternatives and trade-offs | No credible alternative or downside | Alternative named but comparison incomplete | Feasible comparison and downside or sensitivity addressed |
| Execution and learning | No implementation or review | Some operational steps but no enforceable gate | Owner, measurement, gate, and next action are actionable |

## 14. Interview questions

Use the same core questions and scoring criteria for comparable candidates, with consistent job-relevant probes.
Allow an anonymized or hypothetical case where real experience cannot be disclosed.
The examples below are answer structures, not scripts to memorize or claims of personal experience.

### Question 1 — decision framing

**Prompt:** How would you turn a company story into a decision exercise?

**Action:** A strong answer names owner, deadline, options, constraints, available evidence, and a deliverable.

**Reason:** A weak answer retells the story without requiring a choice.

### Question 2 — source quality

**Prompt:** A vendor reports a 20% improvement. What do you ask?

**Action:** A strong answer asks for denominator, baseline, population, period, costs, independent verification, and comparison.

**Reason:** A weak answer treats the claim as a causal effect because the number is precise.

### Question 3 — uncertainty

**Prompt:** How would you choose between a positive expected value and an adverse tail?

**Action:** A strong answer calculates the average, shows downside, checks guardrails, and explains risk tolerance.

**Reason:** A weak answer assumes a positive average guarantees a favorable outcome.

### Question 4 — learning from a pilot

**Prompt:** How would you design a replenishment pilot?

**Action:** A strong answer bounds the population and time, defines a comparison, service measure, stop rule, and owner.

**Reason:** A weak answer proposes full rollout and labels it a pilot.

### Question 5 — transfer

**Prompt:** When can a published case guide your own operation?

**Action:** A strong answer compares decision, process, data, economics, and constraints and proposes local validation.

**Reason:** A weak answer assumes the same technology name makes settings equivalent.

### Question 6 — facilitation

**Prompt:** One participant dominates the group. What do you do?

**Action:** A strong answer introduces independent writing and a neutral round-robin while protecting participation.

**Reason:** A weak answer lets the loudest participant set the conclusion or publicly shames them.

### Question 7 — evaluation

**Prompt:** Two candidates select opposite options. How do you score them?

**Action:** A strong answer rates observable reasoning against predefined anchors, not agreement with the assessor.

**Reason:** A weak answer gives full credit only to the assessor's preferred choice.

### Question 8 — attribution

**Prompt:** Tell me about a result your team achieved.

**Action:** A strong answer identifies the candidate's own action, team contribution, evidence, result, and limitation.

**Reason:** A weak answer claims the entire team outcome without explaining personal involvement.

### Question 9 — new evidence

**Prompt:** What would make you change a recommendation?

**Action:** A strong answer identifies a decision threshold, credible new observation, and revised action.

**Reason:** A weak answer treats changing position as inherently poor judgment.

### Question 10 — privacy and accessibility

**Prompt:** How would you use operational data in an interview?

**Action:** A strong answer minimizes data, secures authorization, offers an accessible format, and avoids requesting confidential details.

**Reason:** A weak answer asks candidates to disclose a former employer's sensitive data.

### Question 11 — causality

**Prompt:** Sales improved after a model launch. What can you conclude?

**Action:** A strong answer distinguishes the observed association from causal impact and proposes a credible comparison.

**Reason:** A weak answer assigns the full improvement to the model without considering confounding.

### Question 12 — failure

**Prompt:** Describe a recommendation that did not work as expected.

**Action:** A strong answer gives the decision-time rationale, observed outcome, personal role, and specific lesson without blaming others.

**Reason:** A weak answer invents certainty, hides the outcome, or cannot explain a change in practice.

### Structured interview answer: worked illustration

The following answer is hypothetical and uses the synthetic case; it is not a claim of work experience.

**Context:** A distributor had to choose a six-week replenishment approach for 40 SKUs under a USD 12,000 spending limit.

**Decision:** The director could continue manual planning or authorize a planner-reviewed pilot, subject to a service policy.

**My proposed action:** I would calculate expected contribution and downside, clarify whether the 94% service requirement is a hard pre-launch constraint or a monitored stop, and recommend a bounded pilot only if the director authorizes the latter policy.

**Evidence:** Under the synthetic assumptions, the pilot has a USD 6,400 expected advantage but is USD 8,000 worse in the volatile state; its projected service in that state is 92%.

**Result:** No real result is available because this is a hypothetical.
The intended learning measure would compare defined service and contribution measures against the manual process over the agreed period.

**Reflection:** The ranking depends on assumed state probabilities and the service rule; I would pause or revise scope if credible new information undermined those assumptions.

For a real experience, replace hypothetical language only with actions actually performed and outcomes actually documented.
If the result is unknown, say so and explain what was measured or handed over.
A confident delivery cannot substitute for evidence or justify disclosing confidential data.

## 15. Key terms

- **Alternative:** a feasible option compared with the recommendation; see [mechanics](#5-the-mechanics-step-by-step).
- **Assessor:** person who scores observed behavior against criteria; see [rubric](#13-self-check-answers).
- **Baseline:** reference operation used for comparison; see [vocabulary](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary).
- **Calibration:** alignment of assessors on observable anchors; see [rubric](#13-self-check-answers).
- **Case:** bounded account of a decision and its context; see [mental model](#3-the-core-mental-model).
- **Claim:** assertion requiring attribution and evidence; see [industry practice](#9-industry-practice-and-case-evidence).
- **Commitment:** authorized obligation rather than a proposed plan; see [worked example](#6-worked-example).
- **Constraint:** limit a decision must respect; see [mechanics](#5-the-mechanics-step-by-step).
- **Contribution:** financial measure within a defined scope and horizon; see [worked example](#6-worked-example).
- **Counterfactual:** outcome under an alternative not observed for the same unit; see [vocabulary](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary).
- **Decision owner:** role with authority to choose; see [mechanics](#5-the-mechanics-step-by-step).
- **Decision point:** time at which a choice is made; see [mental model](#3-the-core-mental-model).
- **Evidence:** information bearing on a claim, with provenance; see [industry practice](#9-industry-practice-and-case-evidence).
- **Expected value:** scenario-probability-weighted average; see [calculation](#7-code-query-or-calculation).
- **Facilitator:** person who manages an exercise and discussion; see [mechanics](#5-the-mechanics-step-by-step).
- **Forecast:** estimate of an uncertain future; see [worked example](#6-worked-example).
- **Guardrail:** limit triggering rejection, monitoring, or a stop; see [worked example](#6-worked-example).
- **Inference:** interpretation drawn from evidence and assumptions; see [mental model](#3-the-core-mental-model).
- **Pilot:** bounded test with an explicit learning decision; see [worked example](#6-worked-example).
- **Plan:** intended course of action, not necessarily authorized; see [worked example](#6-worked-example).
- **Probability:** assumed or estimated weight on a scenario; see [calculation](#7-code-query-or-calculation).
- **Provenance:** origin and history of a piece of evidence; see [mechanics](#5-the-mechanics-step-by-step).
- **Reveal:** later information disclosed at a specified exercise time; see [worked example](#6-worked-example).

The synthetic example and rubric are instructional constructions, not published results or validated selection instruments.

### Instructor preparation and use

Before the session, select the competency the exercise is intended to reveal.
Make a copy of the learner packet and retain the reveal separately.
Check the assumptions, the option matrix, and the meaning of every unit.
Give assessors the rubric and a sample answer before the exercise begins.

At the start, explain the deliverable and the time available.
Explain that a recommendation may be conditional on an explicitly stated assumption.
Do not disclose the facilitator's preferred choice.
Offer the agreed accessible format and avoid asking participants to explain a disability to the group.

During individual work, answer clarifying questions without introducing new facts for only one participant.
If a clarification is material, make it available to all comparable participants.
During discussion, ask for the reasoning behind a choice before asking for consensus.
Record the decision and its stated assumptions before the reveal.

After the reveal, ask which earlier assumptions are challenged and whether the decision changes.
During debrief, identify the difference between a result, a causal claim, and a decision made under uncertainty.
Use the rubric to identify an observable strength and an actionable improvement.
Do not report a class example as a validated selection tool.

After the session, retain materials only under the applicable institutional policy.
Document corrections to the exercise before it is reused.
If a change alters the difficulty or the information available, do not assume scores across versions are directly comparable.
