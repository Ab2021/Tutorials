## 1. Why this topic exists

A forecasting product can make a technically excellent prediction and still fail to create business value.
A planner may not trust it, the buyer may not change an order, or a warehouse may lack room to act on the recommendation.
Even if the organization takes the action, finance may find no reduction in cash spending.
The model's score is an input to a decision, not a financial outcome.

The first product-economics question is therefore not “What is the model's accuracy?”
It is “Whose decision changes, who bears the cost, and what outcome would justify that change?”
The answer differs for an executive sponsor, an operator, an implementation team, and an accountable finance partner.
Confusing these perspectives creates business cases that look precise but cannot be approved or verified.

A sponsor can authorize a pilot without being authorized to change an inventory policy.
A planner may use a recommendation without owning the purchasing budget.
An executive may demand a service improvement while the finance owner requires evidence of cash release.
A product leader needs all four perspectives before promising a result.

Consider an inventory recommendation that saves two hours of manual analysis each week.
If a salaried planner uses that time for exception management, the organization may gain capacity.
It does not automatically save two hours of payroll.
If the organization avoids an independently approved temporary hire, there may be an avoidable cost, but finance must identify the hiring decision and evidence.

Similarly, lower inventory can release cash once without producing recurring revenue each year.
A lower probability of disruption can have real decision value without being a bankable savings claim.
A service-level increase is a physical or customer outcome until a defensible economic bridge translates some non-overlapping portion to money.
These categories are related, but they are not interchangeable.

This topic provides a decision-centered way to map stakeholders, negotiate approval rights, classify outcomes, and produce a finance-owned value ledger.
It uses a hypothetical inventory-planning product to show arithmetic and disputed claims.
The numbers are illustrative, not evidence that any organization will achieve the stated results.

### Decisions this topic helps unblock

- Whether the intended operating decision is worth changing.
- Who can sponsor discovery and who can commit budget at each gate.
- Which users, downstream teams, and affected customers must be represented.
- Whether an asserted outcome is a benefit, an intermediate measure, a cost, or a risk.
- Which benefits can be added together and which describe the same underlying event.
- What evidence a finance owner needs before booking or reporting a benefit.
- Whether to proceed, revise, defer, or stop when stakeholders value different outcomes.

### What this topic does not decide

It does not prescribe an accounting treatment for a particular organization.
It does not prove that a model caused an observed improvement.
It does not replace local delegation-of-authority rules, contract review, or worker consultation.
Later topics develop counterfactual design, investment appraisal, and benefits realization.

3. **Classify** cost, cash, capacity, service, revenue, and risk outcomes by unit, timing, and evidence status. Exercises 3 and 4 check the taxonomy.
4. **Trace** an outcome from a changed action to a financial claim and identify missing causal links. Exercise 4 checks the value bridge.
5. **Calculate** a non-overlapping illustrative value case while separating one-time working-capital release from recurring effects. Exercise 5 checks the arithmetic.
6. **Critique** an unsupported or duplicated claim and specify a finance-owned acceptance test. Exercise 6 checks the critique.
7. **Design** a proportionate approval and measurement plan that protects users and affected parties. Exercises 7 and 8 check the design.

An acceptable result names actual roles or role-holders, not just departments.
It also states who can reject a decision, what evidence is needed, and when the decision expires.
For a real assignment, replace illustrative role titles and numbers with approved local records.

## 3. The core mental model

The product is a proposed intervention in a decision system.
Each benefit requires a chain from an authorized decision to a changed action, an observable outcome, and, where appropriate, a finance-approved economic interpretation.

```text
Sponsor defines decision and constraints        Finance defines recognition rules
                 |                                              |
                 v                                              v
User + decision right -> recommendation -> action -> operational outcome
       ^                                     |                 |
       |                              affected parties          v
       +------- feedback and override <--- controls       value taxonomy
                                                                |
                                                  evidence + counterfactual
                                                                |
                                                accepted claim / rejected claim
                                                                |
                                                funding and operating decision
```

The arrows express dependencies, not guarantees.
A model can improve a forecast without changing a purchase order.
An order can change without reducing average inventory if another policy offsets the change.
Inventory can fall without releasing the modeled cash if the units are obsolete, consigned, or immediately replaced.

### Four questions for every claim

1. **Who experiences it?** Identify the person or entity whose work, service, cost, or risk changes.
2. **What changes physically?** Name the decision, action, quantity, and period.
3. **What would otherwise happen?** Define a plausible comparison, not just the previous year.
4. **Who can recognize it?** Name the finance owner and the rule for recording, reporting, or excluding the claim.

A fifth question follows when someone asks to add claims: **Are these distinct events?**
For example, inventory cash release and annual financing-cost reduction are conceptually different effects of a lower inventory balance.
However, adding both to a discounted cash-flow valuation can double count financing if the discount rate already captures the cost of capital; finance must select a consistent valuation approach.
The one-time release is not an annual income statement saving.

### Stakeholder perspectives are not competing truths

A planner may correctly report that an alert reduces investigation time.
A controller may correctly report that the payroll budget is unchanged.
A customer-service leader may correctly value the extra time used to prevent a shortage.
The error is not any one statement; it is relabeling all three as separate cash benefits without evidence.

### Separate the decision and the measurement

A decision right answers who may authorize an action.
A measurement owner answers who produces or checks evidence about its consequences.
One person can have both roles, but neither role implies the other.
A finance partner can validate a calculation without having authority to approve a product release.
A product owner can prioritize a feature without being able to approve a budget transfer.

### The unit of analysis

Choose a unit that makes the causal chain testable: for example, purchase order, stock-keeping unit (SKU)-location-week, or customer-order line.
Fix the population, period, currency, and data cut before measuring.
A benefit's boundary must be narrow enough to identify overlap and broad enough to include displaced cost.
If a change saves a distribution center money but shifts more cost to suppliers, report both views where data and decision scope allow.

## 4. Definitions and vocabulary

| Term | Definition and unit | Common misuse |
|---|---|---|
| Sponsor | Leader who champions a defined business outcome and removes barriers; a named role | Assuming sponsorship confers all financial and operational approvals |
| Economic buyer | Person authorized to commit the relevant funds, subject to local limits | Assuming the most senior meeting participant is always the buyer |
| Budget owner | Person accountable for a specific approved budget, currency, and period | Treating an unapproved forecast as available budget |
| Product owner | Person accountable for prioritizing product outcomes and backlog within delegated scope | Giving the role authority over every affected business process |
| User | Person who interacts with an output or workflow | Assuming a manager who receives a report is the only user |
| Decision owner | Person authorized to choose among specified alternatives | Confusing an analytics recommendation with a decision |
| Process owner | Person accountable for the operating process and its controls | Excluding the downstream process that must execute the action |
| Affected party | Person or entity bearing consequences, including non-users | Restricting stakeholder mapping to people with software accounts |
| Approver | Role whose explicit authorization is required at a particular gate | Treating an informed or consulted stakeholder as an approver |
| Veto or control right | Authority to block a specified action under a defined policy | Claiming a general veto without the governing delegation |
| Consultation | Structured opportunity to supply evidence and concerns before a decision | Treating a consultation as consent or a binding approval |
| Finance owner | Named finance role accountable for the agreed financial interpretation and acceptance process | Assuming the analyst who calculated a spreadsheet has finance authority |
| Baseline | Defined starting measurement over a specified population and period | Treating the baseline as the causal counterfactual automatically |
| Counterfactual | Outcome that would have occurred without the intervention over the same scope and period | Assuming last year's raw result is always the no-product outcome |
| Driver | Measurable intermediate factor connecting an action to an outcome | Counting the driver and the resulting financial outcome as independent benefits |
| Benefit | Favorable change relative to an agreed counterfactual, stated in an appropriate unit | Calling a model output itself a realized benefit |
| Recurring operating saving | Sustained reduction in relevant expenditure against the counterfactual, currency per period | Multiplying staff hours by salary when payroll and hiring are unchanged |
| Cost avoidance | Expenditure that would otherwise have been incurred but is no longer needed, with an evidenced decision | Treating an unapproved aspiration as an avoided purchase |
| Capacity release | Time or throughput made available for another use, hours or units per period | Claiming cash savings without an action that changes expenditure |
| Working capital | Operational current assets less relevant operational current liabilities, defined consistently in currency | Equating an inventory balance reduction with recurring profit |
| Cash release | One-time cash-flow effect from a reduction in investment in working capital, currency at a date | Repeating the same released balance as cash inflow every year |
| Revenue uplift | Incremental revenue versus a counterfactual, currency per period | Treating revenue as profit or ignoring cannibalization |
| Contribution margin | Revenue less the specified variable costs attributable to the sale, currency per unit or period | Using gross revenue where incremental contribution is required |
| Service outcome | Customer or operational performance such as order lines fulfilled on time, percentage and period | Monetizing every service gain with an unsupported revenue multiplier |
| Risk exposure | Distribution of possible loss events and consequences, including timing | Counting an uncalibrated risk score as realized cash |
| Expected loss | Probability multiplied by loss for a specified event and horizon, currency per horizon | Treating an expectation as a guaranteed annual saving |
| Cost | Resource consumption or cash outflow associated with delivery and operation, currency and period | Counting only the software subscription |
| Double count | Including the same economic consequence more than once in an aggregate | Assuming different metric names imply different value |
| Attribution | Evidence-based estimate of the intervention's contribution relative to a counterfactual | Assigning every concurrent change to the product |
| Realization | Evidence that an intended benefit has occurred under agreed recognition rules | Calling an approved forecast a realized outcome |
| Benefit owner | Named business role accountable for actions needed to achieve a benefit | Naming a project manager who cannot change the process |
| Data steward | Role responsible for quality, definition, and appropriate use of specified data | Assuming data access grants authority for any purpose |
| Approval gate | Time-bound decision with inputs, decision maker, criteria, and record | Treating a meeting invitation as authorization |
| Forecast | Best estimate conditional on assumptions, currency or operational unit and period | Presenting a forecast as an authorized target |
| Target | Desired outcome used for management, unit and period | Assuming a target is a causal estimate |
| Plan | Intended actions and resources, dated and owned | Confusing planned use with measured adoption |
| Commitment | Authorized obligation within delegated authority, amount and period | Calling a proposal a binding commitment |

## 5. The mechanics, step by step

### Step 1 — Frame the decision before listing stakeholders

**Input:** a request such as “build an inventory AI dashboard.”

Rewrite it as an operating decision: “Should the replenishment planner change the next purchase-order quantity for selected SKU-locations?”
Specify the decision cadence, alternatives, decision deadline, and constraints.
For example, a buyer may accept, modify, or reject a weekly recommendation before the supplier's ordering cutoff.
Record which decisions remain human, which are automated, and what constitutes an override.

**Output:** a one-sentence decision statement and a bounded decision inventory.

**Branch:** If there is no decision or feasible action, do not invent a financial benefit.
Reframe the request as discovery, regulatory reporting, or another explicitly scoped objective.
If multiple decisions occur, map each separately before aggregating.

### Step 2 — Define boundaries and affected flows

**Input:** the decision statement, product scope, organizational chart, and process map.

Identify legal entity, sites, product categories, customer groups, suppliers, time horizon, and currency.
Map physical goods, orders, information, and money across the boundary.
Name downstream actors who must execute or absorb the decision.
A lower order quantity may affect warehouse workload, supplier minimum order quantities, and customer availability.

Record exclusions such as consigned inventory, discontinued products, or emergency orders, and explain why each is excluded.
Do not exclude inconvenient outcomes solely to improve the business case.
If a cost moves outside the sponsor's budget, show both the sponsor view and a whole-enterprise view.

**Output:** a scope statement with population, dates, and material interfaces.

**Branch:** If impacts cross legal entities or contractual boundaries, involve the relevant owners before relying on internal savings estimates.

### Step 3 — Identify the people, not only the boxes

**Input:** process map, workflow observation, interviews, and records of prior approvals.

List the sponsor, economic buyer, budget owner, product owner, decision owner, process owner, users, finance owner, data steward, technology owner, and affected parties.
A single person may hold several roles; explicitly note that fact.
Capture named primary and alternate contacts where appropriate, their interest, evidence they require, and their decision constraints.
Include frontline users on different shifts and at sites with materially different workflows.
Include customer, supplier, labor, privacy, security, legal, and accessibility representation where relevant.

Ask users what they do today, what they can change, what would make an output useful, and what happens when the recommendation is wrong.
Ask sponsors which outcome is worth funding, which outcome is non-negotiable, and what would cause the project to stop.
Ask finance whether an effect can be realized as expenditure, cash, revenue, or a nonfinancial outcome, and what evidence would support that classification.

**Output:** a stakeholder register with role, interest, authority, impact, contact, and evidence need.

**Branch:** If a claimed benefit belongs to a team not represented, add that team's accountable owner or label the claim unowned and unapproved.
A stakeholder's absence from a workshop is not evidence of agreement.

### Step 4 — Separate influence from formal authority

**Input:** stakeholder register, delegation-of-authority policy, procurement policy, and process-control requirements.

For each decision, identify one accountable decision owner and any genuinely required additional approvals.
List consulted and informed parties separately.
Typical decisions include discovery funding, data access, pilot release, purchase-order execution, expansion, budget transfer, and benefit recognition.
Formal approval limits can vary by amount, contract term, entity, and risk.
Obtain the current policy rather than assuming a generic hierarchy.

For each right, record the scope, threshold, prerequisites, evidence, approver, date, and escalation path.
A decision record must state whether approval is conditional and who verifies the conditions.
If the person holding a required right is absent or conflicted, use the authorized alternate or escalate.
Silence after a meeting is not an approval.

**Output:** a decision-rights matrix and a dated decision log.

**Branch:** If a user can override the recommendation but not alter policy, design the workflow accordingly.
If an automated action would exceed the authorized delegation, keep human approval until the delegation and controls change.

### Step 5 — Elicit outcomes in each stakeholder's own units

**Input:** interviews, operating objectives, financial plans, and service agreements.

Ask each stakeholder for the current problem, a measurable desired change, the population affected, and an undesirable side effect.
Translate statements such as “more resilient” into observable indicators, scenarios, and thresholds before monetizing them.
Keep a raw statement alongside its normalized metric so that meaning is not lost.

For each candidate outcome, record numerator, denominator, unit, period, owner, data source, and direction of improvement.
A fill-rate metric needs a clear numerator, denominator, treatment of partial orders, and time window.
A time saving needs observed tasks and a plausible use of released hours.

**Output:** an outcome register with stakeholder-specific units and constraints.

**Branch:** If stakeholders request incompatible targets, record the trade-off and refer it to the decision owner.
Do not conceal a service deterioration inside an aggregate inventory saving.

### Step 6 — Construct a value chain

**Input:** outcome register, process map, and proposed intervention.

For each claim, write a chain of the form:

`recommendation -> authorized action -> operational change -> economic consequence -> evidence`.

Identify prerequisites at each arrow: user adoption, feasible order changes, supplier compliance, customer demand, and measurement availability.
Record the mechanism by which a physical result could affect a budget, cash balance, or contribution margin.
Also record possible adverse outcomes such as expediting, spoilage, workload transfer, or poorer service.

**Output:** an impact map with positive and negative paths.

**Branch:** If an arrow lacks evidence, keep the claim as a hypothesis, not a realized benefit.
If a recommendation is not acted on, measure adoption and investigate the blocker before claiming impact.

### Step 7 — Classify each line once

**Input:** impact map and finance's accounting and management-reporting definitions.

Classify the primary effect as recurring operating expenditure, avoided expenditure, one-time cash release, capacity, revenue or margin, service, risk, or cost.
Assign a unique claim identifier and physical event identifier.
Mark whether a line is monetary, nonmonetary, or an intermediate driver.
Set timing, frequency, currency, and evidence state.

Different views can display the same event, but the aggregation key must prevent adding them as separate independent effects.
For instance, a reduction in planner hours and the resulting avoided temporary hire share an event chain.
Show the hours as the driver and the avoided hire as the monetary claim; do not add an hourly wage valuation to the avoided hire.

**Output:** a classified ledger with one primary claim per economic event and explicit links to its drivers.

**Branch:** If finance cannot verify that expenditure changes, classify the effect as capacity or an unapproved estimate.
If an event is already represented through another claim, exclude one line from the total and retain the cross-reference.

### Step 8 — Agree the baseline and counterfactual

**Input:** historical data, seasonality, business plans, and measurement design options.

A baseline describes the starting point; the counterfactual estimates the outcome without the intervention over the evaluation period.
Where feasible, use randomized assignment or a credible comparison group and predefine exclusions.
Otherwise, explain what historical comparison can and cannot establish, and adjust only with documented rationale.
Separate market demand, pricing, promotions, policy changes, and site closures from the product's effects where the design permits.

Define a measurement window long enough for the decision's operating lag.
Inventory ordered now may arrive weeks later, and the economic effect may not be observable at the first dashboard refresh.
Avoid choosing a favorable end date after inspecting results.

**Output:** an agreed measurement protocol and versioned counterfactual.

**Branch:** If there is no credible comparison, report a descriptive before/after change and uncertainty rather than claiming causality.
See the sibling counterfactual topic for design details.

### Step 9 — Specify realization actions

**Input:** classified ledger, decision rights, and operating plan.

For each monetary claim, identify the owner who can take the action that makes it real.
For example, a capacity gain becomes an avoided expense only if the authorized owner cancels or does not incur a documented planned expense.
An inventory reduction affects cash only as purchasing and payment flows change, net of relevant offsets.
Revenue requires evidence of incremental orders and associated variable costs, not just an improved service score.

Set the verification evidence, acceptance threshold, data cut, reporting period, and treatment of reversals.
Identify the controlling finance role and the operational benefit owner separately.
Record whether the amount is proposed, forecast, accepted, realized, or rejected.
The status of a claim is not the same as approval to spend.

**Output:** a realization contract for each benefit, linked to the decision log.

**Branch:** If no owner can execute the required action, report the opportunity as uncommitted capacity or service, not as bankable savings.

### Step 10 — Reconcile scope, overlaps, and costs

**Input:** ledger, cost estimate, ownership records, and dependencies.

Reconcile claim populations and dates before adding numbers.
Use a shared event identifier to connect related measures.
Identify whether service improvement is already monetized through incremental contribution.
Identify whether an avoided hire is already included in a reduced operating budget.
Show internal transfers separately from enterprise-wide savings.

Include product costs in the same horizon and currency as the claim: implementation, integration, licenses, support, monitoring, training, and process change.
State which costs are incremental and which are sunk for the current decision.
Do not remove a real cost merely because another department pays it.
For a discounted valuation, agree how working-capital changes, tax, and financing are treated before adding line items.

**Output:** a reconciled gross-benefit, cost, and nonfinancial-outcome view.

**Branch:** If an overlap cannot be quantified, do not add both claims to the committed total.
Present an explicit range or separate alternatives with the uncertainty explained.

### Step 11 — Choose a proportionate decision gate

**Input:** decision-rights matrix, reconciled case, evidence status, and constraints.

For discovery, authorize a bounded learning budget and define an evidence milestone.
For a pilot, authorize a bounded population, operating controls, user training, and rollback conditions.
For scale, require evidence that the process can act on the output, the affected owners accept their obligations, and finance understands the value claim.
A high-risk, irreversible, or external commitment may require additional gates under local policy.

Write the decision as proceed, proceed with conditions, defer pending evidence, or stop.
Include the amount and period approved, conditions, responsible approvers, expiry, and next review.
Do not convert a pilot authorization into an implied enterprise deployment approval.

**Output:** signed or otherwise policy-compliant gate record.

**Branch:** If an approver rejects a critical condition, change the proposal or escalate under policy.
Do not relabel a rejection as an endorsement.

### Step 12 — Review results and revise ownership

**Input:** operational outcomes, finance evidence, user feedback, and the approved plan.

At a set cadence, compare observed results with the approved measurement protocol.
Record what changed in the product, process, population, and counterfactual assumptions.
Reconcile a reported benefit to source records and check for reversals or displacement.
Invite users and affected functions to identify harm, workarounds, or unequal workload.

A finance owner can accept a measured claim, reject it, or return it for more evidence.
The product owner can use the same evidence to adjust functionality, but cannot unilaterally book savings.
Close or replace ownership when people change jobs, rather than leaving a stale name in a spreadsheet.

**Output:** a dated benefits review and a new decision where warranted.

**Branch:** If a control threshold is breached, invoke the approved pause or rollback path.
If the result is not attributable, retain the observation but downgrade or withdraw the causal claim.

### Decision clinic A — Who approves the pilot?

**Situation:** A distribution leader asks for a 13-week trial costing USD 35,000.
The sponsor has a USD 25,000 delegated spending limit; procurement policy requires a contract review.
The supplier offers a one-year agreement even though the proposed trial lasts 13 weeks.

**Decision:** Approve a bounded trial only through the person holding the relevant spending authority and any mandatory contracting approvals.
The sponsor can recommend the work and provide operational access, but cannot extend their own limit.
The contracting owner must assess the actual one-year obligation rather than only the proposed pilot invoice.

**Stakeholders:** Sponsor, economic buyer, procurement, security, process owner, controller, and trial users.
The sponsor's operational approval and the economic buyer's financial approval answer different questions.
The controller reviews the expected economics but does not automatically sign the contract.

**Value classification:** The trial cost is an investment or operating cost under the organization's applicable finance treatment.
An estimated future saving is a forecast, not an offset to the legal commitment.
Training hours and implementation effort are costs even if another department funds them.

**Required record:** Scope, full contract term, termination rights, amount, conditions, decision maker, and review date.
If required authority is unavailable, defer the commitment or use a policy-authorized alternate.
Do not divide the order into smaller purchases to evade approval limits.

### Decision clinic B — Can a dashboard make orders?

**Situation:** The release owner proposes sending recommendations directly to the ordering system to reduce manual work.
The approved pilot described advisory recommendations and buyer review.
The buyer can authorize orders only below a specified delegated threshold.

**Decision:** Keep the advisory control unless the authorized process owner and other required control owners approve a change.
The release owner can deploy software only within the release authority actually granted.
The purchase-order authority must be mapped to the action the system performs, not assumed from the user's login.

**Stakeholders:** Buyer, process owner, technology release owner, security, supplier, finance, and service owner.
Users should test overrides and identify exceptions that cannot safely be automated.
Suppliers need notification where order formats, timing, or commitment behavior change.

**Value classification:** Reduced clicks are an activity measure.
Observed buyer time may become capacity if the same work is genuinely eliminated.
It becomes a monetary saving only when a supported expenditure change occurs.

**Required record:** Decision thresholds, human review points, audit trail, rollback, monitoring, and escalation.
An accidental automated order is an operational incident, not evidence of adoption.
The next investment gate should distinguish any automation benefit from the advisory pilot's outcome.

### Decision clinic C — A supplier carries the inventory

**Situation:** A retailer reduces its balance by USD 500,000, while its supplier holds an additional USD 380,000 of equivalent stock.
Assume for illustration that the values use comparable cost bases and that no other inventory changes occur.
The retailer's reported balance falls, but goods have not all disappeared from the network.

**Decision:** The retailer may report its own balance change, subject to finance's cash reconciliation.
For a joint supply-chain discussion, show both parties' changes rather than treating the retailer's entire reduction as a network reduction.
Supplier finance must validate its own costs and ownership; the retailer cannot approve the supplier's records.

**Stakeholders:** Retail sponsor, retailer controller, supplier commercial owner, supplier controller, and contract owners.
Service and availability owners should examine whether lead time or stockout risk changed.
Each party may value the outcome differently because financing, contracts, and risk differ.

**Value classification:** The simple network inventory balance change is USD 500,000 minus USD 380,000, or USD 120,000 lower.
That is not necessarily USD 120,000 of immediate cash release because payables, valuation, and payment timing may differ.
The retailer's USD 500,000 and the network's USD 120,000 are alternative boundary views, not values to add.

**Required record:** Dates, ownership, valuation basis, stock population, contractual terms, and unknown offsets.
If comparable supplier data are unavailable, report the retailer view and state that the network effect is unknown.
Avoid asserting a partnership benefit from a one-sided ledger.

### Decision clinic D — Capacity without a headcount cut

**Situation:** A planning team measures 12 hours/week of reduced manual investigation.
It retains all staff and uses the time to review supplier exceptions.
The product team suggests applying a USD 60/hour fully loaded rate to claim USD 37,440/year.

**Decision:** Report 624 hours/year of released capacity under a 52-week assumption.
Do not accept USD 37,440/year as payroll saving without a change in payroll or an evidenced avoided expense.
Ask the process owner to describe the redeployment and its operational objective.

**Stakeholders:** Planners, planning manager, human resources where appropriate, controller, and affected suppliers.
Use proportionate time-study methods and protect individual-level data.
Do not infer that every minute freed is available for a different productive activity.

**Value classification:** `12 hours/week × 52 weeks/year = 624 hours/year` is capacity.
`624 hours/year × USD 60/hour = USD 37,440/year` is a hypothetical cost-equivalent valuation, not verified cash.
If an independently planned temporary contract is avoided, evaluate that contract's incremental cost instead.

**Required record:** Task observations, covered weeks, use of capacity, and any authorized expense decision.
If the staffing plan is uncertain, retain the hours and mark the monetary conversion unapproved.
Do not add both a cost-equivalent valuation and the avoided contract for the same activity.

### Decision clinic E — Improve service without inventing sales

**Situation:** A pilot increases on-time order lines from 9,300 of 10,000 to 9,500 of 10,000 in comparable periods.
The commercial lead proposes valuing all 200 additional on-time lines at USD 100 revenue per line.
Many orders might otherwise have shipped late rather than been lost.

**Decision:** Record the service improvement first and investigate the incremental commercial consequence separately.
The customer-service owner verifies the numerator, denominator, and promised-date definition.
The controller reviews any subsequent contribution claim.

**Stakeholders:** Customer service, sales, operations, finance, and customers affected by late delivery.
The sales owner should distinguish customer retention from timing changes and internal substitution.
The warehouse owner should assess whether extra fulfillment cost accompanied the improvement.

**Value classification:** On-time performance rises from 93% to 95%, or 2 percentage points.
The 200 additional on-time lines are a service driver, not proof of 200 additional sales.
Even if incremental sales are established, contribution rather than gross revenue is generally the relevant operating benefit.

**Required record:** Comparable order cohorts, cancellations, substitution, incremental price and variable cost, and offsetting service costs.
If causal evidence is unavailable, retain the nonfinancial result and do not assign USD 20,000 of incremental revenue.
A service guardrail can still matter to a decision without being monetized.

### Decision clinic F — Avoided loss versus actual loss

**Situation:** A risk owner estimates a disruption probability of 10% per year with a USD 400,000 loss if it occurs.
A proposed control is estimated to reduce probability to 6% per year without changing the loss severity.
The control costs USD 8,000/year, and all numbers are hypothetical.

**Decision:** Show the expected-value arithmetic and uncertainty, not a guaranteed annual cash saving.
The risk owner validates the scenario; finance reviews the economic representation.
The authorized decision maker weighs expected value along with tail exposure and risk appetite.

**Stakeholders:** Risk owner, operations, finance, control operator, affected customers, and investment approver.
The operator must be able to maintain the control throughout the year.
The customer owner should identify consequences not captured in the stated loss amount.

**Value classification:** Expected loss before control is `0.10 × USD 400,000 = USD 40,000/year`.
Expected loss after control is `0.06 × USD 400,000 = USD 24,000/year`.
The estimated gross risk reduction is USD 16,000/year; net of control cost it is USD 8,000/year in expected value.

**Required record:** Event definition, probabilities, severity, horizon, evidence, dependency, and sensitivity.
No disruption in a particular year does not prove the control saved USD 16,000 in that year.
If the control is also credited elsewhere through observed avoided losses, reconcile the claims before aggregation.

### Decision clinic G — Different sites, different effects

**Situation:** The pilot reduces average inventory by USD 250,000 at site A but raises it by USD 90,000 at site B.
Site B serves customers with a stricter minimum service requirement.
The project team wants to report only the positive site.

**Decision:** Report both site results and the aggregate within the approved population.
Investigate whether the site B increase reflects necessary protection, data error, or an unintended policy change.
The process owner and service owner decide whether the pattern is acceptable under their delegated rights.

**Stakeholders:** Both site managers, customer service, inventory-policy owner, finance, and planners.
Site B's users need a chance to explain constraints that may not appear in aggregated data.
The finance owner checks whether the amounts share a cost basis and measurement window.

**Value classification:** The arithmetic aggregate is `USD 250,000 - USD 90,000 = USD 160,000` lower inventory.
That balance difference is not automatically attributable or equivalent to cash.
The site-level service measures remain separate guardrails, not additional dollar claims.

**Required record:** Site populations, comparable counterfactuals, service outcomes, ownership, and timing.
If site B is excluded for a valid predeclared reason, display the exclusion and report the consequence.
Do not select sites retrospectively merely to maximize the reported benefit.

### Decision clinic H — A planned hire is postponed

**Situation:** A manager had budget approval for a six-month temporary planning contract costing USD 24,000.
After an advisory tool reduces workload, the manager cancels the contract before commitment.
The same time saving was also valued in the product team's spreadsheet at USD 30,000.

**Decision:** Finance investigates whether the contract would actually have been procured without the intervention.
Evidence includes the approved plan, timing, decision to cancel, and relationship to the workload change.
The budget owner confirms whether the approved budget is reduced or redeployed.

**Stakeholders:** Planning manager, controller, procurement, human resources where applicable, and product owner.
The contract decision belongs to the person with staffing and spending authority.
The product owner supplies evidence but cannot alone approve the avoided expense.

**Value classification:** The supported candidate avoided expense is up to USD 24,000 for the six-month period.
It is not automatically a recurring USD 24,000/year saving.
The USD 30,000 time valuation describes the same chain and must not be added to the candidate contract avoidance.

**Required record:** Approved counterfactual, cancellation decision, contract scope, period, and any replacement spend.
If the contract would have been canceled for another reason, attribution must be adjusted or the claim rejected.
If its budget is spent on a replacement service, report that offset in the relevant boundary.

### Decision clinic I — Reconcile invoice savings and allocated cost

**Situation:** A site reports that freight cost fell by USD 100,000/year after fewer urgent orders.
Of that amount, USD 65,000 is lower carrier invoices and USD 35,000 is a reduction in an internal allocation.
The central logistics team reports that total contracted fixed charges did not change.

**Decision:** Reconcile invoices and costs at the enterprise boundary before accepting the full claim.
The site can report its budget view while finance separately reports the enterprise effect.
The central logistics owner verifies contract terms and any other displaced costs.

**Stakeholders:** Site controller, central logistics controller, carrier manager, and process owner.
The sponsor should see both the local incentive and the enterprise consequence.
Internal allocations can inform accountability without creating cash savings by themselves.

**Value classification:** The USD 65,000 invoice reduction is a candidate enterprise operating saving if attributable and not offset.
The USD 35,000 allocation change is a transfer unless underlying external expenditure also falls.
Adding the full USD 100,000 to another team's unchanged cost would overstate enterprise value.

**Required record:** Carrier invoices, rates, volume, allocation rules, comparison period, and contract minimums.
Check for increased freight at another site or later in the year.
Finance accepts only the portion supported by the agreed scope and evidence.

### Decision clinic J — Decide with a negative annual effect

**Situation:** A proposal forecasts USD 180,000 of one-time working-capital release and USD 40,000/year of net recurring cost.
The service owner expects a material reliability improvement but lacks a credible monetary estimate.
The sponsor asks for immediate scale because the release exceeds one year of cost.

**Decision:** Require a dated investment appraisal and an explicit decision about the nonfinancial objective.
The scale approver may proceed, defer, or stop according to authority and risk appetite.
No single year's comparison of the release to operating cost establishes long-term value.

**Stakeholders:** Sponsor, controller, service owner, economic buyer, users, and technology owner.
Finance must review timing, ramp, residual working capital, cost horizon, and valuation convention.
The service owner must define an observable guardrail and the consequences of missing it.

**Value classification:** USD 180,000 is a forecast one-time balance-related cash effect under stated assumptions.
USD 40,000/year is a forecast recurring net cost, not a one-time fee.
The unmonetized reliability objective stays visible alongside, rather than hidden inside, the financial total.

**Required record:** Alternatives, cash-flow timing, sensitivity to benefit persistence, service evidence, and conditions.
If the data are weak, a limited pilot may purchase information without promising the full release.
The approved commitment is the authorized expenditure, not the forecast result.

### Decision clinic K — A model metric improves but no one acts

**Situation:** Forecast error falls by 12% relative to a benchmark, but buyers accept only 5% of recommendations.
Interviewed buyers say that many recommendations arrive after supplier cutoff.
The product team proposes estimating financial value from the forecast-error change alone.

**Decision:** Treat the accuracy improvement as a technical result and investigate decision timing before claiming operational value.
The buyer and process owner identify feasible delivery times and required exceptions.
The product owner may prioritize a timing fix within the approved scope.

**Stakeholders:** Buyers, process owner, model owner, integration team, supplier, and finance.
The supplier can confirm actual cutoff and lead-time constraints.
Finance needs evidence that the revised workflow changes expenditure, cash, or a defined nonfinancial outcome.

**Value classification:** Forecast accuracy is a model metric; 5% acceptance is an adoption indicator.
Neither number by itself is a cash saving or service improvement.
The 12% relative improvement is not necessarily 12 percentage points and has no automatic monetary conversion.

**Required record:** Comparable benchmark, timestamped delivery, eligibility, acceptance definition, overrides, and resulting orders.
Sample rejected cases for reasons rather than assuming users are resistant.
After a process change, measure again against the agreed counterfactual.

### Decision clinic L — Product benefit is shared across programs

**Situation:** A replenishment product and a supplier-negotiation program launch in the same quarter.
Freight invoices fall by USD 120,000/year, and both teams claim the entire reduction.
The contract rates and the number of expedited orders both changed.

**Decision:** Finance and the two benefit owners establish a common attribution approach before adding claims.
They may separate a rate effect and a volume effect if data and assumptions support that decomposition.
If interaction cannot be allocated credibly, report a shared combined effect rather than two full claims.

**Stakeholders:** Both program owners, carrier manager, controller, sponsor, and measurement analyst.
An independent measurement owner can mediate the calculation without deciding investment priorities.
Neither team should change the comparison window after seeing which one maximizes its result.

**Value classification:** The observed USD 120,000/year invoice difference is a combined candidate outcome.
It is not USD 240,000/year merely because two initiatives contributed.
Allocation between teams is a management decision; it must not change the enterprise total.

**Required record:** Baseline and new rates, volumes, mix, timing, concurrent changes, and reconciliation.
An interaction term or residual should remain visible when rate and volume effects depend on decomposition order.
If causality is unresolved, downgrade the claim and present uncertainty to both sponsors.

### A compact decision-record template

Use the following fields for any of the clinics or for a real gate.
The record is a practical control, not a substitute for the organization's formal approval process.

1. Decision title, date, scope, and expiration.
2. Alternatives considered, including no change where feasible.
3. Authorized decision maker and required additional signatories.
4. Sponsor, budget owner, finance owner, process owner, and user representation.
5. Population, sites, financial entity, period, and currency.
6. Forecast, target, plan, and actual commitment in separate fields.
7. Benefit categories, unique event identifiers, and exclusions.
8. Operational risks, service guardrails, and affected-party impacts.
9. Costs, cash timing, uncertainties, and counterfactual assumptions.
10. Evidence needed, owner, due date, and acceptance criteria.
11. Conditions, pause or rollback triggers, and escalation route.
12. Decision, rationale, sign-off record, and next review date.

For an unresolved field, write “unknown” and name who will resolve it.
Do not imply that an unknown is an approval or a zero cost.
Retain superseded versions so later reviewers can reconstruct what was known at the gate.

## 6. Worked example

### Situation and assumptions

A fictional distributor considers a replenishment recommendation product for a selected group of products.
The pilot covers 240 SKU-locations across two distribution centers for 26 weeks.
Amounts are in US dollars (USD); annual figures refer to a 52-week operating year.
The illustration assumes stable demand mix for calculation; a real evaluation must test this assumption.

The business decision is whether a buyer changes each weekly order quantity after reviewing a recommendation.
The output is advisory: the buyer retains the authorized purchase-order decision.
The project sponsor is the vice president of supply chain.
The economic buyer for the pilot is the operations budget owner, subject to the organization's actual spending limits.
The inventory policy owner approves the permitted policy range.
The controller is the finance owner for claim classification and verification.
The product owner controls backlog priorities within the approved scope, not purchasing policy.

### Decision-rights matrix

| Decision | Accountable decision owner | Required additional approval | Consulted or informed | Record |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fund a bounded pilot | Operations budget owner | Procurement or other authority if policy requires | Sponsor, controller, product owner | Approved amount, term, and conditions |
| Grant access to order data | Authorized data owner | Security and privacy approvals where applicable | Data steward, users | Access scope and expiry |
| Release advisory recommendations | Product release owner | Process owner and control functions as required | Planners and support | Release and rollback record |
| Change inventory-policy limits | Inventory policy owner | Other policy signatories as locally required | Buyers, customer service, finance | Authorized limits and date |
| Place a purchase order | Buyer within delegated authority | Higher authority when threshold requires | Supplier and planning team | Order and approval trail |
| Accept a financial claim | Controller under local reporting rules | Accounting or budget owner if required | Benefit owner and analyst | Evidence and accepted status |
| Scale beyond pilot | Authorized investment decision maker | Required technology, process, finance, and procurement approvals | Users and affected sites | New scope, funding, conditions |

The titles are illustrative; the organization must verify the actual policy and signatories.
Finance validation does not itself approve deployment.
The sponsor's support does not override a buyer's purchasing limit.

### The proposal at the pilot gate

The forecast is a possible 8% reduction in average eligible inventory relative to an agreed no-product counterfactual, with no deterioration beyond the service guardrail.
The target is an 8% reduction for management review, not a guaranteed result.
The plan is to run a 26-week advisory pilot with weekly reviews and a predeclared comparison design.
The commitment is only the approved pilot expenditure and conditions, if signed under local authority.
No forecast benefit is a commitment to produce cash.

Suppose finance and the process owner agree the following illustrative measurements at review:

| Measure | Counterfactual | Observed or accepted estimate | Difference | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Average eligible inventory at cost | USD 4,800,000 | USD 4,416,000 | USD 384,000 lower | Estimated change in invested inventory balance |
| Annual expedites | 420 orders/year | 350 orders/year | 70 fewer orders/year | Operational driver |
| Incremental cost per expedite | USD 640/order | USD 640/order | USD 640/order avoided | Only costs that change are included |
| Planner investigation time | 44 hours/week | 36 hours/week | 8 hours/week released | Capacity, not automatically payroll savings |
| Order-line fill rate | 95.2% | 95.5% | 0.3 percentage points higher | Service outcome, not separately monetized |
| Annual product operating cost | USD 0/year | USD 72,000/year | USD 72,000/year higher | Incremental cost after implementation |
| One-time implementation cost | USD 0 | USD 125,000 | USD 125,000 higher | Upfront cash outflow in this simplified view |

The inventory values and expedite figures are assumed estimates attributable to the intervention for teaching purposes.
In practice, the team must establish a credible comparison and quantify uncertainty before using that interpretation.
The inventory figure is the difference in average balances, not necessarily the cash already received on a particular day.
For an actual cash statement, reconcile purchase receipts and payment timing and any offsetting payables or other working-capital changes.

### Arithmetic and classifications

Inventory balance difference:

`USD 4,800,000 - USD 4,416,000 = USD 384,000`.

Percentage reduction:

`USD 384,000 / USD 4,800,000 = 0.08 = 8%`.

Under the simplifying assumption that the full reduction is sustained, financed by the company, and converted into a corresponding reduction in net working capital with no offset, the estimated one-time cash release is USD 384,000.
It is not USD 384,000 of annual profit and must not be repeated each year as a fresh release.
If the balance returns to its former level, the release reverses.

Expedite-cost reduction:

`(420 - 350) orders/year × USD 640/order = USD 44,800/year`.

This is an annual operating-cost estimate only if the 70 orders and USD 640 per order are incremental, comparable, and not already captured elsewhere.
It excludes fixed freight contracts that do not change with the number of expedites.

Capacity released:

`(44 - 36) hours/week × 52 weeks/year = 416 hours/year`.

The 52-week assumption is illustrative; replace it with the actual covered working weeks.
The planners remain employed and their pay does not change.
Finance therefore accepts 416 hours/year as capacity, not as payroll savings.
The process owner plans to redeploy the capacity to supplier exception reviews; that is a plan, not proof of an additional quantified benefit.

Net annual operating effect before tax and excluding financing:

`USD 44,800/year - USD 72,000/year = -USD 27,200/year`.

The one-time simplified cash difference at implementation and steady state is:

`USD 384,000 - USD 125,000 = USD 259,000`.

This is not a net present value or payback calculation.
It mixes a hypothetical sustained balance change with an upfront cost without specifying exact dates, taxes, discounting, or ramp-up.
It is included only to distinguish stock and flow; an investment decision needs a dated cash-flow model.
The annual operating shortfall matters even if the initial working-capital release is attractive.

### The contested labor claim

The sponsor proposes valuing 416 hours/year at USD 50/hour and adding USD 20,800/year to savings.
The controller asks whether payroll or an evidenced future expenditure will fall.
No position is removed and no approved hire is avoided in this case.
The controller rejects the USD 20,800/year cash claim while accepting the 416 hours/year capacity observation, subject to measurement.
The process owner can report how the capacity is used without presenting it as realized payroll savings.

If, instead, an authorized and documented temporary contract costing USD 18,000/year would otherwise have been purchased and is not purchased because of the released capacity, the finance owner may consider a USD 18,000/year avoided-expense claim.
The contract decision and counterfactual must be evidenced.
The 416 hours remain an explanatory driver and must not also be multiplied by a wage and added to the USD 18,000/year.

### The contested service claim

Customer service values the 0.3 percentage-point increase in fill rate.
Sales suggests multiplying all newly filled lines by selling price and adding that revenue to the case.
Finance rejects that shortcut because some orders may have been filled later, substituted, or purchased elsewhere within the same company.
A contribution claim would require a defined control, incremental units, incremental price, variable cost, and treatment of cannibalization.
Until that evidence exists, report fill rate as a nonfinancial service outcome and guardrail.

### A decision under competing priorities

The sponsor prefers immediate scale because the inventory estimate is attractive.
The controller points to the annual operating shortfall and the uncertainty around cash timing.
The process owner reports that the pilot's two sites have different supplier constraints.
The decision maker defers scale and authorizes a time-bounded investigation only if the relevant authority approves its cost.
The next gate requires a dated cash-flow case, a site-level comparison, a plan for ongoing cost, and user evidence that recommendations remain actionable.
This is a decision example, not a prescribed answer for every company.
A different service-risk objective or stronger evidence could legitimately change the choice.

## 7. Code, query, or calculation

The following Python 3 example uses only the standard library.
It calculates the worked case and separates an inventory balance change, an annual cost effect, and nonfinancial capacity.
It deliberately does not calculate net present value, causal attribution, tax, or payroll savings.
The input amounts are assumed attributable estimates, not raw observations proving causality.

```python
from decimal import Decimal

D = Decimal

# All amounts are USD; annual quantities refer to a 52-week year.
# The two inventory amounts are comparable eligible average balances.
counterfactual_inventory = D("4800000")
observed_inventory = D("4416000")
counterfactual_expedites = D("420")  # orders/year
observed_expedites = D("350")        # orders/year
incremental_cost_per_expedite = D("640")  # USD/order
hours_before = D("44")              # hours/week
hours_after = D("36")               # hours/week
working_weeks = D("52")             # weeks/year
annual_product_cost = D("72000")     # USD/year
implementation_cost = D("125000")   # USD once

inventory_change = counterfactual_inventory - observed_inventory
inventory_change_pct = inventory_change / counterfactual_inventory * D("100")
expedite_saving = (
    counterfactual_expedites - observed_expedites
) * incremental_cost_per_expedite
capacity_hours = (hours_before - hours_after) * working_weeks
annual_net_operating = expedite_saving - annual_product_cost

# A cash release of the full balance difference is an assumption,
# not an automatic consequence of average inventory falling.
assumed_net_working_capital_release = inventory_change
simplified_one_time_net = assumed_net_working_capital_release - implementation_cost

print(f"Inventory balance difference: USD {inventory_change:,.0f}")
print(f"Inventory reduction: {inventory_change_pct:.1f}%")
print(f"Avoided expedite expense: USD {expedite_saving:,.0f}/year")
print(f"Released capacity: {capacity_hours:,.0f} hours/year")
print(f"Net annual operating effect: USD {annual_net_operating:,.0f}/year")
print(f"Assumed one-time net: USD {simplified_one_time_net:,.0f}")
```

Expected output:

```text
Inventory balance difference: USD 384,000
Inventory reduction: 8.0%
Avoided expedite expense: USD 44,800/year
Released capacity: 416 hours/year
Net annual operating effect: USD -27,200/year
Assumed one-time net: USD 259,000
```

### How to use the artifact

1. Replace each input only after fixing its scope, unit, period, source, and counterfactual.
2. Confirm that the expedite unit cost is genuinely avoidable, not an allocated fixed cost.
3. Set working weeks to the actual coverage; leave is not automatically a productive week.
4. Reconcile the inventory change to net working-capital and payment flows before labeling it realized cash.
5. Keep the capacity output separate unless a documented decision creates a distinct avoidable expense.
6. Give finance the inputs, calculation version, evidence, and ownership record for review.
7. Do not combine the simplified one-time number and annual number as an investment appraisal.

The code can return a negative result if inventory rises or costs exceed savings.
That result should be investigated, not clipped to zero.
For a production application, define input validation, data lineage, review controls, and an independently reviewed valuation model appropriate to the decision.
Those operational requirements are outside this small teaching calculation.

## 8. Data, standards, and regulatory context

### Minimum evidence fields

A useful claim record includes a unique identifier, statement, decision, affected population, owner, source, calculation, unit, period, baseline, counterfactual, and evidence status.
Add the data extraction date, version, exclusions, known quality issues, and approval record.
A monetary claim also needs currency, timing, cost or benefit category, and the finance acceptance rule.
A nonfinancial claim needs a clear numerator, denominator, and interpretation.

Order data may include promised and actual dates, order lines, quantities, product and site identifiers, exceptions, and cancellations.
Inventory data may include ownership, valuation basis, on-hand quantity, in-transit stock, and end-of-period or average balances.
Financial data may include actual invoices, ledger accounts, budgets, and the classification of fixed and variable cost.
Time data should state the observed workflow, sampling method, and whether paid time or elapsed time is measured.

### Identifier and time discipline

Consistent product, location, and trading-partner identifiers enable joining physical events with financial records.
GS1's Global Trade Item Number and Global Location Number are examples of standardized identifiers; adoption and contractual requirements depend on the trading context.
They do not establish that an intervention caused a benefit or that a particular claim is financially valid.
Record identifier mappings, effective dates, unit-of-measure conversions, and master-data changes.
A mismatched case-versus-each conversion can create a fictitious service or inventory improvement.

State time zones, order cutoff times, financial period boundaries, and the difference between order date, receipt date, invoice date, and payment date.
Average inventory should use an agreed sampling method, not whichever month-end happens to be lowest.
A working-capital claim must account for relevant ownership and payable effects.
Retention and access rules should follow applicable policy and law.

### Financial reporting is not a universal product taxonomy

The IFRS Foundation's IAS 2 describes inventory accounting under International Financial Reporting Standards, including cost and measurement requirements.
Those requirements apply when relevant to the reporting entity and framework; a management calculation in this lesson is not a substitute for an accounting determination.
The same physical stock reduction may affect cash flow, inventory balances, cost of sales, or expense timing in different ways depending on the transaction.
A local controller must determine the appropriate treatment.

Management benefit categories such as “capacity” and “cost avoidance” are useful controls, not a prescribed set of financial-statement captions.
A finance-approved forecast is not the same as an audited accounting entry.
Organizations should reconcile the management ledger to applicable financial records rather than assume that every ledger line is bookable.

### Privacy, employment, and automated decisions

Planner activity logs and time studies can contain personal data.
Use the minimum data needed, specify purpose and retention, restrict access, and involve the appropriate privacy and employment stakeholders.
Where the EU General Data Protection Regulation applies, lawful basis, transparency, data minimization, and other obligations must be assessed for the actual processing.
Article 22 has specific conditions concerning certain solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects; it should not be assumed to apply to every recommendation.
Local counsel should determine applicability and any employment or consultation requirements.

A human override is a process control, not proof of compliance with every legal obligation.
Security, privacy, procurement, accessibility, and safety requirements arise from the actual deployment and jurisdiction.
This section describes educational considerations as of November 2026, not legal advice.

### Evidence and access controls

Separate rights to view source data, modify a calculation, approve a claim, and authorize a deployment.
Preserve calculation versions and original data extracts where policy permits.
Log exclusions, late corrections, and changes to definitions.
Provide finance sufficient reproducibility without unnecessarily exposing individual employee information.
A dashboard can display an approved estimate, but it cannot grant approval by itself.

## 9. Industry practice and case evidence

### Public evidence is not a transferable business case

Published company cases commonly describe a combination of demand sensing, forecasting, ordering, inventory, and operating change.
A company statement about an outcome is evidence that the company reported it; it is not independent proof that a particular algorithm alone caused the result.
Details of counterfactuals, costs, failed trials, and stakeholder approvals are often absent.
Do not apply a reported percentage to another organization's inventory without checking definitions, population, and conditions.

### Dell: virtual integration

The Harvard Business Review interview “The Power of Virtual Integration” describes Dell's approach to coordinating information and activities across organizational boundaries.
The documented lesson is that information sharing and process coordination can change how replenishment decisions are made across organizational boundaries.
It does not establish an ROI for the hypothetical product in this lesson.

**Inference:** A product manager considering a similar collaboration should map both parties' decision rights, data-sharing terms, and incentives.
A retailer's inventory reduction could shift inventory or volatility to a supplier rather than reduce total supply-chain cost.
Finance owners on both sides may use different accounting boundaries; neither view alone proves enterprise-wide or network-wide benefit.

### Zara: responsiveness and operational design

The Harvard Business Review article “Rapid-Fire Fulfillment” describes Zara's coordinated design, production, distribution, and retail processes for responsiveness.
It is evidence of an operating model, not a claim that a standalone forecast dashboard produced a stated return.

**Inference:** For a replenishment analytics proposal, responsiveness depends on who may change orders, the lead time available, and the physical network's ability to execute.
A more accurate recommendation has limited value if the order cutoff has passed or production is inflexible.
The appropriate stakeholder map includes store, merchandising, distribution, and supplier process owners, not just data scientists.

### Maersk and IBM: platform participation

IBM and Maersk announced the discontinuation of TradeLens in 2022, stating that the platform had not reached the level of commercial viability required to continue.
This public announcement is a concrete reminder that participation and commercial viability are separate from technical feasibility.
It does not prove a single cause for the outcome or establish that the platform lacked technical capability.

**Inference:** A cross-company supply-chain product needs a stakeholder and incentive map for data contributors, users, payers, and counterparties.
An approving sponsor at one company cannot compel other organizations to share data or alter workflows.
Test adoption assumptions and counterparty commitments before capitalizing their supposed network benefits.

### Interpreting the cases

All three examples concern different products and operating contexts.
They should not be combined into a numerical benchmark.
The practical questions are consistent: who can act, who bears the cost, who receives the benefit, and what evidence supports the claim?
For real investment cases, supplement public descriptions with organization-specific operational and financial evidence.

| Sponsor mistaken for approver | “The vice president likes it” substitutes for a funding record | Support is confused with delegated authority | Unauthorized commitment or delayed launch | Verify approval matrix and record the gate |

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why needed | What breaks it | How to test or respond |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decision rights are current | Approvals must be valid | Policy or delegated limits change | Check the governing policy at each gate |
| Stakeholders represent the workflow | Design reflects affected users | One site or shift is omitted | Sample sites, shifts, and exception paths |
| Users can act on recommendations | Output can influence orders | Supplier cutoff or policy prevents action | Observe decisions and measure feasible-action rate |
| Counterfactual is credible | Difference can be attributed | Concurrent promotion or policy change | Use a comparison design and sensitivity analysis |
| Inventory is company-financed | Lower balance can release company cash | Consignment or customer-owned stock | Reconcile ownership and payable terms |
| Inventory reduction persists | Cash is not immediately reinvested | Rebound or seasonal rebuild | Track balance and purchasing over the full cycle |
| Expedite unit cost is avoidable | Fewer orders reduce expenditure | Contract minimum or fixed charge | Reconcile invoices and contract terms |
| Hours are comparable | Time difference measures capacity | Work mix or sampling changes | Observe comparable tasks and periods |
| Service is consistently defined | Percentage-point comparison is meaningful | Order-line denominator changes | Version metric and reconcile source records |
| Product costs are complete | Net economics are meaningful | Support, training, or integration excluded | Obtain cost-owner review across the lifecycle |
| Effects do not overlap | Summation is valid | Multiple claims represent the same event | Use event identifiers and a reconciliation ledger |
| Working-capital and financing treatment is consistent | Valuation avoids double counting | Financing charge added inconsistently to discounted cash flows | Agree valuation convention with finance |
| No unmeasured external harm | Enterprise result is not misleading | Supplier cost or customer loss displaced | Identify affected boundaries and report unknowns |
| Point estimates are stable | Single arithmetic illustration is interpretable | Demand or supplier performance varies | Present ranges and scenario results |

The worked case uses assumed attributable differences for transparent arithmetic.
Actual benefit estimates are uncertain and may be negative.
The organization should choose evidence strength appropriate to the size, reversibility, and risk of the decision.
An approval record supports accountability, but does not by itself validate the causal estimate.

Assume the entire inventory difference becomes a one-time net working-capital release and all differences are attributable.

**Deliverable:** Calculate inventory reduction in dollars and percent, annual avoided expedite cost, net annual operating effect, and the simplified one-time net after implementation.

**Acceptance criteria:** Show units and arithmetic; keep the one-time and annual effects separate; state why this is not net present value.

### Exercise 6 — Find duplicate and unsupported claims

**Time:** 20 minutes.

A proposal adds USD 15,000/year for planner hours, USD 15,000/year for an avoided temporary contract caused by those same hours, and USD 25,000/year for the gross revenue of orders already included in a USD 8,000/year incremental-contribution estimate.
No contract decision has been documented.

**Deliverable:** A claim-by-claim critique and corrected status table.

**Acceptance criteria:** Identify both overlap chains, distinguish revenue from contribution, and state what finance needs before accepting the contract and contribution claims.

### Exercise 7 — Design a conditional pilot

**Time:** 30 minutes.

The buyer wants a six-month pilot; planners worry that the system will create orders automatically; finance cannot establish a credible comparison for every site.

**Deliverable:** A one-page gate record with scope, named role-level approvers, constraints, evidence, pause condition, cost authority, and next review.

**Acceptance criteria:** Keep purchasing authority explicit, require local approval verification, distinguish descriptive from causal reporting, and provide a route to stop or rollback.

### Exercise 8 — Resolve conflicting stakeholder objectives

**Time:** 30 minutes.

A sponsor prioritizes inventory cash, customer service rejects any decline in fill rate, and the controller wants operating expenditure reduced.
The product has a USD 200,000 expected one-time release, a USD 25,000/year operating shortfall, and uncertain customer impact.

**Deliverable:** A decision memo with at least two alternatives, a recommended conditional decision, owner, evidence gaps, and an explicit trade-off.

**Acceptance criteria:** Do not sum one-time and annual amounts as if they share timing; include a service guardrail and show who can approve each decision; distinguish forecast, target, plan, and commitment.

## 13. Self-check answers

### Exercise 1

A strong register includes sponsor, budget owner, buyer, inventory-policy owner, planner user, controller, technology release owner, data owner, supplier, warehouse, and customer-service representative.
Some roles may be held by the same person; the register should make that explicit.
The budget owner and policy owner cannot be inferred merely from the sponsor's title.
A supplier is an affected party even if the supplier never logs into the product.
A controller needs a calculation, evidence, counterfactual, and reconciliation; a planner needs timely recommendations and a safe override.

### Exercise 2

Discovery approval permits only the scope and expenditure actually authorized.
Automating orders changes the operating control and needs the relevant process and technology approvals.
A three-year contract requires the authorized contracting and procurement path, including applicable spending limits.
Data access and claim recognition are distinct decisions.
A complete answer names policy verification, approvers or authorized alternates, conditions, escalation, and a written decision record.

### Exercise 3

| Item | Category | Timing or unit | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| USD 90,000 inventory investment reduction | Potential working-capital cash release | One-time USD balance change | Ownership, payables, payment timing, persistence |
| 600 planner hours/year | Capacity | Hours/year | Comparable tasks and realized use of time |
| USD 12,000/year freight invoice reduction | Recurring operating saving, if incremental and attributable | USD/year | Invoices, counterfactual, no offsetting costs |
| 2 percentage-point service improvement | Service outcome | Percentage points over defined period | Numerator, denominator, mix, comparison |
| USD 30,000/year hypothetical avoided loss | Estimated risk reduction | Expected USD/year under assumptions | Event probabilities, loss distribution, horizon, overlap |

An expected risk reduction is not a realized cash saving merely because it is expressed in currency.

### Exercise 4

A possible chain is: recommendation -> buyer changes an authorized order -> supplier executes -> inventory or expedite activity changes -> incremental cash or expense changes -> finance accepts evidence.
Check recommendation delivery and acceptance, order history, supplier confirmation, physical inventory and service, invoice and ledger evidence, and the comparison without the product.
A plausible adverse effect is a stockout or a supplier expedite cost shifted outside the sponsor's budget.
Accuracy alone does not establish any downstream link.

### Exercise 5

`USD 2,500,000 - USD 2,300,000 = USD 200,000` inventory reduction.
`USD 200,000 / USD 2,500,000 = 8%`.
`(190 - 150) orders/year × USD 300/order = USD 12,000/year` avoided expedite cost.
`USD 12,000/year - USD 20,000/year = -USD 8,000/year` net annual operating effect.
`USD 200,000 - USD 40,000 = USD 160,000` simplified one-time net under the stated assumptions.
It is not net present value because it omits exact dates, discounting, taxes, and subsequent changes in working capital.

### Exercise 6

The hour valuation and avoided contract are two views of the same capacity-to-expense chain.
Do not add both; without evidence of the contract decision, retain hours as capacity and mark the avoided contract unverified.
Finance needs the authorized baseline contract plan, the non-purchase decision, relevant period and amount, and attribution evidence.
The USD 25,000 gross revenue and USD 8,000 contribution describe the same orders; do not add them.
Before accepting contribution, check incremental orders against the counterfactual, variable costs, cannibalization, and overlap with other claims.

### Exercise 7 rubric

A strong gate record identifies a bounded six-month scope and authorized budget, keeps the buyer's order authority, and blocks automatic ordering unless separately approved.
It specifies data and release approvals according to local policy, a comparison plan, and a reporting distinction between measured changes and attributable effects.
It identifies a pause or rollback trigger, accountable owners, review date, and conditions for scale.
A weak response assumes sponsor approval covers deployment and treats before/after results as proof.

### Exercise 8 rubric

A strong memo compares at least a controlled pilot and defer or stop, with an explicit service guardrail.
It states that USD 200,000 is a forecast one-time release and USD 25,000/year is an expected annual shortfall, not directly additive without dated valuation.
It names the decision maker, finance reviewer, policy owner, and user representation.
It marks the target, planned actions, forecast, and any actual approved commitment separately.
A defensible recommendation depends on evidence and decision authority; no single numeric outcome is prescribed.

## 14. Interview questions

1. **Who is the sponsor, and is that person necessarily the economic buyer?**  
   Strong: distinguishes championing from delegated spending authority and checks policy.  
   Weak: assumes the most senior person can authorize everything.
2. **How would you map a planner who uses a tool but cannot change orders?**  
   Strong: identifies the planner as user and finds the authorized buyer and handoff.  
   Weak: treats tool use as purchasing authority.
3. **What is the difference between an approver and a consulted stakeholder?**  
   Strong: defines an explicit gate, required authorization, and record.  
   Weak: treats attendance or silence as approval.
4. **Why is an hour saved not necessarily a dollar saved?**  
   Strong: explains capacity and requires an actual expenditure or hiring decision.  
   Weak: multiplies hours by fully loaded wages without checking cash impact.
5. **How would you explain inventory cash release to an executive?**  
   Strong: describes a one-time change in investment, payment timing, offsets, and possible reversal.  
   Weak: reports the full reduction as recurring annual profit.
6. **How would you prevent double counting a service improvement?**  
   Strong: links the service driver to any monetized incremental contribution and reconciles event populations.  
   Weak: adds gross sales, margin, and service score as independent benefits.
7. **What evidence connects a forecast to financial value?**  
   Strong: traces recommendation, action, operational outcome, counterfactual, and financial evidence.  
   Weak: relies only on model accuracy.
8. **What does finance ownership mean?**  
   Strong: names a finance role, acceptance rules, reconciliation, and limits of authority.  
   Weak: assumes the product analyst can approve and book a saving.
9. **What if a sponsor's budget improves but a supplier's cost rises?**  
   Strong: separates entity and network boundaries and reports shifted costs and uncertainty.  
   Weak: assumes local savings equal total supply-chain savings.
10. **When would you defer a scale decision?**  
    Strong: points to unmet controls, uncertain counterfactual, unowned benefits, or unresolved service risk and specifies the next gate.  
    Weak: treats a favorable dashboard as sufficient for enterprise rollout.

## 15. Key terms

- [Sponsor](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — champion of a defined business outcome.
- [Economic buyer](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — holder of relevant spending authority.
- [Budget owner](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — accountable owner of a specified budget.
- [Product owner](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — owner of product priorities within delegated scope.
- [User](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — person interacting with a workflow or output.
- [Decision owner](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — person authorized to select an action.
- [Process owner](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — accountable owner of an operating process.
- [Affected party](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — person or entity experiencing consequences.
- [Approver](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — role whose explicit gate authorization is required.
- [Consultation](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — opportunity to provide input, not automatic consent.
- [Finance owner](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — accountable finance role for claim interpretation and acceptance.
- [Baseline](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — defined starting measurement.
- [Counterfactual](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — estimated outcome without the intervention.
- [Driver](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — measurable intermediate mechanism.
- [Benefit](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — favorable change relative to a comparison.
- [Operating saving](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — sustained reduction in relevant expenditure.
- [Cost avoidance](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — evidenced expenditure not incurred.
- [Capacity release](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — resources made available for another use.
- [Working capital](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — consistently defined operational current assets less liabilities.
- [Cash release](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — dated reduction in working-capital investment.
- [Contribution margin](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — revenue less specified incremental variable costs.
- [Service outcome](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — customer or operational performance measure.
- [Risk exposure](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — possible loss outcomes and their uncertainty.
- [Double count](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — inclusion of the same consequence more than once.
- [Attribution](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — estimate of the intervention's causal contribution.
- [Realization](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — evidence that an intended benefit occurred.
- [Benefit owner](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — owner of actions needed to achieve an outcome.
- [Approval gate](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — recorded decision with criteria and authority.
- [Forecast](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — conditional estimate of an outcome.
- [Target](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — desired management outcome.
- [Plan](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — intended actions and resources.
- [Commitment](#4-definitions-and-vocabulary) — authorized obligation within delegated authority.
