## 1. Why this topic exists

A hospital's clinical engineering department is responsible for every powered device that touches a patient: infusion pumps, ventilators, patient monitors, anaesthesia machines, dialysis units, laboratory analysers, and imaging systems. The department has a fixed number of technicians, a fixed parts budget, and a maintenance schedule that was written when the fleet was smaller. Every week it decides which devices to service, which parts to stock, and which devices to replace. That decision is the subject of this topic.

The industry problem is not that hospitals lack data. Modern devices emit error codes, usage counters, battery cycles, motor current, sensor offsets, and self-test results. The problem is that the data are used to answer the wrong question. A dashboard reports "device health scores" that rank devices by how often they alarmed. A vendor reports a "prediction" that is a threshold on a single sensor. A service manager asks for a list of devices likely to fail this month and receives a list of devices that were already reported as faulty. None of these is a maintenance decision.

Four failures recur.

**The service call predicted instead of the failure.** A service call is generated by a clinician, a nurse, or a technician in response to a symptom. The symptom may be caused by the device, by the consumable, by the patient, or by the user. A model trained on service calls learns the behaviour of the people who call, not the degradation of the machine. The calls cluster at shift changes, after training days, and on the wards with the least experienced staff. The model is accurate and useless.

**The failure rate computed on the wrong denominator.** A department reports 22 failures in a fleet of 240 pumps over two years and calls the rate 4.6 per 100 device-years. But 90 of those pumps left the fleet during the period, several after only four months. The time they were actually at risk is much smaller than 240 times two years. The true rate is 6.1 per 100 device-years, 34% higher. Every downstream calculation, including the business case, is wrong by the same factor.

**The remaining useful life that is an extrapolation.** A model fits a degradation curve to 18 months of data and reports a remaining useful life of 60 months. No device in the dataset has been observed for more than 24 months. The number is produced by the shape of the fitted curve, not by evidence. A planner who uses it defers replacement for five years and is surprised when the fleet fails at year three.

**The business case that counts only the avoided repair.** A predictive programme is justified by avoided failures and avoided repair cost. The costs it creates are omitted: technician time for false alerts, devices pulled from wards during a shift, loaner inventory, and the erosion of trust when the alerts are wrong. In a small fleet the fixed platform cost alone can exceed the entire scheduled maintenance budget.

The stakes are asymmetric. A missed failure on a ventilator, an infusion pump delivering a vasoactive drug, or a dialysis machine is a patient safety event. A false alarm on 240 infusion pumps is a workflow event that trains staff to ignore alarms. A model that treats both errors as symmetric will be tuned to the wrong operating point.

There is also a regulatory dimension that data scientists often miss. Maintenance is not only an engineering choice. In the United States, accredited hospitals must either follow manufacturer recommendations or run an alternative equipment maintenance (AEM) programme with written justification. In the United Kingdom, the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) publishes guidance on managing medical devices that expects a documented maintenance policy. A model that changes when a device is serviced changes the organisation's compliance position, and the change has to be documented before it is deployed, not after.

This topic covers the full chain: the failure modes of the four device classes that dominate hospital fleets, the time-to-event framing that makes censoring explicit, the construction and validation of health indicators, the cost model that sets an alert threshold, and the evaluation design that can tell whether the programme worked. It builds on the signal and feature topics and on the deployment topic, and it is the only topic in the module where the outcome is an operational decision rather than a measurement.

### 1.1 Who is blocked

Five roles ask five different questions of the same fleet, and the data scientist usually receives the request from one role and builds for another.

The clinical engineering director asks how many technicians and how much budget the fleet needs next year. The answer is a capacity and cost model, not a list of devices. The maintenance planner asks which work order to open this morning. The answer is a ranked queue with a lead time, not a risk score. The ward manager asks whether there is a working pump for the next infusion. The answer is an availability number by ward and shift. The finance function asks whether to lease, buy, or extend the life of the fleet. The answer is a total cost of ownership comparison. The quality and safety function asks whether the maintenance policy is defensible to an accreditor after an incident. The answer is a documented justification with the evidence behind it.

A model built for the planner produces a list. A model built for the director produces a business case. A model built for the accreditor produces a written justification. They share a dataset but not a deliverable, and a team that does not name the role will produce the wrong artifact and discover it at the steering committee.

### 1.2 The four costs, and who bears them

| Cost | Who bears it | Typical unit | How it is measured |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unplanned failure | Clinical area, biomed, patient | Per event | Repair invoice, loaner, cancelled activity |
| Unnecessary service | Biomed budget | Per visit | Technician hours and parts |
| False alarm | Ward and biomed | Per alert | Device out of service, staff time, trust |
| Missed failure | Patient and organisation | Per event | Harm, incident investigation, recall |

The first two are visible in a budget line. The third is partly invisible because the ward absorbs it as disruption rather than cost. The fourth is not in the maintenance budget at all, which is why a business case built from maintenance invoices will systematically under-weight the error that matters most.

### 1.3 Why the data do not settle the question

Three properties of maintenance data make the analysis harder than a standard prediction problem.

The outcome is rare. A well-run fleet of 240 pumps produces about 11 confirmed failures a year. Eleven events cannot support a complex model, a twelve-covariate regression, or a subgroup analysis by campus. The analysis has to be designed around the event count, not around the data volume.

The record is generated by the process being modelled. A service call exists because someone decided to make it. A repair exists because someone decided the device was faulty. The maintenance policy, the staffing level, and the ward culture are all inside the label.

The counterfactual is missing. The programme is justified by failures it prevented, and a prevented failure leaves no record. The only way to observe it is to hold some devices under the old policy, which is expensive and politically difficult.

Those three properties are why this topic spends as much time on event definitions, denominators, and evaluation design as on models.

## 3. The core mental model

Predictive maintenance is not a forecasting problem attached to a machine. It is a decision problem with a measurement problem inside it. Three layers have to be kept separate, because an error in one layer is easily mistaken for an error in another.

```text
   PHYSICAL LAYER              OBSERVATION LAYER              DECISION LAYER
   -------------               -----------------              --------------

   degradation                 telemetry                    alert rule
   wear, fatigue,     ---->    error codes          ---->    threshold on
   corrosion,                 usage counters                P(fail before
   calibration drift,         self-test results             next slot)
   design-induced             service records                    |
        |                          |                              |
        | hazard h(t)              | censoring                    | cost of
        | survival S(t)            | missingness                  | inspection
        |                          | endogeneity                  | cost of failure
        v                          v                              v
   time to failure          estimate of h(t)              work order
                                                             |
                                                             v
                                                       feedback: the
                                                       intervention changes
                                                       the failure process
```

Read the diagram from left to right, then read the arrow at the bottom back to the left. The model never observes degradation directly. It observes a proxy, and the proxy is produced by a process that includes the maintenance organisation. That back-arrow is the difference between equipment reliability and clinical equipment reliability.

### 3.1 The P-F interval is the budget

Every failure mode has a point at which it becomes detectable (the potential failure, P) and a point at which the device stops performing its function (the functional failure, F). The time between them is the P-F interval. It is the budget for the entire programme.

| Device class | Failure mode | Detection at P | Functional failure at F | Typical P-F interval |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Infusion pump | Occlusion pressure sensor drift | Self-test offset exceeds limit | Under-delivery or false occlusion alarm | Weeks to months |
| Infusion pump | Battery capacity fade | Runtime on battery falls below threshold | Pump stops during transport | Minutes to hours |
| Ventilator | Blower bearing wear | Vibration or current signature rises | Loss of tidal volume or shutdown | Weeks |
| Ventilator | Oxygen cell depletion | Cell output falls at calibration | Delivered fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) below setpoint | Days to weeks |
| Computed tomography (CT) scanner | X-ray tube arcing | Filament current instability, exposure faults | Scan aborted, no image | Days to weeks |
| Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) | Cryogen boil-off | Helium level trend | Magnet quench, system offline | Weeks |
| Laboratory analyser | Peristaltic tubing wear | Sample volume variance rises | Failed calibration, incorrect result | Days |
| Laboratory analyser | Photometer lamp ageing | Lamp hours, absorbance drift | Bias in every result | Weeks |

If the model's lead time is shorter than the P-F interval, the alert arrives after the failure is already inevitable but before it is visible. If the lead time is much longer than the P-F interval, the alert is a forecast with a wide error bar and the planner cannot act on it. The useful horizon is the interval in between, and it differs by device class. A single "30-day failure risk" score applied to a fleet of pumps, ventilators, and analysers is a horizon that is wrong for at least two of them.

### 3.2 Three clocks

A device ages on at least three clocks: calendar time, powered-on hours, and cycles (infusions delivered, scans acquired, ventilator breaths). The clocks diverge. A pump in a 24-bed intensive care unit may run ten times the infusion count of a pump in an outpatient clinic in the same calendar month. A CT scanner that is used for 40 examinations a day accumulates tube heat units far faster than one used for 10.

Calendar age is the default because it is the only clock in the asset register. It is also the worst predictor of mechanical wear. The first modelling decision is which clock defines the time scale, and the answer is the one that correlates with the failure mechanism, not the one that is easiest to join.

### 3.3 The decision is not "will it fail"

The output of the model is not a probability of failure in the abstract. It is a comparison of two expected costs at a point in time:

```text
  act now:        cost of inspection + cost of taking the device out of service
  wait:           P(failure before the next opportunity) x cost of failure

  act if:         P x C_failure  >  C_inspection + C_disruption
```

The threshold that falls out of this comparison is usually much lower than a clinician expects, because an inspection is cheap relative to a failure. That is the good news. The bad news is that the number of alerts at that threshold is usually larger than the technician capacity, so the binding constraint is capacity, not cost. A programme that ignores capacity produces a queue, and a queue produces delayed responses, which destroys the lead time the model was supposed to provide.

### 3.4 Why a service call is not a random event

A service call is a human decision. It depends on who is on shift, how busy the ward is, whether the device is in a critical care area, whether a loaner is available, and whether the previous call for the same device was resolved. It is also endogenous to the maintenance policy: a department that responds quickly to calls receives more calls, because calling has a low cost to the caller.

This matters because the call record is often the only timestamped outcome available. If the label is a call, the model estimates the hazard of calling. If the label is a confirmed failure, the model estimates the hazard of failing. The two hazards have different shapes, different covariates, and different interventions. A programme that reports "we predict 80% of service calls" has built a demand-forecasting model for the biomed department, which may be useful, but it is not a reliability model.

### 3.5 Four quantities a programme must report

A maintenance programme is described by four quantities, and a model that reports only one of them cannot be operated. The four are not alternatives; they are the minimum set.

| Quantity | Question it answers | Unit | Consequence if missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alert volume | How many interventions per week | Alerts per device-year | The team is overwhelmed or idle |
| Alert precision | How many are real | Proportion | Trust collapses on false alarms |
| Lead time | How much notice | Hours, distribution | No time to act |
| Coverage | Which failures are seen | Proportion of failures | Blind spots stay blind |

A programme with high precision and low coverage catches a few failures early and misses the rest. A programme with high coverage and low precision floods the team and trains it to ignore alerts. A programme with good precision and coverage but a lead time of two hours cannot be actioned on a device that fails within one shift. The four quantities trade off against each other, and the trade-off is a service-design decision, not a modelling decision.

| Term | Definition | Common misuse | Unit or coding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asset | A single maintained item with a unique identifier | Used for a model or a device type | Asset ID, unique device identification (UDI-DI) |
| Fleet | The set of assets under one maintenance policy | Used for all devices in the hospital | Count |
| CMMS | Computerised maintenance management system; the work-order and asset database | Treated as a source of truth for failure | System |
| Work order | A record of a maintenance task, planned or corrective | Equated with a failure | Identifier |

| Run-to-failure | Policy of repairing only after failure | Used for safety-critical devices by mistake | Policy |

| Failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) | Structured enumeration of failure modes and consequences | Replaced by a data-driven feature search | Method |
| Criticality | Combination of severity, occurrence, and detectability | Reduced to repair cost | Score |
| False alarm | Alert without a true impending failure | Counted only as technician time | Event |
| Missed detection | Failure without a preceding alert | Not measured | Event |
| Alert precision (positive predictive value, PPV) | Share of alerts that are true | Confused with model accuracy | Proportion |
| Lead time | Time between alert and failure | Reported as a mean without a distribution | Hours |
| Downtime | Time a device is unavailable for clinical use | Measured only as repair time | Hours |

| Duty cycle | Utilisation relative to the design maximum | Ignored when pooling sites | Proportion |
| Alarm fatigue | Desensitisation after repeated non-actionable alarms | Treated as a user problem | Behavioural |
| Availability | Share of demand for which a working device exists | Measured per device rather than per demand | Proportion |
| Calibration drift | Gradual offset between measured and true value | Treated as a failure only at the limit | Unit of the measurand |
| Failure threshold | The value of the health indicator that defines failure | Chosen after seeing the data | Unit of the indicator |
| Model card | Short document of purpose, data, performance, and limits | Replaced by a slide | Document |
| Negative control | A change expected to move the indicator but not the failure risk | Omitted from validation | Method |
| Prognostic horizon | The furthest time ahead at which the prediction is validated | Confused with the RUL itself | Hours |
| Reliability | Probability of performing a required function for a stated period | Used loosely as "quality" | Proportion |
| Shadow mode | Running a model without acting on its output | Skipped to save time | Deployment mode |

Three pairs in the table cause most of the confusion in practice.

**Failure rate versus hazard rate.** The failure rate of a fleet is a single number with person-time in the denominator. The hazard rate is a function of device age. A fleet can have a constant failure rate while every individual device has a rising hazard, if the fleet contains a mix of ages. Reporting one as the other hides the ageing profile that a replacement decision depends on.

**MTBF versus device life.** MTBF is person-time divided by events. A fleet with a 6.1 per 100 device-year failure rate has an MTBF of 16.3 device-years. That does not mean a device lasts 16 years. It means that, at the current rate, one failure occurs per 16.3 device-years of operation across the fleet. With a rising hazard, the MTBF changes as the fleet ages.

**Health indicator versus failure probability.** A health indicator is a measurement with a direction. A failure probability is a model output with a calibration claim. A vendor that reports a "health score from 0 to 100" has not reported a probability, and a threshold on the score is not a decision threshold. The score has to be mapped to an outcome before it can set a maintenance interval.

### 4.1 Units and time scales

A number without a unit is a defect, and in maintenance data the defect is usually in the time scale rather than the value. The same fleet can be described by four different denominators, and they answer different questions.

| Denominator | Definition | Question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Device-years | Sum of time in service | How often does a device fail per year of use | 6.14 per 100 device-years |
| Device-months | The same, in months | Short-window planning | 0.51 per 100 device-months |
| Per 100 devices | Events divided by device count | Budgeting per asset | 4.6 per 100 devices per year |
| Per 1,000 infusions | Events divided by cycles | Wear per use | Depends on the fleet |

The first two are rates. The third is a proportion and is often mislabelled as a rate. The fourth is the most mechanically meaningful for a pump and the least available, because the infusion counter is often not exported to the CMMS.

When a vendor reports a failure rate, the first question is which denominator. A rate "per device" without a time unit is a count, not a rate, and it cannot be compared across fleets of different ages.

## 5. The mechanics, step by step

The procedure assumes you have access to the CMMS, the device telemetry, and the people who maintain the fleet. It produces a maintenance decision rule, not a model in isolation.

### Step 1. Define the decision, the owner, and the decision unit

**Input:** the current maintenance policy, the budget, the clinical criticality of the device class, and the people who act on an alert.

**Output:** a decision statement naming the device class, the cadence, the action, the owner, and the time window.

**Rationale:** a prediction without a decision is a dashboard. The decision fixes the horizon (how far ahead the model must look), the acceptable false-alarm rate (how much technician time the owner will spend), and the data required (which device class, which failure modes). Different decisions need different models, and a single model cannot serve all of them.

**Branch point:** four outcomes, shown in the table.

| Decision | Horizon | Model output | Owner | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dispatch now | Hours | P(failure before next shift) | Duty biomed | Pump alarming on a ward |
| Schedule at next service slot | Days to weeks | P(failure before the slot) | Maintenance planner | Pump with rising motor current |
| Plan replacement | Months to years | Expected cost of continued ownership | Capital committee | CT tube at end of life |
| Monitor only | None | Indicator and trend | Clinical engineering | New model with no history |

**Edge case:** if the decision is capital replacement, RUL alone is not the output. The output is the expected cost per study or per infusion over the remaining life, compared with a replacement. A device with a long RUL can still be uneconomic to keep.

### Step 2. Define the failure event operationally

**Input:** work orders, device error logs, clinical incident reports, and the maintenance policy.

**Output:** a written event definition and an event table with one row per event, the time, the type, and the evidence.

**Rationale:** the event is the label, and the label determines what the model learns. Four records are routinely conflated: a service call, a work order, a confirmed failure, and a planned maintenance visit. They have different generators and different biases.

**Branch point:** four event definitions, each with a different meaning.

| Event definition | What it measures | Main bias | Appropriate use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Service call | A human response to a symptom | Reflects staffing and ward culture | Demand forecasting for the biomed team |
| Work order | An administrative task | Includes planned work and repeat visits | Labour and parts planning |
| Confirmed failure | Loss of the device's clinical function | Under-records failures fixed without a record | Reliability and RUL |
| Unscheduled downtime | Loss of availability | Depends on how downtime is logged | Service-level reporting |

**Edge case:** a device can fail and be repaired without a work order, for example when a nurse swaps a pump for a spare and the faulty unit is repaired in a batch. A work order can exist without a failure, for example a planned battery replacement. The event table should record the evidence, not only the category.

### Step 3. Enumerate failure modes by device class

**Input:** manufacturer service manuals, failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) or failure mode, effects, and criticality analysis (FMECA) documents, recall and complaint history, and technician interviews.

**Output:** a failure-mode register with, for each mode, the mechanism, the observable proxy, the P-F interval, and the criticality.

**Rationale:** telemetry features are proxies for specific mechanisms. A model built without a failure-mode register is a correlation search over hundreds of channels, and the correlations it finds are usually with utilisation, cleaning, or firmware version. The register also tells you which modes are not observable and therefore cannot be predicted from the data you have.

| Device class | Dominant modes | Observable proxy | P-F interval | Criticality |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Infusion pump | Occlusion sensor drift | Pressure zero offset at self-test | Weeks | High |
| Infusion pump | Motor and gearbox wear | Current draw, delivery-time variance | Weeks to months | High |
| Infusion pump | Battery capacity fade | Runtime on battery test | Hours at failure | High |
| Infusion pump | Door latch and free-flow valve | Latch cycle count, failed self-test | Days | High |
| Ventilator | Blower bearing wear | Vibration, motor current | Weeks | Critical |
| Ventilator | Flow sensor drift | Offset at calibration, tidal volume error | Days to weeks | Critical |
| Ventilator | Oxygen cell depletion | Cell output at calibration | Days | Critical |
| Ventilator | Expiratory valve sticking | Valve response time, pressure waveform | Days | Critical |
| CT scanner | X-ray tube arcing | Filament current, exposure fault count | Days to weeks | High |
| CT scanner | Detector calibration drift | Air-scan uniformity, ring artefact index | Weeks | Medium |
| MRI | Cryogen boil-off | Helium level trend, compressor duty | Weeks | High |
| Laboratory analyser | Peristaltic tubing wear | Sample volume variance, pressure trace | Days | Medium |
| Laboratory analyser | Photometer lamp ageing | Lamp hours, absorbance drift | Weeks | Medium |
| Laboratory analyser | Probe clog | Aspiration failures, wash pressure | Hours to days | Medium |

**Edge case:** a mode that is design-induced, such as the sound-abatement foam degradation in the Philips Respironics recall of 2021, is not predictable from operating telemetry because it is not a function of use. It is detected through complaint monitoring and supplier quality data, not through a degradation model. A predictive programme should not claim to cover it.

### Step 4. Build the asset master and the exposure timeline

**Input:** the CMMS asset register, the finance register, the unique device identification (UDI) records, location history, and install and retirement dates.

**Output:** one row per device per interval of observation, with start, end, and the reason the interval ended.

**Rationale:** every rate needs a denominator, and the denominator is time at risk. Devices move between departments, are loaned to other sites, are traded in, and are cannibalised for parts. A register that records only the current location cannot produce the exposure.

| Field | Example | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Asset identifier | PUMP-00417 | Join key across CMMS and telemetry |
| UDI-DI | Unique device identification, device identifier | Model-level recall matching |
| Model and firmware | Model X, firmware 3.2.1 | Stratification and version drift |
| Install date | 2024-03-11 | Left-truncation boundary |
| Retirement date | 2025-09-30 | Censoring time |
| Location history | ICU-A, then Ward 4 | Duty cycle and case mix |
| Powered hours | Cumulative counter | Alternative time scale |
| Infusion count | Cumulative counter | Alternative time scale |
| Last PM date | 2025-11-02 | Interval-censoring boundary |

**Edge case:** the CMMS register and the finance register disagree by 5% to 15% in most organisations, usually because devices are transferred without a record or retired without disposal. The disagreement is itself a finding: the fleet size is uncertain, so every rate has an additional denominator uncertainty that should be reported rather than hidden.

### Step 5. Classify censoring and truncation

**Input:** the exposure timeline and the event table.

**Output:** each device-interval labelled as an event or a censored observation, with the reason for censoring.

**Rationale:** censoring is not missing data. A censored device provides information: it survived until the censoring time. Discarding it wastes that information; counting it as a survivor to the end of the window overstates survival. The distinction between independent and informative censoring determines whether the standard estimators are valid.

**Branch point:** six situations, each with a different treatment.

| Situation | Observation | Treatment | Risk if ignored |
|---|---|---|---|
| In service at window end | Right-censored at window end | Include as censored | Understates the rate if dropped |
| Traded in or retired | Right-censored at retirement | Include, test for informativeness | Overstates survival if retirement is health-related |
| Transferred to another site | Censored at transfer, re-enters if tracked | Link records where possible | Double-counts or loses exposure |
| Record lost | Censored at last confirmed use | Report the loss rate | Unknown direction |
| Already old at window start | Left-truncated | Condition on survival to start | Length-biased sample |
| Failure between two PM visits | Interval-censored | Use interval-censored methods | Treats a 6-month interval as an exact time |

**Edge case:** informative censoring is the dangerous one. If a department retires the least reliable pumps first, the units that remain look healthier than the fleet they came from. The test is to compare the pre-censoring failure rate of units that were later retired with the rate of units that stayed. A large difference means the censoring carries information, and the survival curve needs a model that allows it, or the analysis needs to be restricted to a cohort with a common retirement rule.

### Step 6. Compute the rate with person-time

**Input:** the event table and the exposure timeline.

**Output:** the number of events, the device-years at risk, the rate per 100 device-years, and a confidence interval.

**Rationale:** the failure rate is events divided by time at risk. The naive alternative, events divided by devices divided by the window length, assumes that every device was at risk for the whole window. In a fleet with turnover, that assumption is false, and the bias is always in the same direction: the naive rate is too low.

**Arithmetic:** 22 failures, 240 devices, 24 months. The naive rate is 22 / (240 x 2) = 4.58 per 100 device-years. The actual exposure is 4,299 device-months, or 358.2 device-years. The person-time rate is 22 / 358.2 = 6.14 per 100 device-years (95% CI 4.04 to 9.33, log-normal approximation). The naive rate is 34% too low.

**Branch point:** if the rate is below about 1 per 100 device-years, a single fleet will produce too few events to fit a stratified model. The options are to pool device classes with a hierarchical model, to extend the window, or to change the outcome from failure to a leading indicator.

**Edge case:** a rate computed on device-years assumes a constant hazard within the stratum. If the hazard rises with age, the rate is an average over the age distribution and will change as the fleet ages, even if the underlying hazard does not.

### Step 7. Stratify by duty cycle and model

**Input:** utilisation counters, location history, and model identifiers.

**Output:** rates by stratum and a decision about whether to pool.

**Rationale:** calendar age is a poor time scale. A pump in intensive care may deliver ten times the infusions of a ward pump in the same month. If the model pools them on calendar time, the intensive-care pumps look like early failures and the ward pumps look durable, and the model learns the ward rather than the wear.

| Time scale | Correlates with | Use when | Data source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calendar months | Administrative cycles | Staffing and contract questions | Asset register |
| Powered hours | Electrical and thermal ageing | Electronics, batteries, lamps | Device counter |
| Cycles or infusions | Mechanical wear | Pumps, ventilators, analysers | Device counter |
| Scans or examinations | Tube and detector load | Imaging systems | Modality log |
| Cumulative dose | Radiation damage | CT tube | Dose report |

**Branch point:** if the utilisation counters reset on a firmware update or a board replacement, the time scale is broken for the affected interval. The options are to reconstruct from work orders, to treat the interval as missing, or to start a new exposure interval at the reset.

**Edge case:** a device can be powered on but idle. Powered hours overstate mechanical wear for a pump that sits on a shelf; infusion count understates standby degradation for a defibrillator that is rarely used but must work on demand. The choice of clock should follow the failure mode, and a device with several modes may need more than one clock.

### Step 8. Fit the time-to-event model

**Input:** the stratified event data with censoring.

**Output:** a Kaplan-Meier curve, a parametric fit with parameters and intervals, and a check of the model's assumptions.

**Rationale:** the nonparametric curve makes no assumption about the shape of the hazard and is the reference. The parametric model is needed for extrapolation and for RUL, but it is only trustworthy inside the range where it has been checked against the nonparametric curve. Outside that range it is an assumption dressed as a number.

**Arithmetic:** the 240-pump fleet gives a Weibull shape of 1.494 and a scale of 92.48 months. A shape above 1 indicates a rising hazard, consistent with wear-out. The fitted survival at 24 months is 0.875, and the Kaplan-Meier estimate is 0.876, so the model is adequate inside the window. The median is not reached: survival is still 0.876 at 24 months, so the data cannot estimate a median life.

**Branch point:** if the Kaplan-Meier curves for two strata cross, the proportional hazards assumption fails. The options are to stratify the baseline hazard, to use an accelerated failure time model, or to model the cause-specific hazards separately.

**Edge case:** with 22 events, a model can support about two covariates at the conventional rule of ten events per variable. A model with twelve covariates and 22 events is a fit to noise, and the confidence intervals will be wide enough to include every plausible effect even if the point estimates look precise.

### Step 9. Build the health indicator from telemetry

**Input:** high-frequency telemetry, self-test results, and calibration records.

**Output:** one scalar per device per window, with a defined unit, direction, and reference distribution.

**Rationale:** the health indicator is the bridge between the physics and the survival model. It has to respond to degradation and not to utilisation, cleaning, consumable lot, or firmware. A feature that passes the first test and fails the others will produce alerts that track the maintenance schedule rather than the machine.

| Indicator | Device | What it captures | Main confounder |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure zero offset | Infusion pump | Occlusion sensor drift | Temperature at self-test |
| Motor current RMS | Infusion pump, ventilator | Bearing and gearbox wear | Fluid viscosity, tubing brand |
| Delivery-time variance | Infusion pump | Mechanism friction | Back-pressure from the line |
| Blower vibration crest factor | Ventilator | Bearing pitting | Mounting and enclosure resonance |
| Flow sensor offset | Ventilator | Sensor drift | Humidity and gas composition |
| Tube heat units per scan | CT scanner | Tube load | Protocol mix |
| Helium boil-off rate | MRI | Cryogen system efficiency | Ambient temperature, shield vacuum |
| Sample volume variance | Analyser | Tubing wear, probe clog | Sample viscosity, clot burden |
| Lamp hours and absorbance drift | Analyser | Photometer ageing | Reagent lot |

**Branch point:** if the indicator is a residual from a normal-behaviour model, the residual is only valid for the operating conditions in the training data. A pump that is used with a new tubing brand, or a ventilator running a new mode, will produce a residual that looks like degradation. The model needs the operating condition as a covariate, or it needs to be retrained per condition.

**Edge case:** a step change in the indicator after a firmware update is a measurement change, not a degradation change. The indicator distribution should be recomputed after every firmware change, and the change should be recorded in the model's version history.

### Step 10. Validate prospectively, at the alert level

**Input:** the indicator, the event table, and a temporal split.

**Output:** alert precision, recall, lead-time distribution, and false alarms per device-year.

**Rationale:** discrimination metrics answer a different question from the one the programme asks. An area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.85 says that a randomly chosen failing device is ranked above a randomly chosen surviving device 85% of the time. It says nothing about how many alerts a week the department will receive, or whether the alerts arrive early enough to act.

**Arithmetic:** a rule that raises 46 alerts per year, of which 30 precede a confirmed failure and 16 do not, has an alert precision of 30 / 46 = 65%. If 22 failures occur in the year and 30 alerts precede a failure, the recall can exceed 100% because a device can generate more than one alert before it fails; the denominator has to be failures, and the numerator has to be failures with at least one preceding alert, which is a different count from alerts that precede a failure.

**Branch point:** if precision is low and recall is high, the threshold is too low or the indicator is too noisy. If precision is high and recall is low, the indicator is too specific and is missing a failure mode.

**Edge case:** a random train-test split leaks information, because the same device appears in both sets and the model memorises the device. The split should be by time, by installation cohort, or by site. The reported performance should be the performance on devices the model has never seen.

### Step 11. Set the threshold with a cost model

**Input:** alert precision at each candidate threshold, the lead-time distribution, the cost of an inspection, the cost of a failure, the cost of disruption, and technician capacity.

**Output:** a threshold, an expected annual cost, and an expected alert volume.

**Rationale:** the threshold is an economic decision, not a statistical one. The rule is to act when the expected cost of waiting exceeds the expected cost of acting.

**Arithmetic:** with an inspection cost of USD 260 and a failure cost of USD 2,850 (2026 prices, fully loaded), the break-even probability is 260 / 2,850 = 0.091. An alert with a 10% chance of a failure before the next service slot is worth inspecting. Adding a disruption cost of USD 180 per alert raises the break-even probability to (260 + 180) / 2,850 = 0.154.

**Branch point:** the cost-optimal threshold usually produces more alerts than the team can service. The decision table then has three outcomes, not two.

| Condition | Action | Owner | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| P(failure) above the cost threshold and capacity available | Inspect now | Duty biomed | Same shift |
| P(failure) above the threshold and capacity unavailable | Triage by criticality and lead time; add a shift or a loaner | Maintenance manager | Within 24 hours |
| P(failure) below the threshold | Monitor; reassess at the next scheduled service | Planner | Next PM |
| No usable indicator | Fall back to the manufacturer interval | Planner | Per manual |

**Edge case:** the threshold should be set per device class, because the failure costs differ by orders of magnitude. A ventilator failure in an intensive care unit and a laboratory analyser failure in a satellite lab do not share a cost, and a single threshold will over-service one and under-service the other.

### Step 12. Design the service response

**Input:** the alert, the spare-parts inventory, the technician skills matrix, and the clinical schedule.

**Output:** a work-order template and a service-level agreement for each alert tier.

**Rationale:** an alert that cannot be actioned within its lead time is noise. The response design determines whether the model's lead time is used or wasted. It also determines the false-alarm cost, because a false alarm that consumes a scheduled slot displaces real work.

| Alert tier | Response | Parts | Who | Window |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Critical, life-support | Swap device, then repair | Pre-kitted | Duty biomed plus clinical lead | Immediate |
| High, therapy device | Inspect before next use | Pre-kitted | Duty biomed | Same shift |
| Medium, diagnostic device | Schedule in the next service block | Standard stock | Planner | 72 hours |
| Low, monitoring only | Add to the PM list | None | Planner | Next PM |

**Edge case:** night and weekend capacity is usually the binding constraint. A model tuned to a weekday response time will produce alerts that sit in a queue until Monday, and the failures it was meant to prevent will occur on Saturday.

### Step 13. Evaluate the programme

**Input:** the pre-intervention period, the intervention, and the post-intervention period.

**Output:** an effect estimate on failures, downtime, cost, and harm, with a design that supports the claim.

**Rationale:** a before-after comparison is confounded by seasonality, device ageing, concurrent recalls, and changes in clinical demand. The programme will look effective in December if the fleet was new in January. A design that compares sites or staggered rollout dates removes some of that confounding.

**Arithmetic:** with 11 failures per year in a 240-pump fleet, a 30% reduction is 3.3 fewer failures. A simple Poisson comparison of 11 against 7.7 has low power; detecting that difference reliably needs several years or several sites. If the programme cannot be evaluated on failures, the primary outcome should be a process measure, such as alert-to-repair time or the share of failures with a preceding alert.

**Branch point:** if randomisation is impossible, a stepped-wedge rollout by campus or by device class gives a stronger design than a single before-after comparison.

**Edge case:** the intervention changes the data-generating process. Once the team services alerted devices, the failures the model would have predicted do not occur, so the observed failure rate falls and the model's apparent precision changes. Evaluation needs a shadow mode, a holdout, or a control group that continues under the old policy.

### Step 14. Monitor, recalibrate, and retire the model

**Input:** live alerts, outcomes, firmware versions, and the maintenance record.

**Output:** a monthly drift report, a re-estimation schedule, and written retirement criteria.

**Rationale:** a model trained on one pump generation, one firmware version, and one case mix does not transfer silently. The population shifts when a new model is introduced, when a ward changes its protocol, and when a manufacturer changes a consumable. The model has to be monitored on the same alert-level metrics used at validation.

| Metric | Frequency | Trigger | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alert precision | Monthly | Below 50% for two months | Recalibrate or raise threshold |
| Alert volume | Weekly | Above technician capacity | Triage or suppress low tiers |
| Missed failures | Monthly | Any failure with no alert | Review the failure mode |
| Indicator drift | Monthly | Distribution shift beyond 2 SD | Recompute the reference |
| Firmware change | Event-driven | Any version change | Re-validate the indicator |
| Fleet composition | Quarterly | More than 10% new models | Re-estimate the baseline |

**Edge case:** a model can be retired for the wrong reason. If the failure rate falls because the programme works, the model appears to have too many false alarms, because the failures it prevented are no longer in the denominator. The retirement decision should use the counterfactual, not the observed rate.

### Step 15. Write the model card and the change record

**Input:** the event definition, the exposure, the model, the validation results, the threshold, and the cost assumptions.

**Output:** a model card and a change record that the quality system can file.

**Rationale:** a maintenance change is a change to a controlled process. In an accredited hospital it has to be justified in writing, and in a regulated manufacturer it may be a change under the quality management system. The model card is the minimum document that lets a reviewer reconstruct what was done and decide whether the change is safe.

| Section | Content | Why the reviewer needs it |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | The decision the model supports | Prevents scope creep |
| Population | Device classes, models, sites, window | Defines the boundary of validity |
| Event definition | What counts as a failure | The label is the claim |
| Time scale | Calendar, hours, or cycles | Comparability across sites |
| Data sources | CMMS, telemetry, firmware versions | Reproducibility and drift |
| Performance | Alert volume, precision, lead time, coverage | Operability |
| Threshold | Value and cost assumptions | The decision rule |
| Limits | Failure modes not covered, horizon | Honest scope |
| Owner and review date | Who is accountable | Governance |

**Edge case:** if the model changes a maintenance interval, the change record is not enough. The organisation needs an AEM justification that covers the equipment's use, the consequences of failure, backup availability, and incident history. The model card is an input to that document, not a substitute for it.

### Step 16. Hand over, train, and retire

**Input:** the model card, the alert feed, the escalation path, and the training plan.

**Output:** trained staff, a feedback route from the ward back to the model owner, and written retirement criteria.

**Rationale:** a model that is not understood by the person acting on it is either ignored or over-trusted. Both are failures. The handover is where the programme acquires the organisational knowledge that keeps it alive after the data team moves on.

| Role | What they need to know | What they do with it |
|---|---|---|
| Duty biomed | What an alert means and what to check first | Triage and repair |
| Maintenance planner | How to schedule around the lead time | Capacity planning |
| Ward manager | Why a device is being taken out of service | Clinical coordination |
| Clinical engineering director | Cost, coverage, and residual risk | Budget and policy |
| Quality and safety | The evidence behind the change | Accreditation and incident review |

**Edge case:** the feedback route is also the model's early-warning system. A technician who finds that an alert was caused by a new tubing brand, or that a failure had no alert because the device was not transmitting, is producing the most valuable data the programme will receive. If there is no route to record it, the model will drift without anyone noticing.

### Decision table for the programme review

| Finding | Interpretation | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Precision above 60%, coverage above 70%, lead time above the P-F interval | The programme is working as designed | Continue and monitor |
| Precision above 60%, coverage below 50% | A failure mode is not observed | Add an indicator or accept the gap |
| Precision below 40%, coverage high | Too many alerts, threshold too low | Raise the threshold or add confirmation |
| Precision high, lead time below the P-F interval | The alert is too late to act | Move the indicator earlier or shorten the response |
| Alert volume above capacity | The service design is the constraint | Triage, add capacity, or reduce scope |
| Failure with no alert | A blind spot or a data outage | Review the mode and the telemetry link |

The table is a review instrument, not an algorithm. Its purpose is to make the trade-off explicit at the quarterly review, so that the programme is adjusted on evidence rather than on the loudest complaint.

## 6. Worked example

### 6.1 The case

A hospital group operates 240 infusion pumps across four campuses. The clinical engineering department runs a scheduled programme: four planned maintenance visits per pump per year, at USD 145 per visit (2026 prices, fully loaded technician time and consumables). Unscheduled failures cost USD 2,850 each (2026 prices: repair, expedited freight, loaner, and the clinical cost of a cancelled or delayed infusion).

The department is offered a predictive maintenance platform: sensors and a gateway on each pump, a cloud analytics subscription, and an alert feed into the CMMS. The platform costs USD 60,000 per year for the fleet (2026 prices), plus USD 260 per alert inspection. The vendor claims it will prevent 7 of the 11 annual failures.

The department has 24 months of CMMS data and 18 months of telemetry. The question is whether the programme is worth running, and at what threshold.

### 6.2 The event definition

A failure is defined as a pump removed from clinical service because it cannot deliver at the programmed rate within 5%, or cannot complete an infusion because of an occlusion-sensor, motor, battery, or door-latch fault, confirmed by a biomedical technician.

Excluded from the count: user error (misloaded line, wrong cassette), planned maintenance, software configuration, and cosmetic damage. Also excluded: the 50 service calls in the year that were resolved without a repair, which are recorded separately.

This definition matters. If the department had used "service call" as the event, the annual count would be 61 rather than 11, and the rate would be 8.51 per 100 device-years rather than 6.14. The model would then be trained to predict calls, and the strongest predictor would be ward staffing.

| Record | Count in the year | Included | Reason |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Confirmed pump failure | 11 | Yes | The outcome of interest |
| Service call resolved without repair | 50 | No | User error, consumable, or no fault found |
| Planned maintenance visit | 960 | No | Scheduled, not a failure |
| Battery replaced at PM | 74 | No | Planned wear item |
| Device swapped out, tested normal | 19 | No | No confirmed fault |
| Failure repaired off-site without a work order | 3 | Yes, if recovered | Found by audit, not by the CMMS |

The last row is the one that audits usually catch. Three failures repaired in a batch, without an individual work order, are missing from the CMMS and therefore from the naive event count. If the department repairs 15% of faults that way, the event table is a sample with unknown selection, and the rate is understated by roughly the same proportion.

### 6.3 Exposure and the denominator

The fleet is not constant. Ninety pumps left during the window: some were traded in when a new model arrived, some were transferred to a sister hospital, and some were retired after a recall campaign. Their departure times are recorded. The remaining 128 were still in service at the end of the window.

| Quantity | Value | Unit |
|---|---:|---|
| Pumps at window start | 240 | devices |
| Confirmed failures | 22 | events |
| Pumps censored early | 90 | devices |
| Pumps censored at window end | 128 | devices |
| Total device-months at risk | 4,299 | device-months |
| Total device-years at risk | 358.2 | device-years |
| Mean observed follow-up | 17.9 | months |

The exposure is the sum of each pump's time in service, not 240 times 24. The difference is 1,461 device-months, or 121.8 device-years, which is a third of the naive denominator.

| Component | Device-months | Device-years | Share |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Time to failure, 22 events | 279.6 | 23.3 | 6.5% |
| Time to early exit, 90 censored | 947.4 | 79.0 | 22.0% |
| Time to window end, 128 censored | 3,072.0 | 256.0 | 71.5% |
| Total | 4,299.0 | 358.2 | 100% |

The table is worth producing because it shows where the information is. Only 6.5% of the exposure ends in an event; the rest is censored. An analysis that discards censored observations throws away 93.7% of the information and estimates the rate from 22 pumps that happened to fail.

### 6.4 The rate

The naive rate is 22 / (240 x 2) = 4.58 per 100 device-years. The person-time rate is 22 / 358.2 = 6.14 per 100 device-years, with a 95% confidence interval of 4.04 to 9.33. The naive calculation understates the rate by 34%.

That factor propagates. The expected number of failures next year is not 11; at the person-time rate and the current exposure it is 6.14% of 358.2, or 22 over two years, which is the same 11 per year only because the exposure happens to be about 179 device-years per year. If the fleet shrinks to 180 pumps with the same turnover, the expected count changes, and the business case changes with it.

### 6.5 The survival model

A Weibull model fitted to the 22 events and 218 censored observations gives a shape of 1.494 and a scale of 92.48 months. The shape above 1 is consistent with wear-out. The fitted survival at 24 months is 0.875, and the Kaplan-Meier estimate is 0.876, so the model tracks the data inside the window.

The median residual life for a pump that has survived 18 months is 60.3 months. That number should not be used as a replacement date. No pump in the dataset has been observed beyond 24 months, and the Kaplan-Meier median is not reached. The 60-month figure is an extrapolation from a curve fitted to the first 24 months. What the data do support is a three-month failure probability of 2.2% for a pump that has survived 18 months, which for 240 pumps is about 5.3 expected failures in the next quarter.

### 6.6 The cost comparison

**Scheduled programme, annual.**

| Line | Arithmetic | USD per year, 2026 prices |
|---|---|---:|
| Planned visits | 240 x 4 x 145 | 139,200 |
| Unscheduled failures | 11 x 2,850 | 31,350 |
| Total | | 170,550 |

**Predictive programme, annual.**

| Line | Arithmetic | USD per year, 2026 prices |
|---|---|---:|
| Platform | fixed subscription | 60,000 |
| Alert inspections | 46 x 260 | 11,960 |
| False alerts | 16 x 180 | 2,880 |
| Residual failures | 4 x 2,850 | 11,400 |
| Total | | 86,240 |

The annual difference is USD 84,310 in favour of the predictive programme, or USD 351 per pump per year. The cost per avoided failure is 84,310 / 7 = USD 12,044, which is high relative to the repair cost but low relative to the clinical cost of a failure in a critical care area.

The USD 2,850 failure cost is a maintenance and disruption figure. It excludes patient harm, litigation, and reputational cost, which are not monetised here and should not be. A programme justified by avoided repair cost alone will under-invest in the failure modes that matter most, because the most serious failures are the least frequent and the least expensive to repair.

### 6.7 Diagnostic one: the denominator changes the answer

If the analysis uses 240 pumps for 24 months, the rate is 4.58 per 100 device-years and the expected annual failures are 11.0. If it uses only the 128 pumps that survived to the end, the rate is 22 / (128 x 2) = 8.59 per 100 device-years. Applying that rate to the full 240-pump fleet gives 20.6 expected failures a year instead of 11.0. The two errors bracket the correct value of 6.14, and neither is obviously wrong from the spreadsheet.

The diagnostic is simple: compute the rate three ways (all devices, survivors only, person-time) and compare. If the three differ by more than 10%, the denominator is doing the work and the analysis is not ready. In this fleet the spread is 88%, which is a red flag on any downstream cost model.

The second denominator problem is the fleet size itself. The CMMS register lists 240 pumps, the finance register lists 252, and the telemetry gateway sees 231 unique device identifiers. The 21-device gap is 8.75% of the fleet, which translates to about plus or minus 0.5 failures per 100 device-years on a rate of 6.14. That is smaller than the sampling interval of 4.04 to 9.33, but it is not negligible when the rate is used to forecast a procurement count.

### 6.8 Diagnostic two: a service call is not a failure

The department logged 61 service calls in the 12 months to 31 December 2025. Eleven were confirmed failures. Fifty were resolved without a repair: misloaded lines, wrong cassette, air-in-line alarms caused by the consumable, and pumps that were swapped out and tested as normal.

If every call is counted as a failure, the rate is 61 / 2 = 30.5 events per year divided by 358.2 device-years, or 8.51 per 100 device-years, 39% above the confirmed-failure rate. The model trained on that label would learn to predict calls. The strongest predictors would be ward, shift, and staff tenure, and the alerts would arrive when the ward was busy, not when the pump was degrading.

The diagnostic is to compare the distribution of "events" across shifts and wards. A confirmed failure should be roughly uniform across shifts after adjusting for utilisation. A service call will cluster at shift change, at the end of a training day, and on wards with high staff turnover. If the event rate correlates with staffing variables more strongly than with device variables, the label is contaminated.

There is a second, subtler version of the same problem in the censoring. The 90 early exits were not random: the trade-ins were concentrated at the campus that received the new model first, and the retired units were the oldest. If the oldest units were also the least reliable, the censoring is informative, and the Kaplan-Meier curve overstates survival for a fleet that keeps its old devices. The test is to compare the failure rate before exit for the units that left with the rate for the units that stayed. If the leavers failed at twice the rate, the survival curve is biased upward and the RUL is biased long.

### 6.9 The varied assumption: what the incumbent programme actually costs

The business case above assumes four planned visits per pump per year at USD 145. Many departments run two visits per year, and some run one. The assumption is not a detail; it is the entire comparison.

| Visits per year | Cost per visit (USD) | Scheduled total (USD) | Predictive total (USD) | Difference (USD) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 4 | 145 | 170,550 | 86,240 | 84,310 |
| 3 | 145 | 135,750 | 86,240 | 49,510 |
| 2 | 145 | 100,950 | 86,240 | 14,710 |
| 2 | 110 | 84,150 | 86,240 | -2,090 |

At two visits per year at USD 110, the predictive programme costs USD 2,090 more per year than the scheduled programme. The conclusion flips on a plausible baseline. The programme is not a technology that is cheaper than maintenance; it is a technology that is cheaper than a particular amount of maintenance.

The fleet size matters for the same reason. The platform cost is fixed, so a 60-pump fleet carries the same USD 60,000 platform against one quarter of the maintenance saving. At 60 pumps the predictive programme costs USD 23,922 more per year; at 120 pumps it saves USD 12,155; at 480 pumps it saves USD 228,620.

The break-even point can be interpolated between the 60-pump and 120-pump cases. The difference moves from -23,922 to +12,155, a change of 36,077 over 60 pumps, or 601 per pump. The break-even is 60 + 23,922 / 601 = 99.8, or about 100 pumps. That number is sensitive to the same assumptions as the rest of the case: at two visits per year at USD 110, the break-even fleet is larger than 240.

### 6.10 What the analysis does not show

The analysis does not show that the programme prevents patient harm. It has no data on harm, and the failure count is too small to estimate a harm rate. It does not show that the alert arrives before the P-F interval for each failure mode; the lead-time distribution has not been measured prospectively. It does not show that the model transfers to the new pump model arriving next year. And it does not show that the 7 prevented failures are the 7 the model would have caught; that is the vendor's claim, not a measured result.

Those gaps are not reasons to reject the programme. They are the list of things a pilot should measure.

### 6.11 Lead time and the P-F interval

The cost model assumes an alert is actionable. That assumption depends on lead time, and lead time is not one number. Suppose the 30 true alerts in the year had the following distribution of time between the alert and the confirmed failure.

| Lead time | Alerts | Share | Action possible |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Less than 24 hours | 4 | 13% | Only with an immediate spare and a free technician |
| 1 to 7 days | 9 | 30% | Yes, in the next service block |
| 1 to 4 weeks | 11 | 37% | Yes, at the next planned visit |
| More than 4 weeks | 6 | 20% | Yes, but the prediction may not survive a model update |

Twenty of the 30 true alerts arrive more than a week before the failure, which is enough for a planned intervention. Four arrive inside a day, which is only useful if the department holds a spare pump on the ward. The same model can therefore be excellent for a slow failure mode and useless for a fast one, and the aggregate precision of 65% hides the difference.

The practical rule is to report lead time by failure mode, not by model. A mode with a P-F interval of days needs an alert policy and a spares policy that can respond in days. A mode with a P-F interval of weeks can tolerate a weekly review.

### 6.12 What a pilot should measure

A twelve-month pilot on 80 pumps, with three months in shadow mode and then a stepped rollout, will observe about four confirmed failures at the fleet rate of 6.14 per 100 device-years. Four events cannot estimate a failure-rate reduction. The pilot has to be judged on process outcomes that are measurable at that scale.

| Metric | Definition | Target for a go decision | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alert volume | Alerts per device-year | Below technician capacity | Operability |
| Precision | True alerts divided by all alerts | Above 50% | Trust and workload |
| Coverage | Failures with a preceding alert, divided by all failures | Above 70% | Blind spots |
| Median lead time | Median hours from alert to failure | Above the P-F interval | Actionability |
| Alert-to-repair time | Median hours from alert to device back in service | Below the lead time | Service design |
| False alarms per technician-hour | Alerts not leading to a repair, per hour worked | Below 0.2 | Workload |
| Unexpected failures | Confirmed failures with no alert and a working telemetry link | Below 20% of failures | Coverage gap |
| Data completeness | Device-days with telemetry divided by device-days in service | Above 95% | The denominator for everything |

The last metric is the one most often omitted. If the telemetry link is down for 15% of device-days, the model is blind for 15% of the exposure, and the failures in that period are not random: devices in poor network locations are also the ones that are hard to service.

# Months from window start to confirmed failure (22 events).
FAILURES = [2.1, 3.4, 4.0, 5.2, 6.7, 7.1, 8.3, 9.0, 9.8, 11.2, 12.5,
            13.1, 14.6, 15.2, 16.0, 17.3, 18.1, 19.4, 20.2, 21.0, 22.3, 23.1]

# 90 units left the fleet early (trade-in, transfer, contract end).
CENSORED = [round(4.0 + (i % 13) * 1.1, 1) for i in range(90)]

# Remaining units were still in service when the window closed.
ADMIN = [WINDOW] * (FLEET - len(FAILURES) - len(CENSORED))
TIMES = FAILURES + CENSORED + ADMIN
EVENTS = len(FAILURES)


def person_years(times):
    return sum(times) / 12.0


def rate_ci(events, exposure, z=1.96):
    """Poisson rate with a log-normal 95% interval."""
    r = events / exposure
    se = 1.0 / math.sqrt(events)
    return r, r * math.exp(-z * se), r * math.exp(z * se)


def weibull(times, events, lo=0.3, hi=4.0, step=0.001):
    """Profile-likelihood fit; scale is the characteristic life."""
    best = None
    b = lo
    while b <= hi + 1e-12:
        scale = (sum(t ** b for t in times) / events) ** (1.0 / b)
        ll = -sum((t / scale) ** b for t in times)
        ll += sum(math.log(b) - math.log(scale)
                  + (b - 1.0) * (math.log(t) - math.log(scale))
                  for t in times[:events])
        if best is None or ll > best[2]:
            best = (b, scale, ll)
        b += step
    return best


def residual_life(age, beta, scale):
    """Median remaining months given survival to age (months)."""
    return (age ** beta + scale ** beta * math.log(2.0)) ** (1.0 / beta) - age


def km_curve(times, events):
    records = [(t, 1) for t in times[:events]] + [(t, 0) for t in times[events:]]
    surv, out = 1.0, {}
    for t in sorted(set(times)):
        at_risk = sum(1 for u, _ in records if u >= t)
        deaths = sum(1 for u, e in records if u == t and e == 1)
        if deaths:
            surv *= 1.0 - deaths / at_risk
        out[t] = surv
    return out


pt = person_years(TIMES)
print(f"units {FLEET} | failures {EVENTS} | device-years at risk {pt:.1f}")
naive = EVENTS / (FLEET * WINDOW / 12.0)
r, lo, hi = rate_ci(EVENTS, pt)
print(f"naive rate {naive * 100:.2f} per 100 device-years")
print(f"person-time rate {r * 100:.2f} per 100 device-years "
      f"(95% CI {lo * 100:.2f}-{hi * 100:.2f})")
print(f"understatement {(r / naive - 1) * 100:.1f}%")

beta, scale, ll = weibull(TIMES, EVENTS)
print(f"weibull shape {beta:.3f} | scale {scale:.2f} months | loglik {ll:.2f}")
for age in (6, 12, 18, 21):
    print(f"age {age:>2} mo -> median RUL {residual_life(age, beta, scale):5.1f} mo")

km = km_curve(TIMES, EVENTS)
for h in (6, 12, 18, 24):
    print(f"KM S({h:>2}) {km[max(k for k in km if k <= h)]:.3f} "
          f"| weibull {math.exp(-((h / scale) ** beta)):.3f}")
p3 = 1 - math.exp(-((21 / scale) ** beta) + ((18 / scale) ** beta))

Three things in the output deserve comment.

The person-time rate is 34% above the naive rate, and the gap is entirely due to the 90 early exits. The script does not print the denominator decomposition, but it can be reconstructed: 22 failures contribute about 271 device-months, the 90 early exits contribute about 956, and the 128 survivors contribute 3,072.

The Weibull and Kaplan-Meier survival estimates agree to three decimal places inside the 24-month window. That agreement is a check on the fit, not evidence that the extrapolation is correct. The `residual_life` function will happily return 60 months for an 18-month-old pump; the data stop at 24.

The three-month failure probability of 0.022 is the decision-relevant number. It is also the number that can be validated prospectively. A pilot that measures whether the 18-month-old pumps with a 2.2% predicted risk fail at about that rate is testing the model; a pilot that measures whether the 60-month RUL is right would have to wait five years.

### 7.1 Adapting the script to a real extract

The script takes three arrays: event times, censoring times, and the window length. A real extract replaces them with three joins, and the joins are where most errors enter.

| Parameter | Where it comes from | What to check before running |
|---|---|---|
| `FAILURES` | Work orders with a failure code, deduplicated by device | One row per event, not per work-order line |
| `CENSORED` | Retirement, transfer, and trade-in dates | Reason recorded, not only the date |
| `ADMIN` | Devices active on the last day of the window | Reconciled against the telemetry inventory |
| `WINDOW` | The analysis period | Same period for every device |
| `FLEET` | Asset register at the start, plus additions | Additions enter with their install date |

Two checks are worth building into the extract. The first is a reconciliation count: devices in the CMMS, devices in the finance register, and devices seen by telemetry should agree within a few percent. The second is an event audit: a random sample of 20 work orders should be re-read against the event definition, and the disagreement rate reported. A 10% disagreement rate is normal; a 30% rate means the event definition has not been operationalised.

The model itself is deliberately simple. The point of the script is not the Weibull fit; it is the visible arithmetic of exposure, censoring, and extrapolation. A production system will use a richer model and the same discipline.

## 8. Data, standards, and regulatory context

Predictive maintenance for medical equipment sits at the intersection of three regulatory regimes that were not written for it: device safety, maintenance practice, and cybersecurity. The data scientist needs to know which requirements are binding and which are convention.

### 8.1 Maintenance practice

In the United States, hospitals that participate in Medicare must meet the Conditions of Participation for the physical environment, including requirements for equipment maintenance (42 CFR 482.41). Accredited hospitals additionally follow The Joint Commission's Environment of Care standards. Standard EC.02.04.01 requires the organisation to identify maintenance activities and frequencies for all medical equipment, either per manufacturer recommendations or through an alternative equipment maintenance (AEM) programme. The AEM programme must be justified in writing, must not reduce safety, and is restricted for certain equipment, including imaging and radiologic equipment, medical lasers, and new equipment with insufficient maintenance history.

This is binding for the organisations it covers, and it constrains what a model can change. A model that extends a maintenance interval is an AEM decision. It needs a written justification that covers the equipment's use, the consequences of failure, backup availability, incident history, and the manufacturer's recommendations. A retrospective analysis of 22 failures is unlikely to satisfy that standard on its own.

In the United Kingdom, the MHRA's *Managing Medical Devices* guidance (January 2021) sets out expectations for acquisition, maintenance, repair, and disposal. It is guidance rather than statute, and it is the reference document that National Health Service organisations use to write their maintenance policies. It expects a documented maintenance policy, validated maintenance records, and annual audit.

ANSI/AAMI EQ56:2024, *Standard for a medical equipment management program*, is a voluntary consensus standard. It is not law, but it is frequently cited by accreditors and by hospitals writing their own policies. Its companion, ANSI/AAMI EQ89:2015 (reaffirmed 2023), covers maintenance strategies and procedures.

### 8.2 Device safety and quality standards

The standards below are published by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC).

| Standard | Title | Status | What it constrains |
|---|---|---|---|
| IEC 60601-1 | Medical electrical equipment, general requirements for basic safety and essential performance | Voluntary, referenced by regulators | Design and testing of the device |
| IEC 62353:2014 | Recurrent test and test after repair of medical electrical equipment | Voluntary | Electrical safety testing at maintenance |
| IEC 62304 | Medical device software, software life cycle processes | Voluntary, harmonised in some jurisdictions | Software changes, including model updates on device |
| EU Medical Device Regulation 2017/745 | European Union (EU) device regulation | Binding in the EU | Risk management and post-market surveillance |
| ISO 14971:2019 | Application of risk management to medical devices | Voluntary, required for CE marking and US premarket | Risk file for any change in maintenance |
| ISO 13485:2016 | Medical devices, quality management systems | Voluntary, incorporated into US QMSR | Change control and records |
| IEC 81001-5-1:2021 | Health software and health IT systems safety, effectiveness and security, Part 5-1: Security, activities in the product life cycle | Voluntary; referenced in EU guidance | Secure development and maintenance of the telemetry software |
| ISO 55000:2024 / ISO 55001:2024 | Asset management, vocabulary and requirements | Voluntary | The asset management system around the programme |
| ISO 17359:2018 | Condition monitoring and diagnostics of machines, general guidelines | Voluntary | How a condition monitoring programme is set up |
| ISO 20816-1:2016 | Mechanical vibration, measurement and evaluation, general guidelines | Voluntary | Vibration severity thresholds used as indicators |

Two of these are commonly over-claimed. ISO 17359 and the ISO 13374 series were written for industrial machines, not for devices in clinical use. They describe how to set up a condition monitoring programme, how to acquire and process data, and how to present results. They do not define acceptable alert performance for a ventilator, and they do not address censoring, informative maintenance, or the clinical cost of a false alarm. A programme that says it is "ISO 17359 compliant" has said something about its data architecture, not about its predictions.

IEC 62304 matters more than it appears. If the model runs on the device, or if a firmware update changes the telemetry, the change is a software change under the device's quality system. If the model runs in the cloud and only reads telemetry, the device's regulatory status may be unaffected, but the interface and the data flow still need to be in the risk file. The boundary is drawn by the manufacturer's regulatory team, not by the data team.

### 8.3 Cybersecurity

Connected device telemetry is a network attack surface. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) premarket cybersecurity guidance, *Cybersecurity in Medical Devices: Quality Management System Considerations and Content of Premarket Submissions*, was revised on 3 February 2026 and supersedes the September 2023 and June 2025 versions. It describes a secure product development framework, security risk management distinct from ISO 14971 safety risk management, a software bill of materials (SBOM), security architecture views, and premarket testing. For devices that meet the statutory definition of a cyber device, Section 524B of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act) makes these submissions a legal requirement.

IEC 81001-5-1:2021 is the health-software security life-cycle standard. It sets out processes for security risk management, configuration management, and problem resolution across the product life cycle, and it maps to IEC 62443-4-1. It is voluntary in most jurisdictions but is harmonised under the EU Medical Device Regulation.

The architecture that most hospital programmes converge on is a read-only outbound telemetry path, a segmented device virtual local area network (VLAN), an on-premise gateway that aggregates and de-identifies, and a cloud analytics layer that receives operational data only. The claim "no patient data leaves the device" is a design goal, not a guarantee: infusion pump logs can contain dose and drug identifiers, and imaging logs can contain patient identifiers in study descriptions. The data flow should be inspected, not assumed.

### 8.4 The maintenance record as a dataset

The CMMS is the primary source for events, and it was designed for work-order administration rather than for analysis. Its fields are a useful starting point and a poor endpoint.

| Field | Typical content | Analytical problem |
|---|---|---|
| Work order identifier | Sequential number | One failure can generate several orders |
| Asset identifier | Local asset tag | Not always linked to UDI |
| Open and close time | Timestamps | Close time is administrative, not repair time |
| Fault code | Local code set or free text | No controlled terminology |
| Labour hours | Technician entry | Rounded, often to the half hour |
| Parts used | Free text or stock code | Cannibalised parts are not recorded |
| Status | Open, closed, cancelled | "Closed" can mean "no fault found" |
| Downtime | Rarely recorded | Usually derived, with error |

Two harmonisation problems follow. The first is the fault code. There is no LOINC or SNOMED CT equivalent for maintenance faults, and every CMMS uses a local taxonomy. Mapping local codes to failure modes is a manual exercise that has to be repeated at every site. The second is the unique device identification. The UDI is the key that links a device to its model, its recalls, and its manufacturer's service history, but it is often absent from the work order and present only in the asset master, if at all.

### 8.5 Binding requirement versus convention

| Requirement | Source | Status | What it constrains |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equipment maintenance per manufacturer or AEM | 42 CFR 482.41; Joint Commission EC.02.04.01 | Binding for covered organisations | Intervals and documentation |
| Medical device reporting | 21 CFR 803 | Binding for manufacturers and importers | Reporting of deaths and serious injuries |
| Premarket cybersecurity for cyber devices | FD&C Act Section 524B; FDA guidance 2026 | Binding in the US | Telemetry interfaces and SBOM |
| CE marking and post-market surveillance | EU MDR 2017/745 | Binding in the EU | Risk management and vigilance |
| Quality management system | ISO 13485:2016; US Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR) | Voluntary, incorporated into law in some markets | Change control and records |
| Asset management system | ISO 55001:2024 | Voluntary | Governance, not device safety |
| Condition monitoring programme | ISO 17359:2018 | Voluntary | Data architecture, not clinical performance |
| Managing medical devices | MHRA guidance, 2021 | Guidance | Expected practice in the UK |

The distinction matters because a vendor deck often mixes them. "ISO 17359 compliant" is a statement about a condition monitoring programme, not a certification of clinical validity. "FDA cleared" on a device is not a statement about the maintenance model that reads its telemetry. "CE marked" applies to the device and its intended purpose, not to a hospital's local analytics.

### 8.6 What the regulatory framework does not cover

No standard in this list specifies how to validate a remaining-useful-life model. There is no accepted performance metric for an alert rule, no required calibration level, and no agreed definition of a "prevented failure". The manufacturer's instructions for use specify maintenance intervals and tests; they do not specify a prediction model's acceptable false-alarm rate.

That gap is the space the data scientist occupies, and it is why the evidence has to be built from first principles: a defined event, a defensible denominator, a validated indicator, a prospective evaluation, and an honest statement of what the data can support. The regulatory framework will ask whether the maintenance change is safe. The analysis has to answer that question in the language of the maintenance programme, not only in the language of the model.

## 9. Industry practice and case evidence

### 9.1 What is documented

**Remote monitoring is widespread; validated prediction is not.** Most major device manufacturers offer some form of connected service: remote diagnostics, automated consumable replenishment, firmware updates, and asset dashboards. These are documented commercial offerings. The claim that the same systems produce validated remaining-useful-life estimates is harder to verify. Public evidence of prospective, alert-level validation in clinical fleets is thin, and most of what exists is vendor material rather than peer-reviewed evaluation.

**The Philips Respironics recall, 2021.** In June 2021, Philips Respironics recalled certain continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP), and mechanical ventilator devices because the polyester-based polyurethane sound-abatement foam could degrade into particles and off-gas volatile organic compounds. The FDA classified the actions as Class 1 recalls. The affected population was approximately 15 million devices worldwide, including about 10.6 million positive airway pressure devices and 148,729 ventilators in the United States. A consent decree followed in April 2024. This case is instructive for predictive maintenance because the failure was design-induced and not observable in operating telemetry. It was detected through complaint and quality data, and it was addressed by replacement, not by prediction. A programme that claims to predict all failures would have missed it.

**The NASA C-MAPSS benchmark.** The Commercial Modular Aero-Propulsion System Simulation dataset, published by Saxena and colleagues in 2008, is the standard public benchmark for remaining-useful-life estimation. It contains run-to-failure trajectories for turbofan engines under one or two operating conditions and one or two fault modes, with a scoring function that penalises late predictions more heavily than early ones. It is useful for comparing algorithms and misleading as a template for clinical equipment: the engines run to failure under controlled conditions, the failure threshold is known, and there is no maintenance intervention, no censoring, and no clinician in the loop.

**ECRI's hazard list.** ECRI's Top 10 Health Technology Hazards for 2026 includes cybersecurity risks from legacy medical devices and unpreparedness for a prolonged loss of access to electronic systems. Both are maintenance-adjacent: an unpatched device is a maintenance liability, and a fleet that depends on a cloud analytics service inherits the availability risk of that service. The list is a consensus judgement by a patient-safety organisation, not a statistical estimate.

### 9.2 What is inference

The following statements are reasonable inferences from the documented material, but they are not themselves documented findings.

Most hospital "predictive maintenance" programmes in 2026 are threshold rules on a small number of telemetry channels, plus a work-order analytics layer. The machine-learning component is often a classifier for service-call volume rather than a survival model for failure. This is an inference from the shape of the vendor offerings and from the data that hospitals actually hold, not a survey result.

The economic case is strongest for device classes with high utilisation, high failure cost, and a long P-F interval: ventilators, dialysis machines, and high-end imaging. It is weakest for low-cost, high-count devices with short P-F intervals and cheap replacement, such as small infusion pumps in a fleet with a large spares pool.

The organisational constraint is usually larger than the modelling constraint. A department that cannot act on an alert within the lead time gets no value from a better model. The failure of predictive programmes is more often a service-design failure than an algorithm failure.

### 9.3 What the evidence base lacks

There is no large randomised trial of predictive maintenance in hospitals that this topic can cite. The evidence for the underlying methods comes from aviation, wind energy, manufacturing, and rail, where run-to-failure data are abundant and the cost of a false alarm is measured in money rather than patient harm. Transferring those methods to clinical equipment requires re-establishing the event definition, the censoring structure, and the cost model in the clinical setting. The methods transfer; the parameters do not.

### 9.4 How a hospital actually buys this

The purchase is not a data-science decision, and the path it takes shapes what can be evaluated later.

Most programmes enter through one of three routes. A capital purchase puts the platform in the capital plan, where it competes with scanners and beds. A service contract attaches the analytics to an existing maintenance contract with the original equipment manufacturer (OEM). A subscription is operating expenditure, which is easier to approve in-year and harder to cancel once the fleet depends on it. The route determines the contract length, the data rights, and the exit cost.

The buyer is rarely the user. Clinical engineering specifies, procurement negotiates, information security reviews the telemetry path, and finance signs. Each function asks a different question, and a proposal that answers only the clinical question stalls in one of the others.

| Stakeholder | Question | Evidence they ask for |
|---|---|---|
| Clinical engineering | Does it reduce failures | Alert precision and coverage |
| Procurement | What is the total cost | Five-year cost, exit terms |
| Information security | What data leave the network | Data flow, SBOM, penetration test |
| Finance | Is it operating or capital | Budget line and payback |
| Quality and safety | Is the maintenance change defensible | Written justification and audit |

The data-sharing agreement is the item most often negotiated last and most often regretted. If the hospital cannot export its own telemetry, it cannot change vendor, cannot reproduce the model, and cannot audit the alerts. A programme that cannot be audited cannot be defended to an accreditor after an incident.

### 9.5 The evidence hierarchy for a maintenance claim

Claims about a programme are not equally demanding. The table gives the minimum evidence for each and the stronger evidence that would make the claim robust.

| Claim | Minimum evidence | Stronger evidence |
|---|---|---|
| The indicator detects degradation | Bench test and a negative control | Prospective cohort with blinded assessment |
| The model predicts failure | Temporal validation with alert-level metrics | Prospective shadow mode |
| The programme prevents failure | Before-after with a control site | Stepped-wedge or randomised rollout |
| The programme reduces cost | Measured costs with a sensitivity table | Audited total cost of ownership |
| The programme improves safety | Incident reporting and review | Controlled comparison of harm events |

Most vendor claims sit at the first row and are presented as if they were at the third. The gap between "the indicator moves before failure" and "the programme prevents failure" is the gap that a pilot is designed to close, and it is usually wider than the vendor expects.

## 10. Failure modes and red flags

| Failure | Early warning sign | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Service calls used as failures | Model predicts ward and shift | Label reflects human response | Alerts track staffing, not degradation | Define failure operationally |
| Naive denominator | Rate changes when the fleet turns over | Devices counted for time they were absent | Understated rate, underfunded programme | Person-time exposure |
| Informative censoring | Retired units had higher failure rates | Retirement is health-related | Survival overstated, RUL too long | Test leavers versus stayers |
| Left-truncation ignored | Fleet rolled over mid-window | Old devices enter already aged | Length-biased sample | Condition on entry age |
| Interval-censoring ignored | Failures recorded at PM visits | Event time is an interval | Biased hazard shape | Interval-censored methods |
| Calendar age as the clock | ICU and ward pumps pooled | Utilisation differs tenfold | Wrong device ranked highest | Use cycles or hours |
| Indicator confounded by consumable | Alerts follow a supplier change | Tubing or reagent affects the signal | False alarms and lost trust | Record lot and brand |
| Indicator confounded by firmware | Step change after update | Measurement changed, not the device | Spurious alert wave | Recompute reference per version |
| Random train-test split | High validation AUC | Same device in both sets | Performance not reproducible | Split by time, cohort, or site |
| AUC used as the decision metric | No alert volume estimate | Discrimination is not calibration | Unserviceable alert queue | Alert-level metrics |
| Threshold set statistically | 50% cut-off on a score | Ignores asymmetric costs | Over- or under-servicing | Cost-based threshold |
| Capacity ignored | Alerts exceed technician hours | Queue grows, lead time shrinks | Missed failures despite alerts | Triage and capacity plan |
| Fixed platform cost ignored | Small fleet looks attractive | Cost does not scale with fleet | Negative net value | Scale the business case |
| Baseline maintenance overstated | Four visits assumed without evidence | Comparison against a straw man | Wrong adoption decision | Audit the actual programme |
| Intervention changes the data | Failure rate falls after rollout | Prevented events leave the record | Model looks worse or better than it is | Shadow mode or control group |
| RUL extrapolated beyond data | 60-month RUL from 24 months | Parametric curve continues | Deferred replacement, early failure | Report only validated horizon |
| Telemetry gap treated as healthy | Device-days missing from the feed | Absence of data read as absence of risk | Blind spot grows silently | Report completeness; impute or exclude |
| Threshold tuned on the evaluation data | Perfect precision in the pilot | Same data used to choose and to test | Performance collapses at launch | Hold out a validation period or site |
| Device swap resets the episode | Failure attached to a new asset tag | The faulty unit leaves the record | Event count understated | Track the physical unit, not the tag |
| Recall not linked to the fleet | Field safety notice handled separately | Asset master lacks UDI | Affected devices stay in service | Join recalls on UDI-DI |

Five red flags deserve emphasis.

**A validation that reports only discrimination.** An AUC, an accuracy, or an F1 score answers a ranking question. The programme needs alert volume, precision at the operating threshold, lead-time distribution, and false alarms per device-year. A vendor deck without those four numbers has not described a deployable system.

**A business case with one scenario.** The worked example flips from a USD 84,310 saving to a USD 2,090 loss when the incumbent programme is two visits per year at USD 110 instead of four at USD 145. A single-scenario case is a sales document. A sensitivity table with the baseline maintenance cost, the fleet size, and the false-alarm rate is the minimum.

**A claim that the model predicts "all failures".** Failure modes differ in observability. Design-induced failures, supplier defects, and consumable interactions are not in the telemetry. A programme should state which failure modes it covers and which it does not, and the uncovered modes should remain on the scheduled or reactive programme.

**A pilot judged on the mean remaining useful life.** The mean RUL is dominated by the long-lived devices and is not the quantity the programme uses. The pilot should be judged on alerts, coverage, and lead time. A mean RUL that is "close" to the observed failure times can coexist with a model that never raises an actionable alert.

**A model with no named owner.** A model without an owner does not get monitored, recalibrated, or retired. The owner is a role in the maintenance organisation, not the vendor's support desk, and the ownership should be written into the model card.

When one of these signs appears, the first diagnostic is usually the denominator or the data pipeline, not the model. The table is a triage order.

| Symptom | First check | Likely cause |
|---|---|---|
| Alert rate doubles overnight | Telemetry completeness by device | Firmware update or gateway outage |
| Failure rate falls after rollout | Share of repairs with a work order | Informal repairs leaving the record |
| Precision falls gradually | Device model mix | New pump model in the fleet |
| RUL rises without a maintenance change | Censoring pattern | Trade-ins of the oldest units |
| All alerts concentrated on one campus | Data pipeline by site | Gateway configuration |
| No alerts for a known failure mode | Indicator coverage | The mode was never observable |

The order matters because teams reach for the model first. A model is usually the most expensive thing to change and the least likely to be the cause.

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why it is needed | What breaks it | How to test |
|---|---|---|---|
| The event definition captures clinical failure | The label is the target | Repairs without work orders | Audit a sample of repairs |
| Censoring is independent of failure | Standard survival estimators | Retiring unreliable units first | Compare leavers and stayers |
| The fleet register is complete | Denominator | Transfers and disposals unrecorded | Reconcile CMMS, finance, telemetry |
| The time scale matches the mechanism | Hazard estimation | Utilisation varies by site | Compare models on each clock |
| The hazard is homogeneous within a stratum | Pooled estimate | Mixed models or duty cycles | Stratify and test |
| The parametric form is adequate | Extrapolation and RUL | Wear-out with a different shape | Compare with Kaplan-Meier |
| The indicator measures degradation | Alert signal | Consumable, cleaning, firmware | Challenge with negative controls |
| The training population matches deployment | Transportability | New model, new ward, new protocol | Prospective validation |
| Alert precision measured prospectively holds | Threshold setting | Case mix and season change | Monthly monitoring |
| The cost of a failure is known | Threshold | Harm is not monetised | Report harm separately |
| The cost of a false alarm is known | Threshold | Alarm fatigue is not costed | Include staff time and trust |
| Technician capacity is available | Response design | Night and weekend cover | Map capacity by shift |
| The baseline maintenance cost is measured | Business case | Assumed intervals and prices | Audit invoices and work orders |
| The intervention does not change the outcome process | Evaluation | Servicing prevents recorded failures | Shadow mode or control |
| The model transfers across firmware versions | Deployment | Indicator distribution shifts | Re-validate after each update |
| The threshold remains appropriate over time | Operation | Costs or capacity change | Review annually and after any incident |
| Service capacity is constant across shifts | Response design | Night and weekend cover differs | Map capacity by shift and day |
| Telemetry completeness is unrelated to failure risk | Coverage | Poorly networked sites are also hard to service | Compare completeness by site and outcome |

The limits are as important as the assumptions.

The method estimates the hazard of the events it is given. It cannot distinguish a device that failed from a device that was reported, and it cannot recover failures that were repaired without a record. In a department where 30% of repairs are informal, the event table is a 70% sample with unknown selection.

The cost model monetises clinical harm only through a proxy. A ventilator failure in an intensive care unit has a cost that is not the repair invoice. The threshold should be set with the clinical risk owner, and the harm outcome should be reported separately rather than folded into a dollar figure.

The analysis is retrospective. It can show that an indicator was associated with failure in the past. It cannot show that acting on the indicator prevents the failure, because the counterfactual is missing. That is what the pilot is for.

### 11.1 The scope of validity

The model card should state the boundary in the same terms that the analysis used. A boundary written as a marketing sentence is not a boundary.

| Dimension | Valid for | Not valid for |
|---|---|---|
| Device class | Infusion pumps of the modelled families | Ventilators, analysers, imaging |
| Age | 0 to 24 months observed | Beyond 24 months without revalidation |
| Setting | Four campuses with the same consumable supply | A new site with a different tubing brand |
| Utilisation | Ward and intensive care duty cycles | Transport and home care |
| Firmware | Versions in the training window | Any later version until revalidated |
| Maintenance policy | The current scheduled programme | A future AEM change with different intervals |

A model that is valid for the table's middle column and deployed outside it is not a model with a limitation. It is a model used outside its evidence, which is a different and more serious category of error.

## 12. Exercises

### Exercise 1. Event definition (application, 25 minutes)

**Deliverable:** a one-page event definition for infusion pumps in a 300-bed hospital, covering what counts as a failure, what is excluded, and how each excluded category is recorded.

**Acceptance criteria:** the definition distinguishes failure, service call, work order, and planned maintenance; each exclusion has a recording rule; a technician can apply it without asking a question.

**Time:** 25 minutes.

### Exercise 2. Failure-mode register (application, 40 minutes)

**Deliverable:** a failure-mode register for ventilators with at least eight modes, each with a mechanism, an observable proxy, a P-F interval, and a criticality rating.

**Acceptance criteria:** at least two modes are not observable in telemetry and are labelled as such; the P-F interval is justified, not asserted; criticality uses severity as well as frequency.

**Time:** 40 minutes.

### Exercise 3. Person-time calculation (calculation, 30 minutes)

**Deliverable:** for a fleet of 80 pumps over 18 months with 12 failures, 20 early exits at a mean of 7 months, and 48 survivors, compute the naive rate, the person-time rate, and the percentage understatement.

**Acceptance criteria:** the arithmetic is shown; the exposure is in device-years; the confidence interval is reported; the direction of the bias is explained.

**Time:** 30 minutes.

### Exercise 4. Censoring diagnosis (critique, 35 minutes)

**Deliverable:** a short analysis of a fleet where the 15 units retired during the year had a pre-retirement failure rate of 14 per 100 device-years and the units that stayed had 5 per 100 device-years. State whether the censoring is informative, what direction the bias takes, and what you would do.

**Acceptance criteria:** the mechanism is named; the direction of bias in the survival curve and the RUL is stated; at least two remedies are evaluated.

**Time:** 35 minutes.

### Exercise 5. Alert metrics (calculation, 30 minutes)

**Deliverable:** from a log of 60 alerts, 24 confirmed failures, and 18 failures with a preceding alert, compute alert precision, failure recall, and false alarms per device-year for a 200-device fleet.

**Acceptance criteria:** the denominators are stated for each metric; the difference between "alerts that precede a failure" and "failures with a preceding alert" is explained; the result is interpreted for a service manager.

**Time:** 30 minutes.

### Exercise 6. Threshold and capacity (design, 45 minutes)

**Deliverable:** a threshold policy for a 240-pump fleet with an inspection cost of USD 260, a failure cost of USD 2,850, and a disruption cost of USD 180 (all 2026 prices, fully loaded), and 30 technician hours per week. State the break-even probability, the expected alert volume, and what you would do when volume exceeds capacity.

**Acceptance criteria:** the break-even calculation is shown; capacity is converted to alerts per week; the triage rule is explicit; the residual risk is stated.

**Time:** 45 minutes.

### Exercise 7. Business case critique (critique, 40 minutes)

**Deliverable:** a critique of a vendor business case that claims a 70% reduction in failures, a 12-month payback, and no change to technician headcount. Identify at least five missing items and state how each would change the conclusion.

**Acceptance criteria:** at least one missing item is a denominator problem and one is a cost that the vendor does not bear; each item is linked to a direction of bias; a revised sensitivity table is sketched.

**Time:** 40 minutes.

### Exercise 8. Evaluation design (design, 50 minutes)

**Deliverable:** a design for evaluating a predictive maintenance rollout across four campuses over 18 months, including the unit of assignment, the primary outcome, the sample size logic, and the handling of the intervention's effect on the recorded failure rate.

**Acceptance criteria:** the design is not a simple before-after; the primary outcome is measurable; power is discussed with the expected event count; a shadow-mode or control group is included.

**Time:** 50 minutes.

### Exercise 9. Four quantities (recall, 20 minutes)

**Deliverable:** for a programme with 120 alerts per year, 54 true alerts, 38 failures, and 29 failures with a preceding alert, compute alert volume per 100 device-years for a 300-device fleet, precision, and coverage, and state which quantity is missing.

**Acceptance criteria:** each calculation has a stated denominator; the missing quantity is identified as lead time; the interpretation is written for a service manager in three sentences.

**Time:** 20 minutes.

### Submission standard

An event definition must be operational enough for a technician to apply. A rate must state its denominator and its unit. An alert analysis must report precision and recall with their denominators, not only a discrimination metric. A business case must include a sensitivity table with the baseline maintenance cost and the fleet size. An evaluation design must address the fact that the intervention changes the outcome it is measuring.

## 13. Self-check answers

**1. What is the difference between a service call and a failure?** A service call is a request for service, generated by a person in response to a symptom. A failure is a loss of the device's clinical function, confirmed by inspection or repair. A call can occur without a failure, and a failure can occur without a call.

**2. Compute the person-time rate for 22 failures over 358.2 device-years.** 22 / 358.2 = 0.0614, or 6.14 per 100 device-years.

**3. Why is the naive rate lower than the person-time rate?** The naive rate counts every device for the whole window, including devices that left early. The denominator is too large, so the rate is too low. In the worked example the understatement is 34%.

**4. What is right-censoring?** Observation ends before the event of interest. The device contributes exposure up to the censoring time and is not counted as a failure.

**5. What is informative censoring, and why does it matter?** Censoring is informative when the reason for leaving the fleet is related to the risk of failure, for example retiring the least reliable units first. Standard survival estimators assume independence, so informative censoring biases the curve, usually upward, and lengthens the estimated RUL.

**6. What does a Weibull shape of 1.494 mean?** The hazard rises with time, which is consistent with wear-out. A shape of 1 would be a constant hazard; below 1 would be infant mortality or a heterogeneous fleet.

**7. Why can the median residual life of 60.3 months not be used as a replacement date?** No device in the data has been observed beyond 24 months, and the Kaplan-Meier median is not reached. The 60-month figure is a parametric extrapolation, not an observation.

**8. What is the three-month failure probability for a pump that has survived 18 months, and how many failures does it imply for 240 pumps?** 2.2%, which implies about 5.3 expected failures in the next quarter across 240 pumps.

**9. Compute the break-even probability for an inspection cost of USD 260 and a failure cost of USD 2,850.** 260 / 2,850 = 0.091, or 9.1%.

**10. What happens to the break-even probability when a disruption cost of USD 180 is added?** (260 + 180) / 2,850 = 0.154, or 15.4%.

**11. Why is an AUC of 0.85 insufficient for a maintenance decision?** It measures ranking, not calibration or alert volume. The programme needs to know how many alerts it will receive, how many are true, and how early they arrive.

**12. Why must the train-test split be temporal or by site?** A random split puts the same device in both sets, so the model can memorise device-specific patterns. A temporal or site split tests performance on devices and conditions the model has not seen.

**13. What is the P-F interval, and why does it set the prediction horizon?** It is the time between the point at which a failure becomes detectable and the point of functional failure. An alert must arrive inside that interval to be actionable, so the horizon cannot be longer than the interval for the mode being predicted.

**14. Why does the business case flip at two visits per year at USD 110?** The scheduled programme costs USD 84,150, below the predictive programme's USD 86,240. The saving in the base case comes from replacing an expensive scheduled programme, not from prediction being intrinsically cheaper.

**15. What is the difference between alert precision and failure recall?** Alert precision is the share of alerts that are true. Failure recall is the share of failures that had at least one preceding alert. They have different denominators and can move in opposite directions when the threshold changes.

**16. Why is the fleet register a denominator problem?** The rate needs the number of devices at risk. If the register disagrees with the finance record and the telemetry inventory, the fleet size is uncertain, and every rate inherits that uncertainty.

**17. What is lead time, and why report a distribution?** Lead time is the interval between an alert and the failure it predicts. A mean hides the alerts that arrive too late to action, which are the ones that matter for service design.

**18. Why is a health score not a failure probability?** A score is an uncalibrated index with an arbitrary scale. A probability has a defined event, a time horizon, and a calibration claim. A threshold on a score cannot be compared with a cost.

**19. What is the difference between coverage and precision?** Precision is the share of alerts that are true. Coverage is the share of failures with a preceding alert. A model can have high precision and low coverage by alerting only on the most obvious cases.

**20. Compute the expected failures for 240 pumps over three months at a conditional failure probability of 2.2%.** 240 x 0.022 = 5.28, or about 5.3 expected failures.

**21. What is a negative control in indicator validation?** A change that should move the indicator but should not change the failure risk, such as a consumable lot change or a cleaning procedure. If the indicator responds to the control, it is measuring the control, not degradation.

**22. Why can a before-after comparison mislead?** The post period differs from the pre period in fleet age, season, case mix, and concurrent recalls. Any of these can produce a change that is attributed to the programme.

**23. What is the P-F interval for battery failure, and what does it imply for the alert policy?** The P-F interval is short, often minutes to hours, because capacity fade is invisible until the device is asked to run on battery. The alert policy has to be a scheduled battery test, not a prediction from telemetry.

**24. Why does the intervention change the data?** Servicing an alerted device prevents the failure, so the failure does not enter the record. The observed failure rate falls, and the model's measured precision changes, even if the model has not changed.

**Rubric for Exercise 7 (business case critique).** A strong critique identifies the denominator, the omitted false-alarm cost, the fixed platform cost against fleet size, the baseline maintenance assumption, and the missing evaluation design, and links each to a direction of bias. A weak critique lists generic concerns without saying which way the number moves.

**Rubric for Exercise 6 (threshold and capacity).** A strong answer computes the break-even probability, converts the expected alert volume into technician hours, compares it with the 30 hours available, and specifies a triage rule with a stated residual risk. A weak answer sets a threshold from a statistical criterion and does not check whether the team can service the alerts.

**Rubric for Exercise 8 (evaluation design).** A strong answer uses a stepped-wedge or parallel design, names the primary outcome, discusses power with the expected event count, and includes a shadow mode or control group to handle the intervention's effect on the recorded failure rate. A weak answer proposes a before-after comparison and reports a percentage change.

## 14. Interview questions

**1. How would you define a failure for an infusion pump fleet?**
A strong answer gives an operational definition, distinguishes failure from service call and work order, and states what is excluded. A weak answer says "when the pump breaks".

**2. Why is the naive failure rate usually wrong?**
A strong answer explains person-time, turnover, and the direction of the bias. A weak answer says the sample is small.

**3. What is censoring, and when is it informative?**
A strong answer defines right, left, and interval censoring and gives the retirement example. A weak answer says censoring is missing data.

**4. How would you estimate remaining useful life with 22 events?**
A strong answer fits a nonparametric curve first, uses a parametric model only inside the observed range, reports intervals, and states the extrapolation limit. A weak answer names a deep learning architecture.

**5. What is a health indicator, and how would you validate one?**
A strong answer defines it as a scalar with a direction and tests it against negative controls such as consumable changes and firmware updates. A weak answer says it is a feature that correlates with failure.

**6. Why is AUC not enough?**
A strong answer explains calibration, alert volume, precision at the operating point, and lead time. A weak answer says AUC is a good metric.

**7. How do you set an alert threshold?**
A strong answer uses the expected cost of acting versus waiting, includes disruption cost, and checks capacity. A weak answer picks 0.5 or the Youden index.

**8. What would you do if the alert volume exceeds technician capacity?**
A strong answer triages by criticality and lead time, adds a confirmation step, or expands capacity, and states the residual risk. A weak answer raises the threshold without analysis.

**9. How would you evaluate whether the programme worked?**
A strong answer uses a stepped-wedge or parallel design, a pre-specified outcome, and a shadow mode or control group. A weak answer compares the failure count before and after.

**10. Why can a predictive programme look worse after it succeeds?**
A strong answer explains that prevented failures leave the record, so the observed failure rate falls and the alert precision changes. A weak answer does not see the feedback loop.

**11. What regulatory constraint applies to changing a maintenance interval?**
A strong answer names the AEM programme, the written justification, and the equipment excluded from AEM. A weak answer says maintenance is an engineering decision.

**12. What data would you ask a vendor for before believing a predictive maintenance claim?**
A strong answer asks for the event definition, the exposure and censoring, the prospective alert-level validation, the lead-time distribution, and the cost model. A weak answer asks for the model accuracy.

**13. What is the difference between availability and uptime?**
A strong answer says uptime is a property of a device and availability is a property of demand: a ward with four working pumps and six simultaneous infusions has low availability and high uptime. A weak answer treats them as synonyms.

**14. How do you handle a new device model introduced mid-window?**
A strong answer treats it as a new stratum with its own baseline, checks whether the indicator distribution transfers, and does not pool it with the old model until there is enough exposure. A weak answer adds it to the fleet and lets the model average.

**15. What would you do if telemetry completeness were 80%?**
A strong answer reports completeness by site and device, tests whether missingness is related to failure, and either excludes the affected exposure or treats it as a separate stratum. A weak answer imputes the missing values and continues.

**16. How would you explain a 60-month RUL to a capital committee?**
A strong answer says it is a model extrapolation beyond the 24 months observed, gives the validated three-month risk instead, and proposes a review date rather than a replacement date. A weak answer presents the 60 months as a finding.

## 15. Key terms

- **AEM** — alternative equipment maintenance, an accreditor-permitted deviation from manufacturer intervals.
- **Alert precision** — share of alerts that are true.
- **Asset master** — the register of devices, identifiers, and dates.
- **Cannibalisation** — removing a part from one asset to repair another.
- **Censoring** — observation ending before the event.
- **Clinical engineering** — the department responsible for medical equipment.
- **CMMS** — computerised maintenance management system.
- **Condition-based maintenance** — maintenance triggered by a measured condition.
- **Corrective maintenance** — repair after a fault.
- **Criticality** — severity, occurrence, and detectability combined.
- **Degradation path** — the trajectory of a health indicator.
- **Duty cycle** — utilisation relative to design maximum.
- **Edge gateway** — on-premise telemetry aggregator.
- **FMEA** — failure mode and effects analysis.
- **Hazard rate** — instantaneous failure rate given survival.
- **Health indicator** — a scalar that tracks degradation.
- **Informative censoring** — censoring related to failure risk.
- **Interval-censoring** — event known only to fall between two times.
- **Lead time** — time between alert and failure.
- **Left-truncation** — entry into observation after the start of life.
- **MTBF** — mean time between failures, person-time per event.
- **MTTF** — mean time to failure, from the failure-time distribution.
- **P-F interval** — time from detectability to functional failure.
- **Person-time** — total time at risk across units.
- **Planned maintenance** — scheduled inspection or replacement.
- **Predictive maintenance** — maintenance scheduled from a failure forecast.
- **Prognostics** — estimation of remaining life and uncertainty.
- **Remaining useful life** — time until the failure threshold.
- **Right-censoring** — observation ends at the window or exit.
- **Run-to-failure** — repair only after failure.
- **SBOM** — software bill of materials.
- **Service call** — a human request for service.
- **Survival function** — probability of surviving beyond a time.
- **Telemetry** — machine-generated operational data.
- **UDI** — unique device identification.
- **Uptime** — share of time a device is available.
- **Weibull shape** — controls whether hazard rises, falls, or is flat.
- **Work order** — a record of a maintenance task.
