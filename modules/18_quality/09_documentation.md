## 1. Why this topic exists

Documentation is the part of quality that survives people. A model can be rebuilt, a pipeline can
be rerun, and a server can be replaced; what cannot be recovered is the reasoning of the person who
left. Documentation is the mechanism that converts individual knowledge into organisational
knowledge, and its absence is the most common reason that an analysis cannot be maintained, an
audit cannot be answered, or a model cannot be safely changed.

The cost of the gap is not evenly distributed. It falls on the person who inherits the work, the
auditor who cannot conclude, and the organisation that must rebuild knowledge it already paid to
create. None of them was present when the decision was made, which is why the record matters more
than the recollection.

The failure is predictable and it has a shape. A team builds a model, documents it in a repository
README and a slide deck, and moves on. Eighteen months later the model needs a change. The analyst
who built it has left. The README describes the original pipeline, which no longer matches the
code. The slide deck describes the results, not the decisions. The validation report exists, but the
data snapshot it used has been overwritten. The team can see what the model does and cannot
reconstruct why, and the change takes three times as long as it should.

There is a second failure, specific to regulated and commercial contexts. An auditor or a buyer does
not ask "what does the model do?" They ask "show me that you decided to do it this way, that you
verified it, and that the decision is still current." That is a documentation question, and it is
answered by records rather than by explanation. A team can have excellent engineering and poor
records, and it will fail the question.

There is a third failure, which is the most expensive and the least discussed. Documentation written
for engineers is not usable by reviewers, and documentation written for reviewers is not usable by
engineers. The two audiences need different artifacts: the engineer needs a runbook, a schema, and a
decision log; the reviewer needs a summary, a trace, and a statement of limitations. A single
document that tries to serve both is usually too long for the reviewer and too vague for the
engineer. The topic teaches the split.

The three failures share a cause: documentation is treated as a product rather than as a byproduct.
A product is written when someone asks for it, in the format the asker expects, and it is judged on
whether it answers the asker. A byproduct is generated by the work itself, in the format the work
requires, and it accumulates without a project. Teams that generate documentation as a byproduct —
a decision record when a decision is made, a release record when a release happens, a runbook update
when an interface changes — do not experience the eighteen-month reconstruction, because there is
nothing to reconstruct.

The industry evidence is indirect but consistent. The QMSR's incorporation of ISO 13485 makes
document control a regulatory requirement for device manufacturers, and the FDA's inspection
findings repeatedly include inadequate records and procedures. The reproducibility literature shows
that even when code is shared, documentation is often insufficient to run it: in a 2026 scoping
review of repositories linked to prediction-model papers, only about 52 percent had adequate
READMEs and 38 percent specified dependencies. The same pattern appears in clinical AI model cards,
where headings are present and decision-relevant content is not.

The documentation problem is also a hiring problem. Senior analysts evaluate a team partly by the
quality of its documentation, because it determines whether they will spend their first months
learning or reconstructing. A team with good documentation attracts people who want to build; a
team without one attracts people who tolerate chaos, or people who leave. The effect compounds,
which is why documentation is a retention issue as much as a compliance one.

This topic teaches how to write documentation that survives staff turnover and inspection: the
document set, the formats, the ownership, the review cycle, and the test that tells you whether it
works. It builds on topic 06 (the QMS defines what must be documented) and topic 08 (the audit tests
whether it is).

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end of this topic you can:

1. **Distinguish** documents written for engineers from documents written for reviewers, and choose
   the right format for each.
2. **Write** a standard operating procedure with purpose, scope, responsibilities, steps, records,
   and revision history.
3. **Write** a decision record that captures the options considered and the reason for the choice.
4. **Maintain** a model document and a dataset document that stay linked to the artifacts they
   describe.
5. **Operate** a change log that a reviewer can follow without asking a question.
6. **Audit** a documentation set for coverage, currency, and usability, and prioritise the gaps.
7. **Design** a documentation system that survives turnover: ownership, review cadence, and archive.
8. **Test** documentation by giving it to a newcomer and observing where they get stuck.

## 3. The core mental model

Documentation is a set of four layers, each answering a different question.

```text
   decision records   why we chose this        durable, short, dated
        |
   specifications     what it is and how        model doc, dataset doc, schema
        |
   procedures         who does what, when       SOP, work instruction, runbook
        |
   records            what happened             release record, test report, minutes
```

The layers are read in different directions. An engineer reads the specification and the runbook.
A reviewer reads the decision record and the records. An auditor reads all four and checks that
they agree. A newcomer reads the specification first and the decision record when something looks
surprising.

A second model is the reader matrix, which determines what to write and at what length.

| Reader | Question | Document | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| New engineer | How do I run and change this? | Runbook, schema, decision log | Long, detailed |
| Reviewer | Is the claim supported? | Summary, trace, limitations | Short, precise |
| Auditor | Is it controlled and current? | Registers, approvals, records | Complete, indexed |
| Operator | What do I do when it alerts? | Work instruction, escalation | One page |
| Executive | What does it do and what is the risk? | Brief, risk summary | Half a page |
| Future team | Why was it built this way? | Decision records, retrospective | Medium, dated |

A third model is the documentation debt curve. Documentation written at the time of the work costs
little and stays accurate. Documentation written after the work costs more and is already a
reconstruction. Documentation written for an audit costs most and is the least trustworthy, because
it is produced under pressure and its dates are visible.

```text
  cost
   ^                                        *
   |                                   *
   |                          *
   |              *
   |     *
   +--------------------------------------------> time since decision
        at decision   after release   before audit
```

The curve is the argument for writing decision records as decisions are made. A one-page record
written on the day costs twenty minutes; the same record reconstructed a year later costs a day and
is worth less.

A fourth model is the single-source rule. Every fact should have one authoritative location, and
every other document should reference it rather than repeat it. Repetition creates drift: the
README says one threshold, the model card says another, and the reviewer cannot tell which is
current. The rule is not about tidiness; it is about which document wins when they disagree.

The single-source rule has a corollary that teams find harder: the authoritative location is often
not the most convenient one. A threshold may be most conveniently read from the code, most
authoritatively recorded in the change record, and most usefully summarised in the model document.
The rule says the change record is authoritative, the code implements it, and the model document
references it. When the three disagree, the change record wins and the other two are defects.

The layers also have different lifetimes. A decision record is permanent; a runbook is revised
quarterly; a record is retained for a defined period. Treating them as one category of "docs" leads
either to runbooks that are never updated because they are filed with policy, or to decision records
that are edited because they sit next to operational guides.

A note on the relationship between documentation and memory. Documentation does not replace
conversation; it preserves what conversation cannot. A team that documents everything it discusses
wastes time, and a team that documents nothing it decides loses the reasoning. The useful rule is to
document decisions, interfaces, and failures: the three things that are expensive to rediscover and
impossible to infer from the code.

A fifth model is the test. Documentation works when a competent newcomer can complete a defined task
using only the documents. The test is not "does it exist" or "is it long"; it is "can someone who
was not there do the task". Topic 05 applied the same logic to code; this topic applies it to
knowledge.

## 4. Definitions and vocabulary

| Term | Definition | Common misuse | Standard or unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Controlled document | Document with version, approval, and distribution control | Any file on a shared drive | ISO 13485 |
| Record | Evidence that an activity occurred | A document describing an activity | ISO 13485 |
| Standard operating procedure | Documented process with responsibilities and records | A wiki page | ISO 13485 |
| Work instruction | Task-level how-to | An SOP | Practice |
| Runbook | Operational guide for running and recovering a system | A README | Engineering |
| Decision record | Short document capturing a decision, options, and rationale | Meeting minutes | Practice |
| Model document | Description of a model's purpose, data, evaluation, and limits | A slide deck | Model card literature |
| Dataset document | Description of a dataset's motivation, composition, and use | A data dictionary | Datasheet literature |
| Change log | Ordered record of changes with dates and reasons | A commit history | Practice |
| Version history | Record of document versions and approvals | A file name with "final" | Document control |
| Documentation debt | The gap between what is documented and what must be known | "We will document later" | Practice |
| Single source of truth | One authoritative location for a fact | A shared folder | Practice |
| Document lifecycle | Draft, review, approve, effective, obsolete, archive | Ad hoc | Document control |
| Traceability | Linking claims to evidence | A citation | Design control |
| Onboarding document | Guide for a new team member | A README | Practice |
| Retrospective | Structured review of what happened and why | A blame session | Practice |
| Glossary | Defined terms for a project or organisation | A list of acronyms | Practice |
| Style guide | Rules for consistent writing | Personal preference | Practice |
| Archive | Retention of obsolete documents for records | Deletion | Records management |
| ALCOA | Attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original, accurate | A slogan | FDA, MHRA |

Four distinctions carry the topic.

**Documentation debt interest.** The ongoing cost of a documentation gap, paid whenever someone has
to ask a question, search for a record, or reconstruct a decision. It is invisible in a budget and
visible in the time a team takes to change anything.

**Documentation versus knowledge.** Documentation is what is written; knowledge is what the team can
do. A team can have high knowledge and low documentation (the common case after a long-tenured
team), or low knowledge and high documentation (the common case after an acquisition). The first is
fragile to turnover; the second is fragile to novelty, because the documents describe a world that
may no longer exist. The goal is not maximum documentation but sufficient documentation to transfer
the knowledge that matters.

**Document versus record.** A document describes what should happen or what is known; a record is
evidence that something happened. A procedure is a document; a completed checklist is a record. The
distinction matters because document control and record retention have different rules, and because
a team that produces documents but no records has no evidence.

**Living versus archival.** A living document is maintained and current; an archival document is
retained unchanged as a record of a point in time. A model document should be living; a validation
report should be archival. Mixing the two produces either a validation report that is silently
edited or a model document that is frozen at version one.

**Engineer documentation versus reviewer documentation.** Engineer documentation is optimised for
doing the work: commands, schemas, failure modes. Reviewer documentation is optimised for judging
the work: claims, evidence, limitations. The same facts appear in both, organised differently, and
the single-source rule applies to the facts rather than to the prose.

**Documentation debt versus technical debt.** Technical debt is the cost of future work created by a
shortcut in the code. Documentation debt is the cost of future work created by a gap in the record.
They accumulate together and are repaid differently: technical debt is repaid by refactoring,
documentation debt by writing, and the second is harder to schedule because its cost is invisible
until someone leaves.

## 5. The mechanics, step by step

This procedure builds a documentation system for an analytics team and audits an existing one. It
takes three to six weeks to establish and then a small maintenance effort per release.

### Step 0. Name a documentation owner

**Input:** the team structure and the document set.

**Output:** a named owner for the documentation system, with time allocated in their role.

**Rationale:** Documentation without an owner is everyone's second priority. The owner does not
write every document; they maintain the register, chase reviews, and run the newcomer test. Without
that role, the system decays at the first busy quarter.

**Branch point:** if the team is too small for a dedicated owner, assign the role to the person who
would suffer most from its absence, usually the technical lead, and budget two hours per week.

### Step 1. Identify the decisions the documentation must support

**Input:** the product lifecycle, the team structure, and the regulatory context.

**Output:** a list of decisions with the reader and the artifact that must support each.

**Rationale:** Documentation exists to support decisions: to change the model, to approve a release,
to answer an auditor, to onboard a colleague, to investigate an incident. A document that supports
no decision is a candidate for deletion, and a decision with no document is a gap.

**Branch point:** three categories, each with a different obligation.

| Decision | Reader | Minimum artifact |
|---|---|---|
| Change the model safely | Engineer | Specification, runbook, change log |
| Approve a release | Quality | Design inputs, verification, release record |
| Answer an auditor | Auditor | Registers, approvals, records |
| Onboard a colleague | New team member | Specification, glossary, runbook |
| Investigate an incident | Clinical and engineering | Runbook, monitoring data, decision records |

### Step 2. Inventory what exists

**Input:** the repositories, shared drives, wikis, and document management system.

**Output:** a register with document name, type, owner, location, last review, and status.

**Rationale:** The inventory is the baseline. Most teams discover that they have more documents than
they thought and fewer than they need, because the documents are concentrated on the engineering
side and absent on the decision and record side.

**Branch point:** if the same document exists in several places, record all copies and designate one
as authoritative. The others become references or are deleted.

### Step 3. Choose the formats and the level of structure

**Input:** the decision list and the team's size.

**Output:** a small set of templates: decision record, SOP, work instruction, model document,
dataset document, change log entry.

**Rationale:** Structure should be proportional. A five-person team needs templates that fit on one
or two pages; a regulated manufacturer needs controlled documents with approval blocks. A template
that is too heavy will not be used, and an unused template produces no records.

**Branch point:** if the organisation already has a document management system, use its templates
and metadata rather than inventing a parallel system. Duplicate systems are a common source of
version drift.

### Step 4. Write the procedures that describe actual practice

**Input:** the process map and interviews with the people who do the work.

**Output:** procedures with purpose, scope, responsibilities, steps, records, and revision history.

**Rationale:** A procedure written from aspiration will be violated daily. The procedure should
describe the process as it is, with the improvements that the team agrees to adopt, so that
adherence is possible and meaningful.

**Branch point:** if a process varies by product, write the common steps in the procedure and the
variants in work instructions, rather than one procedure with conditional branches that no one can
follow.

### Step 5. Write decision records as decisions are made

**Input:** the decision and the options considered.

**Output:** a one-page record with context, options, decision, rationale, consequences, and date.

**Rationale:** Decision records are the highest-value, lowest-cost documentation an analytics team
can produce. They capture the reasoning that cannot be recovered from code, and they prevent the
same debate from recurring every eighteen months.

**Branch point:** if a decision is reversed, write a new record that supersedes the old one. Do not
edit the old record; the history of the reasoning is the value.

**What a decision record contains.** The format is short enough that there is no excuse for not
writing one.

| Field | Content | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Title and date | Identifies the decision | "Threshold 0.31, 14 March 2026" |
| Context | The situation that required a decision | Alert fatigue at two sites |
| Options | At least two, with their trade-offs | 0.31, 0.38, site-specific |
| Decision | The chosen option | 0.38 for all sites |
| Rationale | Why this option, given the constraints | Sensitivity target and alert volume |
| Consequences | What is accepted, what is deferred | Lower sensitivity; revisit after six months |
| Links | The artifact and the evidence | Release record, validation report |

The consequences field is the one that makes a record useful later. A decision without stated
consequences looks obvious in retrospect, and the next team repeats the analysis to discover what
was already known.

### Step 6. Write the model document

**Input:** the model, its evaluation, and its limitations.

**Output:** a document covering intended use, data, method, evaluation, subgroup performance,
limitations, and monitoring, with version and date.

**Rationale:** The model document is the reviewer's entry point and the engineer's reference. It
should be generated from the same source as the model card where possible, so that the two cannot
drift.

**Branch point:** if the model is a regulated device, the model document is not the label and does
not replace it. The label governs; the document explains.

### Step 7. Write the dataset document

**Input:** the data source, the collection process, and the preprocessing.

**Output:** a datasheet-style document covering motivation, composition, collection, preprocessing,
uses, distribution, and maintenance.

**Rationale:** The dataset document is the artifact that lets a future team understand what the model
learned from and whether a new population is comparable. It is the document most often missing and
the one most often requested in diligence.

**Branch point:** if the data cannot be described in detail for privacy or contract reasons, describe
the distribution and the process at the level that is disclosable, and state the constraint.

### Step 8. Maintain the change log

**Input:** releases and changes.

**Output:** an ordered log with date, version, change, reason, approver, and affected documents.

**Rationale:** The change log is the document that makes the model's history reconstructable. It
should be short per entry and complete across entries, and it should link to the change record for
changes that required one.

**Branch point:** if a change was made without a log entry, record it retrospectively with the date
of the entry and the date of the change. A reconstructed history is weaker than a contemporaneous
one, and it is better than a gap.

### Step 9. Link documents to artifacts

**Input:** the document register and the repository.

**Output:** a link from every document to the artifact it describes, and from every artifact to its
document.

**Rationale:** Links are what make the set navigable. A model document that names version 2.3 but
does not link to the release record forces the reader to search, and search is where documentation
fails.

**Branch point:** if a link cannot be made because the artifact is not versioned, fix the artifact
before writing more documents. Documentation cannot compensate for an unversioned model.

### Step 9a. Write for the reader, not for the archive

**Input:** the document and its named reader.

**Output:** a document that the reader can use without asking a question.

**Rationale:** Documentation fails most often at the sentence level, not the structural level. A
runbook that says "configure the environment appropriately" is structurally complete and unusable.
The test is whether each instruction names the thing to do, the tool, and the expected result.

**Branch point:** three reader-specific conventions.

| Reader | Convention | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Engineer | Imperative, exact commands, expected output | `python train.py --seed 42` returns exit code 0 |
| Reviewer | Claim, evidence, limitation, in that order | "AUROC 0.82 (0.79-0.85) in 4,200 cases; no external validation" |
| Auditor | Requirement, record, date, approval | "QP-07 s5.2; CR-114 approved 2 Feb 2026" |

The conventions are not stylistic preferences. A reviewer who has to infer the claim from a
paragraph will infer a different claim, and an auditor who has to search for the approval date will
record a finding.

### Step 10. Set the review cadence

**Input:** the document type and its criticality.

**Output:** a review interval per document type, with an owner and a trigger.

**Rationale:** Documents decay. A model document reviewed annually is stale for up to a year; a
release record is archival and does not need review. The cadence should reflect how fast the subject
changes and how much harm a stale document causes.

**Branch point:** three cadences.

| Document | Cadence | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Model document | Every release and annually | Model change |
| SOP | Annually | Process change or audit finding |
| Decision record | Never edited | Superseded by a new record |
| Runbook | Quarterly | Infrastructure change |
| Dataset document | Annually and on data refresh | New data source |

### Step 11. Retire and archive

**Input:** the register and the lifecycle state.

**Output:** obsolete documents withdrawn from use, archived with a date, and referenced in the
register.

**Rationale:** An obsolete document left in circulation will be used, and the resulting record will
be untraceable. Withdrawal is the control; archiving is the record.

**Branch point:** if a document is superseded, link the old and new versions so that a reader who
finds the old one is directed to the current one. The link is the difference between an archive and
a trap: an obsolete document without a pointer will be used by someone who does not know it is
obsolete, and the resulting work will have to be redone.

### Step 11a. Document incidents while they are live

**Input:** an incident, the timeline, and the decisions taken.

**Output:** an incident record with detection, impact, actions, timeline, root cause, and follow-up.

**Rationale:** The incident record is written under pressure and is the hardest document to
reconstruct. It is also the one that a regulator, a customer, and a future team will read most
closely. Writing it as the incident unfolds, with timestamps, is the only way to get an accurate
account.

**Branch point:** if the incident triggers a regulatory report, the record must satisfy the
reporting requirements as well as the internal ones. Check the requirement before writing, not
after.

**Minimum content.** Detection time and method; the affected population and period; the immediate
containment; the decision log with times; the communication to users and patients; the root cause
analysis reference; and the corrective actions with owners. The timeline is the part most often
missing and the part an investigator reads first.

### Step 12. Test with a newcomer

**Input:** the document set and a defined task.

**Output:** a test report listing where the newcomer got stuck, and the documents that need
improvement.

**Rationale:** The test is the only evidence that the documentation works. A newcomer following the
runbook will find the missing step, the stale command, and the undefined term, because they have no
prior knowledge to fill the gaps.

**Branch point:** if the newcomer cannot complete the task, the documentation is not complete,
regardless of how many pages it has. The specific point at which they stop is the highest-value
finding the test produces, because it is where the tacit knowledge was hiding.

### The documents that must change together

A change to a model touches a predictable set of documents, and the set is worth listing in the
change procedure so that none is forgotten.

| Change | Documents to update |
|---|---|
| Retraining | Model document, dataset document, validation report, change log, release record |
| Threshold change | Model document, decision record, release record, runbook, monitoring plan |
| New data source | Dataset document, model document, risk file, validation report |
| New site | Validation report, runbook, monitoring plan, training records |
| Interface change | Runbook, work instruction, training material, release record |
| Model retirement | Model document status, archive record, monitoring plan, customer communication |

The list converts a vague instruction ("update the documentation") into a checklist that a release
manager can complete. It also exposes the documents that teams do not have, which is how the check
doubles as a gap analysis at the moment of change rather than at the moment of audit.

### Edge cases

**Incomplete records.** A missing record is a documentation gap even when the work was done. Record
the reconstruction and its date; do not backdate. The difference between a reconstruction and a
fabrication is the label and the metadata, which is why the label matters.

**Selective documentation.** A team that documents only its successes is selecting. Decision records
should include the options that were rejected and the decisions that were reversed.

**Subgroup claims.** A subgroup claim in a model document needs the analysis and the interval. A
claim without the analysis is a documentation defect and a claim defect.

**Protocol deviations.** A deviation from a validation protocol must be recorded in the validation
report and in the change log if it affected the result.

**Post-hoc changes.** A threshold changed after evaluation is a post-hoc choice. Record it as such,
with the date and the reason, rather than presenting it as pre-specified.

**Conflicting reference standards.** If two standards were used, the model document should name both
and state which produced the reported performance.

**Retracted studies.** A citation to a retracted study must be removed or annotated in every
document that contains it, not only in the current version of the paper. A documentation system
that links citations to a reference manager can find them all; one that copies citations into
documents cannot.

The edge cases share a pattern: the document and the work diverge, and the remedy is to make the
divergence visible. A record marked "reconstructed on 14 May 2026 from the release log" is more
useful than a clean-looking record with a false date, because the reader can weigh it correctly.
Documentation that hides its own weaknesses is worse than documentation that admits them.

### Decision table for a documentation gap

| Gap | Consequence | Priority | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| No model document | Change and audit both blocked | High | Write from the evidence |
| No decision records | Reasoning lost; repeated debates | High | Start now; reconstruct recent decisions |
| Stale runbook | Operational failure | High | Update and test |
| No dataset document | Transportability unknown | Medium to high | Write distribution-level description |
| Missing release records | Trace broken | High | Reconstruct; fix the release process |
| No glossary | Onboarding slow | Low | Build incrementally |
| Obsolete documents in use | Wrong procedure followed | Medium | Withdraw and archive |

## 6. Worked example

The case is a documentation gap analysis for a 10-person analytics team that has built three
clinical models. The trigger is the departure of the senior data scientist who built the first
model, which is still in use at two hospitals. The team has a repository, a shared drive, a wiki, and
a set of slides. It has no documentation register.

The case is a composite assembled from common patterns. The scores and effort estimates are
constructed so that the prioritisation arithmetic can be checked.

### The inventory

The team listed 34 documents and classified them by type. The counts by type, against a minimum
expected set for a team of this size and risk, are:

| Type | Expected | Present | Complete | Current |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Model document | 3 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| Dataset document | 3 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Decision records | 12 | 4 | 2 | 4 |
| SOPs | 6 | 4 | 3 | 3 |
| Work instructions / runbooks | 6 | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| Change logs | 3 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Release records | 20 (per release) | 8 | 5 | 8 |
| Validation reports | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| Onboarding guide | 1 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| Glossary | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

The pattern is typical. Validation reports are complete, because they were required for publication
or procurement. Model and dataset documents are thin, because the team considered the repository
self-documenting. Decision records are sparse, because decisions were made in meetings. Release
records are incomplete, because releases were tagged in the repository without an approval record.

### Scoring the gaps

Each gap was scored on three dimensions: criticality (3 high, 2 medium, 1 low), current coverage
(0 absent, 1 partial, 2 complete), and staleness in months beyond the review interval. The
remediation priority is criticality times the coverage deficit, with a staleness bonus.

| Gap | Criticality | Coverage | Deficit | Staleness (months) | Priority score |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Model document for model 1 | 3 | 0 | 2 | 6 | 6.6 |
| Dataset document for model 1 | 3 | 0 | 2 | 6 | 6.6 |
| Release records missing | 3 | 1 | 1 | 3 | 3.3 |
| Decision records missing | 2 | 1 | 1 | 9 | 2.9 |
| Runbook for model 1 | 3 | 1 | 1 | 4 | 3.4 |
| Glossary | 1 | 0 | 2 | 0 | 2.0 |
| Onboarding guide | 1 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 1.2 |
| Obsolete SOP still in use | 2 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 2.0 |

The priority formula used here is: criticality x deficit x (1 + staleness/12). It is a heuristic and
the weights are a judgement; the value is that it makes the judgement explicit and comparable across
gaps.

### The remediation plan

The plan sequences work by priority and by dependency. A model document cannot be written before the
dataset document, because the model section must describe the training data. A decision record for
the threshold cannot be written before the validation report is located, because the rationale
depends on what was measured.

| Phase | Weeks | Work | Owner | Output |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1-2 | Reconstruct model 1 dataset and model documents from repository and validation report | Data lead | Two documents, marked reconstructed |
| 2 | 2-3 | Rebuild release records for the last eight releases; fix the release template | Engineering lead | Register plus template |
| 3 | 3-4 | Write runbook for model 1 and test it with a new engineer | Engineering lead | Tested runbook |
| 4 | 4-6 | Reconstruct six decision records from meeting notes and emails, marked as reconstructed | Product lead | Six records |
| 5 | 6-8 | Write SOPs for model change and release; withdraw the obsolete SOP | Quality lead | Two SOPs, register update |
| 6 | 8-10 | Glossary and onboarding guide | Team | Two documents |

Total effort: about 18 person-days over ten weeks. The estimate is deliberately modest, because the
work is reconstruction from existing artifacts rather than new analysis.

### The gap analysis verdict

Coverage by type, weighted by criticality, is 30.2 percent. Currency is 69 percent, because five of
sixteen documents are past their review interval. The critical gaps
are concentrated in the two documents that describe what the model learned from and what it is for,
which are exactly the documents a new owner or an auditor needs first.

The verdict is that the team can continue to operate the model but cannot safely change it, and
cannot answer a diligence request about it, until phase 1 is complete. That is a stronger statement
than "documentation is incomplete", and it is the statement the plan is designed to address.

### Recommendation

Execute phases 1 to 3 before any change to model 1. Phase 1 addresses the documents that block
change and diligence; phase 2 restores the trace; phase 3 makes operation survivable without the
departed engineer. Phases 4 to 6 improve the system and can run in parallel with normal work.

The recommendation also includes a standing change: add a documentation checklist item to the release
process, so that the gap does not reopen. Without that item, the team will reconstruct the same
documents after the next departure. The checklist item is the cheapest part of the plan and the one
most likely to be dropped, which is why it should be written into the release procedure rather than
left as an intention.

### Why the gap was not noticed earlier

The team was surprised by the size of the gap, and the surprise is itself a finding. Three factors
kept it invisible.

The team measured delivery, not documentation. Releases shipped, models performed, and the absence
of a model document did not appear in any metric. What is not measured is not managed, and
documentation had no metric until the departure forced one.

The knowledge was distributed in a way that felt sufficient. Each person knew their part, and
questions were answered in conversation within minutes. The system worked as long as the people
were present, which is the definition of a system that has not been tested.

The validation reports were good. Because the most visible documents were complete, the team
inferred that the documentation was adequate. The reports answered the external question and not
the internal one, and the difference only appeared when someone needed to change the model rather
than describe it.

### What the plan does not fix

The plan restores documents. It does not restore the knowledge that was never written down, and it
does not change the behaviour that produced the gap. Three things are outside its scope.

It does not recover the reasoning behind decisions that were never recorded. The reconstructed
decision records in phase 4 are inferences from code, meeting notes, and the validation report. They
are useful and they must be labelled as reconstructed, because a future reader needs to know the
difference between a contemporaneous record and a reconstruction.

It does not fix the release process. Phase 2 rebuilds the missing records and fixes the template,
which addresses the symptom. The cause is that the release process did not require the record, and
that is a change to the procedure and to the tooling.

It does not create a habit. The standing recommendation — a documentation checklist item in the
release process — is the mechanism that prevents recurrence, and it depends on someone enforcing it.
A plan without that item produces a clean set of documents that decays again within a year.

### The first thirty days

The plan is ten weeks, and the first thirty days determine whether it survives. Three actions in
that window matter more than the rest.

Publish the register. A visible list of documents, owners, and dates creates accountability without
a meeting. The register is also the artifact that makes progress measurable.

Complete phase 1. The two reconstructed documents for model 1 are the ones that unblock change and
diligence, and finishing them early demonstrates that the plan is real rather than another
intention.

Run the newcomer test once. A test in the first month finds the worst gaps while there is still
budget to fix them, and it converts the documentation work from a compliance task into a usability
task, which is easier to sustain.

### Two diagnostics that would change the verdict

**Diagnostic 1: the departed engineer is available for two days of interviews.** The model and
dataset documents could be written from direct knowledge rather than reconstructed, and the decision
records could be validated rather than inferred. The quality of the documents would rise from
"reconstructed" to "confirmed", the effort would fall by about a third, and the recommendation would
move from "cannot safely change" to "can change after phase 1". The diagnostic shows that the
binding constraint is knowledge, not writing.

**Diagnostic 2: the model is a regulated device with a design history file.** The gap analysis would
change scope entirely. The design history file would be the primary artifact, the model document
would be secondary, and the release records would be a regulatory requirement rather than good
practice. The priority scores would shift toward the design inputs and verification records, and the
ten-week plan would become a regulatory remediation with a notified body timeline. The verdict would
move from "operational risk" to "compliance risk", which changes the audience and the urgency.

### Vary one assumption: team size and turnover

The analysis assumes a ten-person team with one departure. If the team were thirty people with 25
percent annual turnover, the documentation would need to be more structured, not less: role-based
runbooks, a glossary, and onboarding paths become essential because knowledge cannot circulate
through conversation. The coverage targets would rise, the review cadence would shorten, and the
effort estimate would roughly double.

If the team were three people with no turnover, the same documents would still be needed for audit
and diligence, but the decision records would matter less, because the reasoning is held by people
who are still present. The lesson is that documentation requirements are a function of turnover and
external scrutiny, not of team virtue.

## 7. Code, query, or calculation

The script scores a documentation register for coverage, currency, and priority, and produces a
remediation order. It uses only the standard library.

```python
"""Documentation gap analysis.

Each entry: (name, type, criticality, coverage, months_since_review, review_interval_months)
coverage: 0 absent, 1 partial, 2 complete.
Priority = criticality * (2 - coverage) * (1 + max(0, months - interval) / 12)
"""

from collections import defaultdict

REGISTER = [
    ("model-1-doc", "model", 3, 0, 18, 12),
    ("model-2-doc", "model", 3, 1, 8, 12),
    ("dataset-1-doc", "dataset", 3, 0, 18, 12),
    ("dataset-2-doc", "dataset", 3, 1, 10, 12),
    ("runbook-1", "runbook", 3, 1, 10, 3),
    ("runbook-2", "runbook", 2, 2, 2, 3),
    ("sop-change", "sop", 2, 1, 14, 12),
    ("sop-release", "sop", 3, 1, 14, 12),
    ("sop-capa", "sop", 2, 2, 6, 12),
    ("decision-threshold", "decision", 2, 0, 20, 999),
    ("decision-split", "decision", 2, 1, 20, 999),
    ("decision-vendor", "decision", 3, 0, 20, 999),
    ("release-record-2.0", "record", 3, 1, 9, 999),
    ("release-record-2.1", "record", 3, 2, 6, 999),
    ("glossary", "glossary", 1, 0, 0, 12),
    ("onboarding", "onboarding", 1, 1, 8, 12),
]

EXPECTED = {"model": 3, "dataset": 3, "runbook": 6, "sop": 6,
            "decision": 12, "record": 20, "glossary": 1, "onboarding": 1}

CRIT_WEIGHT = {1: 1, 2: 2, 3: 3}


def priority(criticality, coverage, months, interval):
    deficit = 2 - coverage
    overdue = max(0, months - interval)
    return criticality * deficit * (1 + overdue / 12)


rows = []
for name, typ, crit, cov, months, interval in REGISTER:
    p = priority(crit, cov, months, interval)
    stale = months > interval
    rows.append((name, typ, crit, cov, months, interval, p, stale))

print(f"{'document':<22}{'type':<10}{'crit':>5}{'cov':>4}{'months':>7}{'stale':>7}{'priority':>10}")
print("-" * 68)
for name, typ, crit, cov, months, interval, p, stale in sorted(rows, key=lambda r: -r[6]):
    print(f"{name:<22}{typ:<10}{crit:>5}{cov:>4}{months:>7}{'yes' if stale else 'no':>7}{p:>10.1f}")

print()
present = defaultdict(int)
complete = defaultdict(int)
for name, typ, crit, cov, months, interval in REGISTER:
    if cov > 0:
        present[typ] += 1
    if cov == 2:
        complete[typ] += 1

print(f"{'type':<12}{'present':>9}{'expected':>10}{'coverage':>10}")
print("-" * 41)
weighted_cov = 0.0
weight_total = 0.0
for typ in sorted(EXPECTED):
    exp = EXPECTED[typ]
    have = present.get(typ, 0)
    cov = min(1.0, have / exp) if exp else 1.0
    print(f"{typ:<12}{have:>9}{exp:>10}{cov:>10.0%}")
    typ_crit = max([r[2] for r in rows if r[1] == typ] or [1])
    weighted_cov += cov * typ_crit
    weight_total += typ_crit
print()
print(f"unweighted coverage: {sum(min(1.0, present[t]/EXPECTED[t]) for t in EXPECTED)/len(EXPECTED):.1%}")
print(f"criticality-weighted coverage: {weighted_cov/weight_total:.1%}")

stale_count = sum(1 for r in rows if r[7])
print(f"stale documents: {stale_count}/{len(rows)} ({stale_count/len(rows):.0%})")
print(f"documents at coverage 0: {sum(1 for r in rows if r[3] == 0)}")
print()
print("remediation order (top 5):")
for i, r in enumerate(sorted(rows, key=lambda r: -r[6])[:5], start=1):
    print(f"  {i}. {r[0]} (priority {r[6]:.1f}, coverage {r[3]}, {r[4]} months)")
```

The output's ordering is not what a naive count would produce. The decision records are only 25
percent covered, but because there are twelve expected and each has a low individual weight, they
rank below the two missing model documents. The release records are 10 percent covered and rank
lowest in the type table, yet the missing release record for version 2.0 scores 3.0 because a release
record is critical. Coverage and priority are different questions, and the script reports both.

The weighted coverage of 30.2 percent is lower than the unweighted 33.5 percent, which tells you
that the team's existing documents are concentrated in the low-criticality types: the SOPs and the
onboarding guide exist, while the model and dataset documents do not. A team whose weighted coverage
is higher than its unweighted coverage has done the reverse and is in a stronger position. The
comparison is a one-line diagnostic of whether the documentation effort went where the risk is.

A note on the priority formula. The staleness bonus assumes that a document past its review interval
becomes progressively less reliable, which is true on average and false in particular: a runbook for
a stable service may be accurate for years, and a model document may be wrong the day after a
release. The formula is a triage device, not a measurement, and the reviewer should override it when
the context says otherwise.

The `EXPECTED` counts are the other judgement embedded in the script. They encode a view about how
many documents a team of this size should have, and a different team would set different numbers.
The value of stating them explicitly is that the coverage percentages become arguable rather than
mysterious, and a reviewer can substitute their own expectations.

Two limits of the scoring should be stated. The priority formula is a heuristic, and the review
intervals are judgement calls. The coverage measure counts documents, not quality, so a team can
score well with documents that say nothing useful. The script is a prioritisation aid; the quality
judgement requires reading the documents, which is the next step in the plan.

## 8. Data, standards, and regulatory context

**ISO 13485:2016 clause 4.2.** The standard requires a quality manual, documented procedures, and
documents needed to ensure effective planning and control of processes. It requires document control
(clause 4.2.4) covering approval, review, version identification, availability, legibility, and
prevention of unintended use of obsolete documents, and record control (clause 4.2.5) covering
identification, storage, protection, retrieval, retention, and disposition.

**FDA QMSR.** The Quality Management System Regulation, effective 2 February 2026, incorporates ISO
13485:2016 and therefore imports the document and record control requirements. It retains
requirements for design controls and for records that demonstrate conformity. The regulation is
binding for devices marketed in the United States.

**21 CFR Part 11.** Where records are electronic and used to satisfy regulatory requirements, the
regulation requires validated systems, access controls, and audit trails that record the date and
time of operator entries and actions that create, modify, or delete records. Records must be
retrievable and available for review throughout the retention period.

**ALCOA and data integrity.** The principles attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original, and
accurate, extended by complete, consistent, enduring, and available, define what a record must be.
For documentation, the practical implications are: a named author, a date, no backdating, no
overwriting, and retention for the defined period. The FDA's guidance on good documentation
practices and its 2024 question-and-answer guidance on electronic records describe the expectations.

**ICH E6 Good Clinical Practice.** For clinical investigations, GCP requires source documents,
case report forms, and a trial master file with specified content and retention. A model developed
from trial data inherits these obligations for the data it uses.

**TRIPOD+AI and open science.** TRIPOD+AI includes items on protocol availability, registration,
data access, and code access. These are reporting items, and they create a documentation obligation
that overlaps with the audit record: the protocol and the analysis plan are documents that a
reviewer expects to find.

**Records as evidence in an audit.** ISO 19011 treats records as the primary audit evidence, and an
auditor samples them to test whether the documented process was followed. This makes record design a
quality activity rather than an administrative one: a record that cannot be sampled, because it is
unstructured or unindexed, cannot serve as evidence even when it exists. The register and the index
are therefore part of the record system, not a convenience on top of it.

**Model cards and datasheets.** The model card and datasheet literature defines the content of the
model and dataset documents. Topic 04 covers them in detail; for this topic, they are the two
documents that most often block a change or a diligence request when missing.

**Document retention and the archive.** A document that is obsolete is not a document that can be
deleted. ISO 13485 requires records to be retained for a defined period, and the applicable
regulation may extend it. The practical rule is that the archive holds superseded controlled
documents and records for the retention period, with an index that allows retrieval, and that
deletion requires a documented disposal decision. A shared drive that is "cleaned up" annually is
not an archive.

**Retention.** Retention periods are set by the applicable regulation and by contract, and they are
usually longer than teams expect. A validation record for a device may need to be retained for the
lifetime of the device plus a defined period. Documentation that is deleted when a project ends may
be a compliance failure rather than good hygiene.

## 9. Industry practice and case evidence

**Documented fact: documentation is a recurring inspection issue.** The FDA's inspection findings
and the device industry's CAPA guidance both identify inadequate procedures and records as persistent
weaknesses. The QMSR's harmonisation with ISO 13485 raises the expectation rather than lowering it,
because the standard's document control clauses are explicit and auditable.

**Documented fact: shared code is often not runnable.** The 2026 scoping review of repositories
linked to prediction-model papers found code-sharing statements in 12.2 percent of articles, and
among accessible repositories only about 52 percent had adequate READMEs and 38 percent specified
dependencies. The inference: documentation, not code availability, is the binding constraint on
reuse.

**Documented fact: model cards are often thin.** Topic 04 reviews the evidence that model cards
frequently contain headings without decision-relevant content. The same pattern appears in internal
documentation: the sections exist and the numbers do not.

**Inference: why documentation decays.** Three mechanisms. The document is not owned, so no one
notices when it becomes stale. The document is not linked to the artifact, so a change does not
trigger an update. The document is written for an audit rather than for use, so it is never read
between audits and its inaccuracies are never discovered.

**Inference: the onboarding test as a proxy.** Teams that measure onboarding time find that it
correlates with documentation quality more strongly than with team size. A new analyst who can make
a first contribution in two weeks is working in a documented system; one who takes two months is
working in a system where knowledge travels through conversation. The test is cheap to run and hard
to argue with, which is why it is a better management metric than document count.

**Inference: what good documentation costs.** A one-page decision record per significant decision,
a model document per model, a dataset document per dataset, a runbook per service, and a change log
per release. For a ten-person team, the steady-state cost is perhaps two to three days per month.
The benefit is that a departure costs a week rather than a quarter, and an audit costs a day rather
than a month.

**Inference: the archive is a compliance asset and a cost.** Retaining everything forever is
expensive and creates discovery risk; deleting on a schedule that does not match the regulation is
a compliance failure. The workable policy is a retention schedule by record type, approved once,
applied automatically, with a documented disposal decision at the end. Teams that have no schedule
default to keeping everything, which is safer and more expensive, or to deleting at project close,
which is cheaper and riskier.

**Inference: documentation as a leading indicator of quality.** A team whose decision records are
current, whose runbooks are tested, and whose release records carry hashes tends to have fewer
operational surprises, because the same discipline that produces the records produces the control.
The correlation is not causal in either direction; both are consequences of a team that expects to
be asked. But it is a useful screening signal in diligence, and it is cheap to check.

**Inference: the commercial value.** Documentation is a diligence asset. A vendor that can produce a
model document, a dataset document, a change log, and a decision record for the current version
shortens a buyer's review and reduces the perceived risk. A vendor that cannot invites a discount or
a delay, because the buyer must price the uncertainty.

## 12. Exercises

**Exercise 1. Build a document register (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a register of at least 15 documents with type, owner, location, last review, and
criticality. Acceptance criteria: every document has an owner; duplicates are identified with one
authoritative copy. Time: 30 minutes.

**Exercise 2. Write a decision record (design, 25 minutes).**
Deliverable: a one-page record for a real decision, with context, options, decision, rationale,
consequences, and date. Acceptance criteria: at least two options considered; the rationale explains
the rejection of the alternative. Time: 25 minutes.

**Exercise 3. Write an SOP (design, 40 minutes).**
Deliverable: an SOP with purpose, scope, responsibilities, steps, records, and revision history.
Acceptance criteria: a competent colleague can follow it; the records it generates are named. Time:
40 minutes.

**Exercise 4. Write a runbook (design, 35 minutes).**
Deliverable: a runbook for a model service with start, stop, health check, common failures, and
escalation. Acceptance criteria: commands are exact; expected outputs are stated; a failure path is
included. Time: 35 minutes.

**Exercise 5. Run a gap analysis (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a scored register with coverage, currency, and priority, and a phased remediation plan.
Acceptance criteria: scoring rule stated; plan sequenced by dependency; effort estimated. Time: 45
minutes.

**Exercise 6. Test with a newcomer (critique, 40 minutes).**
Deliverable: a test report from a colleague who follows the runbook to complete a task, listing every
point of confusion. Acceptance criteria: the tester had no prior knowledge; each issue maps to a
document and a fix. Time: 40 minutes.

**Exercise 7. Design the documentation system (design, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a one-page specification of document types, templates, owners, cadences, and archive
rules for a team of your size. Acceptance criteria: proportionate; each type has a reader and a
trigger; retention addressed. Time: 45 minutes.

**Exercise 8. Write an incident record (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a one-page record for a simulated incident with detection, impact, timeline,
containment, root cause reference, and follow-up. Acceptance criteria: timestamps present; decisions
recorded with owners; the record distinguishes what was known from what was assumed. Time: 30
minutes.

**Exercise 9. Reconcile two documents (application, 25 minutes).**
Deliverable: a comparison of a model document and a release record for the same version, with every
discrepancy listed and classified. Acceptance criteria: discrepancies are factual, not stylistic;
the authoritative source is named for each. Time: 25 minutes.

### How to use the exercises

Exercises 2 to 4 are the writing skills: decision record, SOP, runbook. They are short on purpose,
because the barrier to documentation is not time but the belief that it must be comprehensive.
Exercises 1 and 5 are the analysis: register and gap analysis, which together produce the plan.
Exercise 6 is the test that closes the loop, and it should be run after the other work, because it
will find the gaps the authors cannot see. Exercise 7 is the system design, which is what makes the
practice survive.

The exercises are deliberately short. A decision record in twenty-five minutes, an SOP in forty, a
runbook in thirty-five: the timings are the argument. Documentation is expensive when it is treated
as a project and cheap when it is treated as a habit, and the exercises are calibrated to the habit.

## 13. Self-check answers

**A1. What is the difference between a document and a record?** A document describes what should
happen or what is known; a record is evidence that something happened. Procedures are documents;
completed checklists are records.

**A2. Why are decision records high value?** They capture reasoning that cannot be recovered from
code, prevent repeated debate, and explain surprising behaviour to a future owner. They are cheap to
write at the time and expensive to reconstruct.

**A3. What are the four documentation layers?** Decision records, specifications, procedures, and
records.

**A4. What does the single-source rule mean?** Every fact has one authoritative location; other
documents reference it rather than repeat it, so that disagreement cannot arise.

**A5. Why does documentation decay?** Because it is unowned, unlinked to the artifact, or written for
an audit rather than for use, so no one notices when it becomes stale.

**A6. What is the test of usable documentation?** A competent newcomer can complete a defined task
using only the documents.

**A7. What belongs in a change log entry?** Date, version, change, reason, approver, and the
documents affected.

**A8. Why must obsolete documents be withdrawn rather than deleted?** Because they are records of
what was in force, and because a document left in circulation will be used. Withdrawal prevents use;
archiving preserves the record.

**A9. What is documentation debt?** The gap between what is documented and what must be known for
the work to continue safely, measured by the cost of reconstructing it.

**A10. Compute the priority.** A criticality-3 document with coverage 1, reviewed 18 months ago with
a 12-month interval: 3 x (2-1) x (1 + 6/12) = 4.5.

**A11. Why is a reconstructed document labelled rather than presented as original?** Because the
reader needs to weigh it differently. A reconstruction is an inference from artifacts and may be
wrong; a contemporaneous record is evidence. The label preserves the distinction, and the metadata
usually reveals it anyway.

**A13. What is the read-back test?** A reader restates the document's instructions or claims in
their own words, and the author checks whether the meaning survived. It detects ambiguity that a
newcomer test detects only when the ambiguity causes a failure.

**A14. Why list the documents that change together?** Because a change typically updates one
document and leaves the others stale, producing a disagreement that a reviewer must resolve. The
list converts the update into a checklist at the moment of change.

**A12. What is the relationship between coverage and currency?** They measure different failures. A
document set can be complete and stale, or current and incomplete. A gap analysis reports both,
because a complete set of stale documents is as misleading as an incomplete set of current ones.

**Rubric for Exercise 5.** Full credit: scoring rule stated; coverage and currency reported
separately; plan sequenced by dependency with effort estimates. Deduction for a list of missing
documents without prioritisation.

**Rubric for Exercise 3.** Full credit: all six sections; records named; a colleague can follow it;
revision history present. Partial: steps present but no records or responsibilities.

## 14. Interview questions

**Q1. How do you make documentation survive staff turnover?**
Strong answer: named owners, decision records written at the time, links to artifacts, review
cadence, and a newcomer test. Weak answer: says write more documentation.

**Q2. What is the difference between documentation for engineers and for reviewers?**
Strong answer: engineers need commands, schemas, and failure modes; reviewers need claims, evidence,
and limitations; the facts are shared but the organisation differs. Weak answer: says one document
serves both.

**Q3. What would you write first for an undocumented model?**
Strong answer: the model and dataset documents, because they block change and diligence, then the
release record trace. Weak answer: starts with a glossary.

**Q4. How do you keep a runbook current?**
Strong answer: quarterly test by someone who did not write it, with a trigger on infrastructure
change; stale commands are treated as defects. Weak answer: annual review by the author.

**Q5. What is documentation debt and how do you measure it?**
Strong answer: the cost of reconstructing missing knowledge; measured by a register scored for
coverage, currency, and criticality. Weak answer: says it is missing documents.

**Q6. A document and the code disagree. Which wins?**
Strong answer: the artifact is authoritative for behaviour; the document is a defect and must be
corrected; the single-source rule determines where the correction goes. Weak answer: says the
document wins.

**Q7. How do you document a decision that was reversed?**
Strong answer: write a new record that supersedes the old one and link them; never edit the old
record, because the history is the value. Weak answer: updates the original.

**Q8. What records does an auditor expect for a model release?**
Strong answer: version and hash, approval, change description, verification evidence, and the
associated documents at their effective versions. Weak answer: says a release note.

**Q9. How do you document data you cannot disclose?**
Strong answer: distribution-level description, collection process, constraint and its reason, and
what a reader cannot assess. Weak answer: says it is confidential.

**Q10. What is the cost of poor documentation?**
Strong answer: slower change, failed audits, longer diligence, and knowledge loss at turnover;
quantified in weeks per departure and days per audit. Weak answer: says it is hard to maintain.

**Q11. How do you decide what not to document?**
Strong answer: documents that support no decision are candidates for deletion; the register is
reviewed for unused documents. Weak answer: says document everything.

**Q12. How do you document a model you inherited and did not build?**
Strong answer: starts from artifacts (code, validation report, release tags), writes a specification
and marks it reconstructed, interviews whoever is available, and records open questions as gaps
rather than filling them with inference. Weak answer: writes a document from assumptions and
presents it as fact.

**Q13. What is the difference between an SOP and a work instruction?**
Strong answer: the SOP defines the process, responsibilities, and records; the work instruction
gives the task-level steps. A team needs few SOPs and several work instructions. Weak answer: treats
them as synonyms.

**Q14. How do you document a change that was made in a hurry and not recorded?**
Strong answer: records it retrospectively with both dates, describes what is known and what is
inferred, and adds a corrective action on the change process rather than only on the document.
Weak answer: backdates the record or omits it.

**Q15. What is the smallest documentation set that would let you take over a model tomorrow?**
Strong answer: model document, dataset document, runbook, release record with the version, and the
last three decision records; explains why each is needed. Weak answer: lists only the code
repository.

**Q16. What would you do if the documentation contradicts the running system?**
Strong answer: treats the system as authoritative for behaviour, records the discrepancy as a defect,
corrects the document, and checks whether the discrepancy affected any decision made from the
document. Weak answer: trusts the document.

**Q17. What makes a decision record useful a year later?**
Strong answer: the options, the constraints at the time, the reason for the choice, and the
consequences accepted; dated and linked to the artifact. Weak answer: records only the decision.

## 15. Key terms

- **Controlled document** — versioned, approved, distributed document.
- **Record** — evidence that an activity occurred.
- **SOP** — documented process with responsibilities.
- **Work instruction** — task-level how-to.
- **Runbook** — operational guide for a service.
- **Decision record** — one-page rationale for a decision.
- **Model document** — description of a model's purpose and evidence.
- **Dataset document** — description of a dataset's provenance and use.
- **Change log** — ordered record of changes.
- **Documentation debt** — cost of missing or stale knowledge.
- **Single source of truth** — one authoritative location per fact.
- **Document lifecycle** — draft to archive.
- **Traceability** — links from claims to evidence.
- **Onboarding guide** — path for a new team member.
- **Glossary** — defined terms.
- **Archive** — retained obsolete documents.
- **ALCOA** — record quality principles.
- **Retention period** — required storage duration.
- **Newcomer test** — usability test of documentation.
- **Bidirectional link** — document to artifact and back.
- **Reconstruction label** — marking a document written after the fact.
- **Document register** — index of documents with owners and dates.
- **Archive index** — finding aid for obsolete documents.
- **Retention schedule** — defined periods for each record type.
- **Incident record** — timeline and decisions for a live incident.
- **Knowledge transfer** — moving understanding from people to artifacts.
- **Documentation coverage** — share of expected documents that exist.
- **Documentation currency** — share of documents within their review interval.
- **Change checklist** — the documents a given change requires.
- **Read-back test** — whether a reader can restate the document's meaning.
- **Record system** — the indexed set of records and their retention.
- **Documentation owner** — the role accountable for the register and reviews.
- **Retention schedule** — approved periods by record type.
- **Disposal decision** — documented authorisation to delete records.
- **Document set** — the complete, minimal collection for a product.
- **Records index** — finding aid for records by type, date, and product.
- **Controlled copy** — the version authorised for use.
