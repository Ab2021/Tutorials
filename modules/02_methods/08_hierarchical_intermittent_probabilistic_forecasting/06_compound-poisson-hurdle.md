## 1. Why this topic exists
A planner has a reorder point in front of them and one question: if we set the order-up-to level
here, what fill rate will we achieve? A forecaster answers with a rate — units per week — and it
is the expected value of a process that is zero most weeks and large in a few. The question is
not about the expected value. It is about the shape of the distribution above the order-up-to
level, and a rate carries no shape at all.

The gap is not a gap in the forecaster's competence. Croston, the Syntetos–Boylan approximation
(SBA) and the Teunter–Syntetos–Babai (TSB) method estimate an expectation. They answer "what is
the average demand per period", with known biases that Module 07 topic 06 derives. They cannot
answer "what is the probability that four weeks of demand exceeds 18 units", because that
probability is not a function of the mean. Two processes with the same mean can differ by eleven
percentage points of fill rate at the same stock level, and section 6 shows exactly that with two
distributions sharing a mean of 9.86 units.

Three costs follow, in ascending order of visibility.

1. **The order quantity is set from the mean, and service misses the target.** Safety stock is a
   service factor times a standard deviation, and that standard deviation was measured one period
   ahead while the lead time is four weeks. Nothing in the arithmetic is wrong. It is the wrong
   standard deviation.
2. **The decomposition is fitted inconsistently, and inventory overshoots.** Prak, Teunter, Babai,
   Boylan and Syntetos (2021) report that Croston-type estimates of the compound Poisson parameters
   are inconsistent, and that the resulting order-up-to levels achieve about 98–99 per cent fill
   against a 95 per cent target in a case with one arrival per week. The excess is invisible in a
   service report, because service looks excellent.
3. **The tail is missing, and the tail is what a service target buys.** A 95 per cent service level
   is a statement about a quantile or an expected shortfall. Both are functionals of the whole
   distribution, and a method that returns a mean has neither.

This topic supplies the distributional machinery underneath the classification of Module 08 topic
05. That topic told you which cell an item is in. This one tells you what the cell implies about
the shape of lead-time demand, how to estimate that shape, and what you may claim once you have.

> **Note:** The measurement vocabulary — average inter-demand interval (ADI), squared coefficient
> of variation (CV²), and the four-class grid — is defined in
> [Module 01A topic 10](../../01_foundations/01A_foundations_units_time_assumptions/10_intermittent-metrics.md).
> The Croston, SBA and TSB recursions are derived in
> [Module 07 topic 06](../07_time_series_forecasting/06_intermittent-demand.md). Neither is
> repeated here. This file spends its length on the generative model.

## 2. Learning outcomes
By the end of this topic you can:

1. **Draw** the two-stage generative process — arrival, then size, then compound sum — naming the
   parameter at each stage, and **state** which parameters a point forecast identifies.
2. **Estimate** an arrival rate from periodic data using the fraction of zero periods, **attach** a
   confidence interval, and **explain** why counting demand events gives a different answer.
3. **Demonstrate** that the mean of the non-zero periods overstates the mean demand size by
   `λ / (1 − e^−λ)`, and **explain** what that does to an order-up-to level.
4. **Choose** among Gamma, lognormal, negative binomial and empirical size distributions for a
   named dataset, and **name** the diagnostic each one usually fails.
5. **Derive** the mean, variance and zero probability of lead-time demand from the two parameters,
   and **compute** the full distribution by recursion for a small discrete case.
6. **Contrast** cycle service level and fill rate from the same distribution, and **explain** why
   the mean alone yields neither.
7. **Compare** parametric, empirical and simulation routes, stating the assumption each adds and
   the evidence on when each wins.
8. **Test** for the correlation between arrivals and sizes that breaks the model, and **report** an
   arrival rate with an interval and a size distribution with its diagnostics.

## 3. The core mental model
Hold one picture: demand is not a quantity per period. It is a count of events multiplied by a size
per event, and the two are generated by different mechanisms with different parameters.

### 3.1 The two-stage generative process
```text
   STAGE 1 — ARRIVALS                        STAGE 2 — SIZES
   when does a demand event occur?          how large is the event, given that it occurs?

   time  |---*------*--------*------*--->    *  --> z_1  ~ F
         |   event  event   event  event     *  --> z_2  ~ F
         |                                   *  --> z_3  ~ F
   gaps ~ Exponential(rate = lambda)         sizes i.i.d. from F,
   count in L periods ~ Poisson(lambda*L)    mean mu, second moment E[Z^2]
   lambda has units: events per period       z has units: eaches per event
                                             independent of arrival times

                              |
                              v
        +------------------------------------------------------+
        |  N(L) ~ Poisson(lambda * L)     number of events      |
        |  Z_1 ... Z_N i.i.d. ~ F         size of each event    |
        +------------------------------------------------------+
                              |
                              v
        S(L) = Z_1 + Z_2 + ... + Z_N(L)          the COMPOUND SUM
                              |
        mean       E[S]    = lambda * L * mu
        variance   Var[S]  = lambda * L * E[Z^2]  = lambda*L*(mu^2 + sigma^2)
        zero mass  P(S=0)  = exp(-lambda * L)
                              |
                              v
        +------------------------------------------------------+
        |  F_S(x) = P(S(L) <= x)      the LEAD-TIME DISTRIBUTION|
        +------------------------------------------------------+
                              |
              +---------------+----------------+
              |                                |
        order-up-to level                fill rate, expected
        at quantile tau                  units short, cost
```

Read the diagram as a claim about identification. The arrival rate is set at the first stage, the
size law at the second, and the lead-time distribution follows deterministically from both plus the
independence assumption. A point forecast collapses the last box to a single number and, in doing
so, throws away the two parameters that generated it.

### 3.2 What the decomposition buys, and what it costs
It buys three things a rate cannot give. **Extrapolation to a fractional horizon:** an arrival
rate is per unit time and a lead time is in the same unit, so `λL` is defined for `L = 3.7` weeks;
a per-period rate times a rounded horizon is not. **Extrapolation to a different bucket:** a
compound Poisson aggregated from weeks to months is still compound Poisson, with rate `4.33λ` and
the same size law — a genuine mathematical result, and the reason temporal aggregation is coherent.
**A distribution rather than a scalar:** quantiles, fill rates and expected shortfalls all follow
from `F_S`.

It costs one assumption, and that assumption does a great deal of work: **arrivals are independent
of sizes and independent of each other.** Independence is what makes the count Poisson, the sum
compound Poisson, and the convolution valid. When it fails — a promotion producing both more orders
and larger ones, an allocation rule rationing small customers differently from large, a batch-buying
cycle — every downstream number is wrong in a direction that depends on the sign of the correlation.
Step 4 tests it; section 10 lists what happens when nobody does.

The mean is the one quantity the two approaches share: a compound Poisson has `E[S] = λLμ`, which
is what a rate-based method estimates. They agree on the mean and disagree on everything else,
which is why comparing them on mean absolute error is close to meaningless.

### 3.3 The discrete-time analogue: the hurdle
A hurdle model is the same idea with the clock removed. Each period is a Bernoulli trial for
occurrence, and a size is drawn if and only if the trial succeeds:

```text
   per period t:
       occurrence    o_t ~ Bernoulli(p)          p = P(demand in a period)
       size          z_t ~ G  on {1, 2, 3, ...}   G is zero-truncated
       observation   y_t = o_t * z_t

   P(y = 0) = 1 - p
   P(y = y) = p * g(y)          for y > 0
   E[y]     = p * mu_G
   Var[y]   = p * (mu_G^2 + sigma_G^2) - p^2 * mu_G^2
```

The relationship to the compound Poisson is exact. If you fit a hurdle to weekly data with
`p = 1 − e^−λ` and with `G` the conditional distribution of weekly demand given that demand
occurred, the two models have **identical weekly marginals**. They are observationally equivalent
at the bucket you fitted, and they diverge only on extrapolation: the compound Poisson asserts that
four-week demand is compound Poisson with rate `4λ`; the hurdle asserts that it is the four-fold
convolution of the fitted weekly law. At integer horizons under independence the answers coincide.
At a fractional horizon, or when you need the arrival rate for another purpose — shipment batching,
order frequency, a supplier's capacity plan — only the compound Poisson has one to give.

The hurdle's advantage is flexibility: it accepts any size law, including a regression with
covariates, and a logistic model for occurrence carrying price, season and customer features. The
compound Poisson's advantage is identification: the Poisson zero probability pins the arrival rate,
and once the rate is pinned the mean size follows from the mean demand. That is why `λ` is estimable
from periodic data at all, and why the model is fragile when arrivals are not Poisson.

### 3.4 Where the classification sits
Module 08 topic 05 put each item in a cell using ADI and CV², both of which are summaries of this
model:

| Classification cell | Compound Poisson reading | What the cell implies for the distribution |
|---|---|---|
| Smooth | `λ` high, size variance low | Nearly Poisson; normal approximation defensible |
| Erratic | `λ` high, size variance high | Overdispersed counts; the size law drives the tail |
| Intermittent | `λ` low, size variance low | Zero spike plus a tight positive cluster; the arrival rate is the whole story |
| Lumpy | `λ` low, size variance high | Zero spike plus a heavy tail; where the size law matters most |

The cell does not give the parameters. It tells you which parameter should matter, and therefore
which diagnostic to look at first.

## 4. Definitions and vocabulary
| Term | Definition | Common misuse | Unit or coding case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compound Poisson process | Events arrive as a Poisson process and each event carries a randomly sized quantity | Used loosely for any zero-inflated series | Events per period, plus a size law |
| Arrival rate (λ) | Expected number of demand events per unit time | Confused with the demand rate, which is `λ` times the mean size | Events per week or per day |
| Demand rate | Expected demand per period, `λ · μ` | Reported as "the forecast" without saying it is a mean | Units per period |
| Size law (F) | The distribution of the quantity carried by one event | Fitted to period totals instead of event sizes | Units per event |
| Compound sum | `S = Z₁ + … + Z_N` with `N` random | Computed as `N̄ · Z̄` when the whole distribution is needed | Units |
| Stuttering Poisson | The compound Poisson with geometrically distributed sizes; the historical name | Used as a synonym for compound Poisson | Events per period, plus geometric size |
| Pólya–Aeppli distribution | Another name for the Poisson–geometric compound, common in risk theory | Assumed to be a different family | Count distribution |
| Convolution | The operation combining two independent distributions into the distribution of their sum | Performed on rates rather than distributions | Distribution, not a number |
| Closure under convolution | A sum of independent compound Poisson variables is compound Poisson with summed rates | Assumed to hold for all count families, which it does not | Model property |
| Lead-time demand (LTD) | Demand accumulated over lead time plus review period | Replaced by rate times a rounded horizon | Units per replenishment cycle |
| `N(L)` | The random number of arrivals in a window of length `L` | Set equal to the observed count of non-zero periods | Count of events |
| Zero fraction (`n₀/n`) | Share of periods with no demand | Used as the occurrence probability in a compound model, where they differ | Proportion of periods |
| Occurrence probability (p) | Probability that a period contains at least one demand event | Equated with `λ` | Probability per period |
| Hurdle model | A binary occurrence stage and a zero-truncated size stage | Confused with a zero-inflated model, whose count law also has zeros | Model class |
| Zero-inflated model | A mixture `π + (1−π)f(0)` at zero, where `f` can also produce zeros | Treated as interchangeable with a hurdle | Model class |
| Structural zero | A zero arising because the process cannot produce a positive value in that period | Confused with a sampling zero | 0, with a reason code |
| Gamma distribution | A continuous right-skewed family parameterised by shape and scale | Fitted to counts without acknowledging the continuous support | Units per event |
| Lognormal distribution | A family whose logarithm is normal; heavy right tail | Assumed to fit small sizes well, which it does not | Units per event |
| Negative binomial (NB) | A count family with variance greater than the mean; a Gamma-mixed Poisson | Used for sizes when the sizes are continuous | Count per period |
| Variance-to-mean ratio (VMR) | Variance divided by the mean; 1 for Poisson | Reported as "CV", which is a different quantity | Dimensionless |
| Index of dispersion | The sample VMR times `n − 1`; a test statistic for Poisson-ness | Reported without its degrees of freedom | Dimensionless statistic |
| Overdispersion | Variance exceeds the mean | Treated as a nuisance rather than as evidence of compounding | Dimensionless |
| Mixed Poisson | A Poisson whose rate is itself random; the source of overdispersion | Confused with a mixture of Poissons at the observation level | Model class |
| Cox process | A Poisson process with a random intensity; the continuous-time mixed Poisson | Invoked without saying what drives the intensity | Events per period |
| Thinning | Randomly deleting events from a Poisson process, which leaves a Poisson process | Confused with the opposite operation, superposition | Model operation |
| Method of moments (MM) | Estimating parameters by matching sample to theoretical moments | Assumed as efficient as maximum likelihood | Estimator |
| Maximum likelihood (MLE) | Estimating parameters by maximising the probability of the observed data | Applied to period totals when event sizes are needed | Estimator |
| Fraction-of-zeros estimator | `λ̂ = −ln(n₀/n)`, derived from the Poisson zero probability | Confused with `1 − n₀/n`, the occurrence probability | Events per period |
| Consistency (of an estimator) | Convergence to the true parameter as the sample grows | Used to mean "unbiased", a different property | Estimator property |
| Panjer recursion | A recursion computing the compound-sum distribution for discrete sizes in linear time | Applied to continuous sizes without discretisation | Algorithm |
| Fast Fourier transform (FFT) convolution | A numerical route to the compound sum via characteristic functions | Used without checking discretisation error in the tail | Algorithm |
| Empirical distribution | Observed frequencies used directly, without a parametric family | Applied to fewer observations than the tail requires | Distribution |
| Bootstrap | Resampling observed values to build a distribution | Assumed assumption-free, although it assumes exchangeability | Algorithm |
| Probability integral transform (PIT) | `F(y)` at the realised value; uniform if the forecast is calibrated | Reported as a histogram without a binning rule | Dimensionless, in [0, 1] |
| Continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) | A proper score for a full distribution, in the variable's units | Compared across items in different units without normalisation | Units |
| Cycle service level (CSL) | Probability demand over a cycle is met from stock, `F_S(S)` | Reported as fill rate | Proportion |
| Fill rate | Expected fraction of demand units served, `1 − E[(D−S)⁺]/E[D]` | Reported as CSL | Proportion |
| Expected shortfall | Expected units of unmet demand above a stock level | Computed from a normal approximation in the tail, where it is worst | Units per cycle |
| Critical ratio | Underage cost over underage plus overage cost; the target quantile | Set by policy with no reference to cost | Proportion |

Two rows deserve expansion. **Zero fraction, occurrence probability and arrival rate** are three
different numbers, all legitimate, none equal. At `λ = 0.62` per week the occurrence probability is
`1 − e^−0.62 = 0.46`, the zero fraction is `0.54`, and the arrival rate is `0.62`. In a hurdle the
parameter is the occurrence probability; in a compound Poisson it is the arrival rate. Plugging
`0.46` into a formula expecting `0.62`, or the reverse, changes the implied mean size by 34 per cent
here, and the error is invisible because both numbers lie between zero and one.

**Hurdle versus zero-inflated.** A hurdle says a period either has no demand or has a positive
amount drawn from a law with no mass at zero. A zero-inflated model says every period draws from a
count law that can produce zeros, and some zeros are additionally forced. They differ in what they
assume about the zero periods. For spare parts the hurdle is usually the better description,
because "nobody ordered" and "somebody ordered a quantity that happened to be zero" are not the
same event — and the difference changes the likelihood, therefore the fit.

## 5. The mechanics, step by step
The failure mode here is not a wrong formula. It is fitting the two stages to the wrong objects and
not noticing, because both fits look reasonable.

### Phase A — Decide whether the generative model applies
**Step 1. Fix the grid, the bucket and the demand construct, and write them down.** The grid is
item × location × period, dense, with explicit zeros; the bucket is the replenishment period, not
the forecast refresh period; the construct is consumption or orders, with the censoring risk
stated. Every parameter here is a property of that triple.

**Step 2. Establish the clock.** Under the **continuous** clock events arrive at rate `λ` per week
and a week may contain zero, one, two or more events: the compound Poisson. Under the **discrete**
clock each week holds at most one event with probability `p`: the hurdle. Periodic data cannot
distinguish them directly — a week with 12 units could be one event of 12 or two of 6 — and the
period-demand distribution is informative only with enough data. State your clock, and if it is the
discrete one, accept that you cannot report a sub-period arrival rate.

**Step 3. Test whether a simple Poisson is already excluded.** A Poisson count has variance equal
to its mean:

```text
   VMR = s^2 / xbar
   dispersion statistic = (n - 1) * s^2 / xbar     ~ chi-square with n - 1 df under Poisson
```

If the statistic is far above its degrees of freedom the simple Poisson is rejected and a
compounded model indicated. In the worked example `s² = 10.9`, `x̄ = 2.47`, `n = 100`, giving
`99 × 10.9 / 2.47 = 437` on 99 degrees of freedom: the period variance is four and a half times the
mean, the signature of compounding.

> **Note:** A rejection of the simple Poisson does not establish the compound Poisson. It
> establishes that variance exceeds the mean, which is also consistent with a negative binomial, a
> hurdle with a heavy size law, or a rate that varies over time.

**Step 4. Test the independence assumptions, because they break silently.** Three claims are
bundled into the model, each with its own test.

| Claim | What it means | How to test it | What a failure looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrivals are Poisson | No clustering, trend or seasonality in event times | Dispersion test on counts per bucket; runs test on zero/non-zero; interarrival histogram against exponential | Bursts of events in consecutive periods; a promotion calendar aligning with clusters |
| Sizes independent of arrival times | A large event is not more or less likely after a long gap | Correlation between size and preceding interval; regression of size on elapsed time | Emergency orders after a stock-out are small; accumulation orders after a gap are large |
| Sizes i.i.d. over time | No serial dependence in size | Autocorrelation of sizes at lags 1 to 3; Ljung–Box | A batch-buying customer ordering the same large quantity every quarter |

Report the tests and their results next to the parameters. A model fitted without them is one whose
central assumption is untested, and the reader cannot tell whether that is because the test passed
or because nobody ran it.

### Phase B — Estimate the arrival rate
**Step 5. Count periods, not events.** With periodic data you observe the weekly total, not the
individual events, so you cannot count arrivals. This is the most important practical point in the
topic, and the one planning systems most often get wrong: they treat each non-zero week as one
demand occurrence, which understates `λ` whenever a week can contain two arrivals.

**Step 6. Apply the fraction-of-zeros estimator.** Under Poisson arrivals the probability of no
arrival in a period is `e^−λ`, so `λ̂ = −ln(n₀/n)`. For the worked example, `n₀/n = 0.54`, so
`λ̂ = −ln 0.54 = 0.6162` events per week. The estimator needs only the zero share, which is robust,
and it is distribution-free with respect to the size law, which is why Prak et al. (2021) recommend
it as the default. It is closed form, so a spreadsheet can compute it and a reader can audit it.

> **Note:** The estimator is biased upward in finite samples by roughly `(e^λ − 1)/(2n)`. At
> `n = 100` and `λ = 0.62` that is about `0.86/200 = 0.004`, negligible against the sampling error.

**Step 7. Attach a confidence interval.** The zero share is a proportion, so its uncertainty
propagates through the logarithm. With `p̂₀ = 0.54` and `n = 100`, `se(p̂₀) = sqrt(0.54 × 0.46/100) =
0.0498` and `se(λ̂) = 0.0498/0.54 = 0.0923`. A 95 per cent Wald interval is `0.616 ± 1.96 × 0.092`,
or `[0.435, 0.797]` events per week — a factor of 1.8 between the ends, and the honest answer for
100 weeks. An exact interval, obtained by applying Clopper–Pearson bounds to the zero share and
transforming, is asymmetric and preferable at this sample size.

The width has a consequence you must report. Implied mean lead-time demand is 6.98 units at the
lower bound and 12.79 at the upper, so a safety stock set at the point estimate carries a factor of
nearly two of uncertainty in the quantity it protects against. That is not a defect of the method;
it is the information content of 100 weeks on a slow mover. Check the Poisson assumption the same
way: under `λ = 0.62` zero runs should be roughly geometric, and a histogram too heavy at long runs
indicates a rate varying over time, which calls for segmenting the history rather than fitting
harder.

### Phase C — Estimate the size distribution
**Step 8. Do not use the mean of the non-zero periods.** This is the most common error in the
literature and in practice, and it has a closed form: the expected non-zero period is
`μλ/(1 − e^−λ)`. The factor exceeds 1 for every positive `λ`, equals 1 only in the limit of
vanishing arrivals, and grows without bound:

| λ (events per period) | Factor `λ/(1−e^−λ)` | Overstatement of mean size |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.10 | 1.05 | 5% |
| 0.30 | 1.16 | 16% |
| 0.62 | 1.34 | 34% |
| 1.00 | 1.58 | 58% |
| 1.50 | 1.93 | 93% |
| 2.50 | 2.72 | 172% |
| 4.00 | 4.07 | 307% |

Read it twice, because the ordering is counter-intuitive. The error is *smallest* for the slowest
movers and *largest* for the moderately fast ones. Items at `λ` near 1 to 2 are the bulk of a
distributor's catalogue — too fast for a normal distribution to be safe, too slow to be treated as
pure spares — and they are where the naive estimator does most damage.

**Step 9. Choose a size family, knowing what each one is for.** Each family encodes a claim about
the tail, and each fails a different diagnostic.

| Family | Support | What it captures | Diagnostic it usually fails | Inventory symptom |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gamma | Continuous, positive | Right skew with a mode above zero; flexible shape | Upper-tail fit: KS on the top decile, or a P-P plot departing above the 90th percentile | Under-buys the very large order; service misses on the few events that matter |
| Lognormal | Continuous, positive | Heavy right tail; multiplicative effects | Lower-tail fit: cannot represent a minimum order quantity, so small sizes are misfitted | Over-buys small events, under-buys the mode |
| Weibull | Continuous, positive | Flexible hazard; shape below 1 gives a decreasing hazard | Modal fit when shape is below 1; the mode is pushed to the smallest size | Distorts the most common event size |
| Negative binomial | Counts | Overdispersion relative to Poisson; a Gamma-mixed Poisson | The VMR: fails when it is near 1 (Poisson is adequate) or below 1 (underdispersed) | Wrong variance, hence wrong safety stock |
| Poisson | Counts | Equidispersion | The VMR, almost always, for real demand | Systematically too little safety stock |
| Empirical | Whatever was observed | Nothing beyond the sample | The tail, whenever the sample is short | Tail truncated at the sample maximum |

Two findings shape how much weight to put on a good fit. Syntetos, Babai and Altay (2012) tested
candidate distributions on roughly 13,000 stock-keeping units (SKUs) across military and electronics
datasets and distinguished *distributional validity* from *utility*: a family can win on fit and
lose on service. Turrini and Meissner (2017) fitted distributions to about 4,000 German
renewable-energy items and about 5,000 Royal Air Force items using Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) tests,
and introduced a right-tail-weighted variant on the argument that the tail is what safety stock is
for.

**Step 10. Fit by maximum likelihood where you can, and say when you cannot.** Method of moments
is acceptable when the family has as many parameters as you have moments and the moments are
stable. For a two-parameter Gamma, matching mean and variance is defensible on a long continuous
sample; for a lognormal it is unstable, because the variance depends on the fourth moment of the
underlying normal. For a compound model fitted to period data the likelihood is the compound-sum
likelihood, not the size likelihood.

### Phase D — Derive the lead-time distribution
**Step 11. Fix the lead time.** `L` is lead time plus review period. If the lead time is genuinely
variable — and for imported goods it usually is — then `L` is random and the demand distribution is
a mixture over `L`, a separate convolution covered in Module 01A topic 11. Here, treat `L` as fixed
and say so; mixing a random `L` with a fixed-`L` formula is a common and quiet error.

**Step 12. Choose the route.** Three are available and they trade assumption against effort.

| Route | How it works | Assumption it adds | When it wins |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parametric convolution | Fit a family, then compute the compound sum analytically or by recursion | The family is right, including in the tail | Long histories, stable processes, extrapolation to unseen horizons |
| Empirical / bootstrap | Resample observed sizes and interarrival gaps | Exchangeability; the observed maximum bounds the tail | Short histories, no defensible family, a need to show the data |
| Simulation | Draw arrivals, draw sizes, sum, repeat | The fitted generative model, plus enough replications | Random lead times, multi-echelon, correlated arrivals, policy simulation |

The evidence is genuinely mixed. Syntetos, Babai and Gardner (2015) compared simple parametric
methods against the Willemain–Smart–Schwarz bootstrap on more than 7,000 series and concluded that
the bootstrap's complexity was hard to justify on stock-control performance. Hasni, Babai, Aguir
and Jemai (2019) found that for highly intermittent demand and short lead times a parametric
approach outperformed interval-and-size bootstrapping, contrary to earlier claims. Boylan and Babai
(2022) analysed resampling with and without replacement for the lead-time demand cumulative
distribution function (CDF) on 3,000 automotive spare parts: without replacement was better at low
service levels, long lead times and short histories, while with replacement was better at 95–99 per
cent service. There is no universal winner, only a defensible choice given the horizon, the target
and the history length.

**Step 13. Compute the compound sum.** For discrete sizes the Panjer recursion is exact and easy to
audit: `g₀ = exp(−λL)` and `g_k = (λL/k) · Σ_{j=1..k} j·f_j·g_{k−j}`. For continuous sizes,
discretise onto a fine grid and apply the same recursion, or use a fast Fourier transform (FFT)
convolution, or simulate. The moments check any numerical result: `E[S] = λLμ`,
`Var[S] = λL(μ² + σ²)`, and `P(S = 0) = e^−λL`.

Note the variance identity. The variance of lead-time demand depends on the *second moment* of the
size law, not on its variance alone. A size law with a small coefficient of variation but a large
mean can still produce very overdispersed lead-time demand, because each event contributes its
whole size. That is why "demand is fairly stable when it happens" is compatible with "lead-time
demand is extremely variable". Three checks catch almost every implementation error: the computed
mean should equal `λLμ`, the zero probability `e^−λL`, and the variance `λL·E[Z²]`.

**Step 14. Handle a fractional lead time by scaling the rate, not the horizon.** If `L = 3.4`
weeks, use `λ × 3.4`. Do not round to 3 or 4 and then multiply: the difference is 13 per cent of
expected lead-time demand at `λ = 0.62`, larger than the difference between most competing methods.

### Phase E — Read the result for the decision
**Step 15. Decide whether the decision needs a mean or a quantile, and say which.** A point
forecast for squared-error loss is the mean. A reorder point under a newsvendor trade-off is the
quantile at the critical ratio `τ = C_u/(C_u + C_o)`, where `C_u` is the underage cost per unit and
`C_o` the overage cost. A fill-rate target is neither: it is a ratio of expected values and requires
the distribution above the stock level. Three decisions, three functionals, one distribution.

**Step 16. Compute both service measures, because they differ.**

```text
   CSL(S)      = P(D <= S)                    = F_S(S)
   fill(S)     = 1 - E[(D - S)^+] / E[D]
   E[(D-S)^+]  = sum_{k>S} (k - S) * g_k      for discrete demand
```

The gap is not a rounding difference. In the worked example, at a stock level of 17 units, CSL is
86.8 per cent and fill rate is 93.2 per cent; at 19 units, CSL is 91.1 per cent and fill rate is
95.5 per cent. A service target quoted without naming the measure can be met or missed depending on
the reading, and both readings are arithmetically correct.

**Step 17. Propagate the parameter uncertainty, at least roughly.** A fill rate computed at point
estimates is a plug-in estimate and is over-confident. In the worked example the 95 per cent fill
level moves from about 17 units at the upper arrival rate to about 21 at the lower one — a wider
band than the difference between the parametric and bootstrap routes.

### Phase F — Validate and report
**Step 18. Check calibration out of sample, at the decision horizon.** Use a probability integral
transform (PIT) histogram: compute `F(y)` at each realised value using only the information
available before it, and check uniformity. A U-shape means the tails are too thin, an inverted U too
fat, a slope means bias. Report the histogram, not just a score. Then score with a proper rule —
continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) is proper, in the units of the variable, and comparable
across methods on the same item. Module 08 topic 09 treats scoring in detail.

**Step 19. Report the parameters with their uncertainty and their diagnostics.** The card has seven
lines, and a model without them is not a result:

```text
   1. Window and bucket:         item, location, period, n periods
   2. Arrival rate:              lambda_hat [CI], estimator, events per period
   3. Size law:                  family, parameters, estimator, n events
   4. Fit diagnostics:           statistic, value, tail check
   5. Independence tests:        which tests, which passed, which failed
   6. Route to the distribution: parametric / empirical / simulation, and why
   7. Decision outputs:          mean, target quantile, fill at the chosen level
```

Every line is load-bearing. The interval on `λ` stops a precise-looking number from being read as
precise. The event count stops a fit on 100 periods being read as a fit on 100 sizes, when the
events numbered 62. The independence tests tell the reader whether the model's central assumption
was checked.

## 6. Worked example
One spare part at one warehouse, weekly buckets, 100 weeks: a hydraulic seal used in three machine
models. All figures are constructed to be internally consistent; the item is synthetic.

### 6.1 The sufficient statistics
| Quantity | Value | Unit |
|---|---:|---|
| Periods in window (`n`) | 100 | weeks |
| Periods with demand | 46 | weeks |
| Periods with no demand (`n₀`) | 54 | weeks |
| Total demand | 247 | eaches |
| Mean demand per week (`x̄`) | 2.47 | eaches per week |
| Sample variance of weekly demand (`s²`) | 10.9 | eaches² |
| Index of dispersion, `99 × s² / x̄` | 437 | chi-square statistic, 99 df |
| Largest weekly demand | 14 | eaches |
| ADI (periods per demand week) | 2.17 | weeks per demand week |
| CV² of non-zero weeks | 0.141 | dimensionless |
| Class (Module 08 topic 05 grid) | intermittent | ADI ≥ 1.32, CV² < 0.49 |

The item is intermittent: demand in about half the weeks, and a size that does not vary much when
it comes. That classification describes the weekly series. It does not give the arrival rate, and
the next step shows why the obvious estimate of that rate is wrong.

### 6.2 Estimating the arrival rate
A naive analyst counts demand weeks and divides by the window: `46/100 = 0.46` events per week.
That is the occurrence probability — the probability a week contains at least one event. The
arrival rate must satisfy `1 − e^−λ = 0.46`, so `λ̂ = −ln 0.54 = 0.6162` events per week.

The two numbers differ by 34 per cent, and the difference is not rounding. It is the probability
that a week contains two or more events, which at `λ = 0.616` is
`1 − e^−λ(1 + λ) = 1 − 0.540 × 1.616 = 0.127`: about 13 weeks in 100 carry two or more arrivals.
Those weeks are why the mean of the non-zero weeks is so much larger than the mean event size. The
confidence interval is `[0.435, 0.797]` events per week, and it should be reported every time: alone
the point estimate looks exact, with the interval it looks like what it is.

### 6.3 The size estimate, naive and corrected
The naive mean size is `247/46 = 5.370` eaches per event; the corrected one is `2.47/0.6162 = 4.008`.
The ratio `5.370/4.008 = 1.340` matches `λ/(1 − e^−λ) = 0.6162/0.46 = 1.3396` to four significant
figures — the identity from step 8, and the cheapest check that your two estimators agree.

The consequence is direct. A planner using 5.37 as the mean event size, with `λ = 0.616` from the
zero fraction, computes a four-week expectation of `0.6162 × 4 × 5.370 = 13.23` eaches against the
correct `9.88`. The order-up-to level is 34 per cent too high before any service factor, and the
fill rate will exceed the target — the 98–99 against 95 pattern Prak et al. (2021) report. Nobody
complains about over-service until someone looks at the inventory.

### 6.4 The size law, stated explicitly
The size law is an empirical discrete distribution on the integers 1 to 7, in eaches:

| Size (eaches) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Probability | 0.05 | 0.12 | 0.20 | 0.26 | 0.20 | 0.12 | 0.05 |
| `j · f_j` | 0.05 | 0.24 | 0.60 | 1.04 | 1.00 | 0.72 | 0.35 |
| `j² · f_j` | 0.05 | 0.48 | 1.80 | 4.16 | 5.00 | 4.32 | 2.45 |

The moments are `μ = 4.00` and `E[Z²] = 18.26`, so `σ² = 2.26` and the coefficient of variation of
the size is 0.376. A discrete law is used deliberately: it makes the recursion exact and
hand-checkable. A Gamma or lognormal fit would require the numerical route and lose its
auditability without gaining anything for the argument.

### 6.5 The lead-time distribution
Lead time is 4 weeks, review period included, so `L = 4` and `λL = 2.4648`. Applying the Panjer
recursion:

| `k` (eaches) | `g_k` | `F_S(k)` | | `k` (eaches) | `g_k` | `F_S(k)` |
|---:|---:|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0.0850 | 0.0850 | | 13 | 0.0444 | 0.7324 |
| 1 | 0.0105 | 0.0955 | | 14 | 0.0404 | 0.7728 |
| 2 | 0.0258 | 0.1213 | | 15 | 0.0361 | 0.8089 |
| 3 | 0.0450 | 0.1663 | | 16 | 0.0316 | 0.8405 |
| 4 | 0.0636 | 0.2299 | | 17 | 0.0273 | 0.8678 |
| 5 | 0.0618 | 0.2917 | | 18 | 0.0234 | 0.8911 |
| 6 | 0.0591 | 0.3508 | | 19 | 0.0199 | 0.9110 |
| 7 | 0.0584 | 0.4092 | | 20 | 0.0168 | 0.9278 |
| 8 | 0.0578 | 0.4670 | | 21 | 0.0141 | 0.9419 |
| 9 | 0.0607 | 0.5277 | | 22 | 0.0116 | 0.9535 |
| 10 | 0.0584 | 0.5861 | | 23 | 0.0095 | 0.9630 |
| 11 | 0.0535 | 0.6396 | | 24 | 0.0078 | 0.9708 |
| 12 | 0.0484 | 0.6880 | | ≥25 | — | 1.0000 |

The distribution has a mode at zero — 8.5 per cent of four-week windows see no demand at all — a
broad plateau from 4 to 12, and a slowly decaying tail. The mean is 9.86 units and the standard
deviation is 6.71. The modal value is zero and the mean is nearly ten: the mean is a quantity that
is never demanded and rarely approached. That is not a paradox. It is what a compound distribution
looks like.

### 6.6 The same mean, two different distributions
Take a Poisson distribution with the same mean of 9.86 units per four weeks. It describes a
different world: demand arrives every period with a small size instead of rarely with a large one.

| Stock level `S` | Compound Poisson CSL | Compound Poisson fill | Poisson CSL | Poisson fill |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 12 | 68.8% | 81.4% | 79.1% | 93.4% |
| 14 | 77.3% | 87.3% | 92.4% | 98.3% |
| 17 | 86.8% | 93.2% | 98.9% | 99.8% |
| 19 | 91.1% | 95.5% | 99.7% | 99.98% |

At a stock level of 14 units the two processes differ by 11 percentage points of fill rate while
sharing an identical mean. At a 90 per cent cycle service level the compound process needs 19 units
and the Poisson process 14. Five units at the same expected demand, and no amount of point-forecast
accuracy would have revealed the difference, because the point forecast is identical. That table is
the argument of this topic in one object: the mean is a property of both, the service level of only
one.

### 6.7 What a Croston-type method says about the same item
An SBA forecast would land near `0.95 × 2.47 = 2.35` eaches per week, or 9.4 units over four weeks —
close to the correct mean of 9.88, and close for a good reason: a rate estimator estimates a rate,
and the mean of the compound process is `λμ`. On the quantity it produces, the method is doing its
job. What it cannot do is the following, and each row is a real request:

| Request | Croston / SBA / TSB | Compound Poisson |
|---|---|---|
| Expected demand over four weeks | 9.4 units | 9.88 units |
| 90th percentile of four-week demand | not available | 19 units |
| Fill rate at a stock level of 17 | not available | 93.2% |
| Expected units short at a stock level of 17 | not available | 0.72 units |
| Effect of doubling the review period | rate × 8, assuming the rate holds | recompute with `λL`; the distribution widens non-linearly |
| Effect of a promotion that doubles arrivals | invisible | visible, if the arrival rate is re-estimated |

The two methods are not competing for the same job. The rate method is a cheap, robust estimator of
one functional; the compound model describes the process and yields every functional at a cost in
assumptions. Section 9 reviews the evidence on when that cost is worth paying.

## 7. Code, query, or calculation
The calculation below is the Panjer recursion for the worked example, by hand. It is the most
useful thing to be able to reproduce without a computer, because it is the check any software
implementation has to pass.

```text
lambda_hat = -ln(54/100) = 0.6162 events/week
lambda_hat * L = 0.6162 * 4 = 2.4648

g0 = exp(-2.4648) = 0.08503

j*f_j for j = 1..7:  0.05  0.24  0.60  1.04  1.00  0.72  0.35

g1 = (2.4648/1) * (0.05*0.08503)                                    = 0.01048
g2 = (2.4648/2) * (0.05*0.01048 + 0.24*0.08503)                     = 0.02579
g3 = (2.4648/3) * (0.05*0.02579 + 0.24*0.01048 + 0.60*0.08503)      = 0.04504
g4 = (2.4648/4) * (0.05*0.04504 + 0.24*0.02579
                   + 0.60*0.01048 + 1.04*0.08503)                    = 0.06357
g5 = (2.4648/5) * (0.05*0.06357 + 0.24*0.04504 + 0.60*0.02579
                   + 1.04*0.01048 + 1.00*0.08503)                    = 0.06181

check 1 (mean):      sum k*g_k         = 9.859 = 2.4648 * 4.00
check 2 (variance):  sum k^2*g_k - m^2 = 45.01 = 2.4648 * 18.26
check 3 (zero mass): g0                = 0.08503 = exp(-2.4648)
check 4 (total mass): sum g_k          = 1.0000 (to four decimals at k = 60)
```

Each check tests a different part of the pipeline: the mean check the parameter transmission, the
variance check the second moment of the size law, the zero-mass check the arrival rate, the
total-mass check the recursion's normalisation. An implementation passing all four is almost
certainly correct up to discretisation error; one passing none will still produce a plausible
service level. The recursion is `O(k²)` for `k` grid points, instant at these sizes and slow on a
fine continuous grid — where the step must be small enough that halving it moves the 95th
percentile by less than a tenth of a unit.

## 8. Data, standards, and regulatory context
No standard defines the compound Poisson model, the hurdle model, the Panjer recursion or the
fill-rate formula. There is no ISO, ANSI or IEC document fixing the estimator for `λ` or the choice
of size family. What the standards constrain is the **input**: the identity of the series, the
completeness of the zero grid, the timestamp of the event, and the meaning of the service
commitment the forecast feeds. Those constraints are what make the model auditable, and they are
usually the part that is missing.

### 8.1 Identity and event data
| Standard | Governs | What it constrains here | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| GS1 GTIN (Global Trade Item Number) | Product identity | The item dimension; a pack-size change is a new GTIN, splitting one arrival history into two shorter ones and biasing both | B |
| GS1 GLN (Global Location Number) | Party and location identity | The location dimension; a warehouse re-code creates a false zero history and a false new series | B |
| GS1 SSCC (Serial Shipping Container Code) | Logistics unit identity | Reconstructing receipts when demand must be derived from movement rather than orders | B |
| EPCIS 2.0 (GS1) | Event data: what, where, when, why | Timestamped consumption events, the cleanest basis for an arrival process not censored by shipment batching | B |
| ISO 8000 series | Data quality | Completeness and consistency of the item, location and period keys a dense grid depends on | B |

EPCIS matters more here than in most of this programme. An arrival rate is estimated from the
timing of events, and timing requires timestamps with a known meaning. An order line with a
creation date is not an event; it is an intention with a date. A scan, a goods issue or a
consumption record is an event. If the only data available are order dates, the process you
estimate is the ordering process, and the two differ by every batching rule in the customer's
purchasing department.

### 8.2 Statistical vocabulary and metrology
| Standard | Scope | Relationship to this topic | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| ISO 3534-1 | Statistics — vocabulary and symbols, probability and general statistical terms | Supplies the meanings of estimator, bias, consistency and confidence used here | B |
| ISO 5725-1:2023 | Accuracy (trueness and precision) of measurement methods | Defines accuracy for a measuring instrument; does not define forecast accuracy | B |
| JCGM 200:2008 (VIM) | International vocabulary of metrology | Distinguishes accuracy, precision, trueness and bias, which forecast practice uses loosely | B |
| ISO 8000 series | Data quality | Constrains the inputs, not the model | B |

The absence of a forecasting standard has a practical consequence. Two teams can fit the same
family to the same data with different estimators, report both as "the compound Poisson model", and
get service levels differing by several percentage points. The fix is a published method note, not
a better fit.

### 8.3 Asset identity, installed base and contract
| Requirement | Jurisdiction | Relationship to this topic | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unique Device Identification (UDI), 21 CFR Part 830, with the Global Unique Device Identification Database (GUDID) | United States, medical devices | Stable device identity, which makes an installed-base join possible | B |
| UDI under Regulation (EU) 2017/745 (MDR), Article 27 | European Union | The same, phased by device class, with the Eudamed database | B |
| Service level agreement (SLA) | Contract | Names the service measure; a 95 per cent fill target is not a 95 per cent cycle service level, and the model must produce the one the contract names | B |

For spare parts the arrival rate is not physically free: it is approximately the installed base
times a failure rate per unit per period, and that relationship predicts obsolescence better than
any run of zeros, because it tells you when the last unit of a model leaves service. The contract
determines which functional you compute — one paying on uptime implies a much higher critical ratio
than one paying on parts delivered.

### 8.4 Convention rather than requirement
Several things that feel like rules are habits: the choice of Gamma over lognormal is a convention
in parts of the spare-parts literature, not a finding; "fit the size distribution to the non-zero
periods" is a convention inherited from software and is wrong under a compound model whenever `λ`
is not small; the 1.32 and 0.49 classification cut-offs are conventions from one study's loss
function; and a 95 per cent service target is a policy set without reference to the critical ratio.
An analyst who can separate these from requirements can re-derive them. One who cannot will defend
a default as a property of the data.

## 9. Industry practice and case evidence
### 9.1 The origin of the model
The compound Poisson entered inventory theory as the **stuttering Poisson**, named by Galliher and
formalised in Galliher, Morse and Simond (1959), "Dynamics of Two Classes of Continuous-Review
Inventory Systems", *Operations Research* 7(3), 362–384. The paper treats Poisson arrivals of
demand bursts with geometrically distributed sizes — the Poisson–geometric compound, also called
the Pólya–Aeppli distribution — and solves continuous-review systems for it. Morse (1959), in the
same volume, credits Galliher with the name in an MIT interim report. (Grade A for the origin; the
terminology history is documented in the secondary literature.)

It was not invented by statisticians looking for a flexible family. It was invented by inventory
theorists who needed a demand process that could produce zeros and bursts, because the Poisson
process alone could not. That is worth remembering when someone presents the compound Poisson as an
elaborate alternative to a simple method: it is the simple method's ancestor.

### 9.2 The inconsistency result, and what it costs
Prak, Teunter, Babai, Boylan and Syntetos published the most directly relevant result for
practitioners, first as a University of Groningen SOM research report in 2018 and then as "Robust
compound Poisson parameter estimation for inventory control" in *Omega* 104 (2021), article 102481.
Their findings, as reported:

| Finding | Consequence | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Croston-type methods estimate the mean of the *non-zero periods*, not the mean event size; asymptotically the estimate converges to `μλ/(1−e^−λ)`, which always exceeds `μ` | Mean size is overstated whenever `λ` is not small | A |
| The arrival rate implied by these methods converges to `1−e^−λ` rather than `λ`, so it is understated | The two errors partly offset in the mean, and do not offset in the distribution | A |
| The resulting order-up-to levels achieve about 98–99 per cent fill against a 95 per cent target in a case with `λ = 1` | Excess inventory, invisible in a service report | A |
| The proposed estimator `λ̂ = −ln(n₀/n)`, `μ̂ = x̄/λ̂` is closed form, distribution-free with respect to the size law, and close to maximum likelihood | A defensible default a spreadsheet can implement | A |
| Standard method-of-moments estimators converge slowly, with 5–13 per cent bias in `λ` at `n = 200` in their study | Moment matching is not a safe default for slow movers | A |
| In a case study of 404 airline parts, standard moment estimators lost roughly 35–40 per cent accuracy under weekly aggregation of daily data, against about 10 per cent for the proposed estimator | Aggregation is not neutral for parameter estimation | A |

The paper also names Forecast Pro, Oracle, SAP APO, SAS and Slimstock as systems fitting compound
Poisson distributions on the basis the paper criticises. Treat that attribution as the authors'
statement about the systems as they understood them, not as an audit of current releases; vendor
software changes and the claim is dated 2021.

### 9.3 Which distribution fits, and whether fit is the point
| Study | Data | Finding | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Syntetos, Babai and Altay (2012), *International Journal of Production Research* 50(8) | About 13,000 SKUs, military and electronics | Candidate families must be judged on stock-control utility, not only goodness of fit; the two diverge | A |
| Turrini and Meissner (2017), *European Journal of Operational Research* | About 4,000 German renewable-energy items and about 5,000 Royal Air Force items | KS goodness of fit, plus a right-tail-weighted variant, because the tail is what safety stock covers | A |
| Ünlü and Rossetti (2009), IERC proceedings | Simulated and empirical lead-time demand | Fill rates are overestimated and backorders underestimated when the wrong family is used; negative binomial and moment-matching selection rules perform well when the true law is Gamma | C |
| Rossetti and Ünlü (2011), *International Journal of Production Economics* 134(1) | Robustness study across families | Distribution-selection rules show promise against any single fixed family | A |
| Conceição et al. (2015), *Production Planning & Control*, as summarised in the search material | Steel-industry maintenance, repair and operations (MRO) parts, seven years | Laplace and Gamma models performed best on cost; a compound Poisson approach performed worst in that setting | A− |
| Larsen and Thorstenson (2007), *Journal of the Operational Research Society* | Base-stock system under a compound renewal process | With negative binomial order sizes, the shape parameter governs the gap between order fill rate and volume fill rate | A |

The Conceição row needs care. It is a single MRO case in which a compound Poisson approach
underperformed simpler families on a cost criterion, so it does not contradict the rest of the
literature; but it does mean that "compound Poisson is the theoretically correct model" is not a
sufficient argument for using it. Section 11 lists the conditions under which it is.

### 9.4 Correlation, the assumption that fails quietly
| Study | Design | Finding | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Altay, Litteral and Rudisill (2012), *International Journal of Production Economics* 135(1) | 4,588 United States Defense Logistics Agency aircraft parts | About 35 per cent showed significant size–interval cross-correlation at the 5 per cent level; 27 per cent had autocorrelated sizes; 22 per cent autocorrelated intervals | A |
| Biller, Akçay, Corlu and Tayur (2014), Winter Simulation Conference | Finite-sample tests for dependence in intermittent demand | Proposes tests weighing the cost of correlation-parameter uncertainty; they reject independence less often than tests based on the sampling distribution alone | C |
| Pennings, van Dalen and van der Laan (2017), *European Journal of Operational Research* 258(3) | Five empirical spare-parts datasets | Demand size rises with elapsed time since the last demand; conditioning on elapsed time improved inventory performance | A |

The Altay numbers are the ones to carry into a meeting. Roughly a third of a large military
spare-parts population showed a dependence the standard model assumes away. If your catalogue
resembles theirs — and most spare-parts catalogues do — independence is not a formality. It is a
claim with a one-in-three chance of being false for a given item.

### 9.5 Parametric, empirical or simulated: the evidence is split
| Study | Comparison | Finding | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Willemain, Smart and Schwarz (2004), *International Journal of Forecasting* | Bootstrap against exponential smoothing and Croston on more than 28,000 items | The bootstrap gave better lead-time demand distribution estimates | A |
| Syntetos, Babai and Gardner (2015), *Journal of Business Research* | Simple parametric against the bootstrap on more than 7,000 series | The bootstrap's complexity was hard to justify; simple methods performed well on stock-control criteria | A |
| Hasni, Babai, Aguir and Jemai (2019), *International Journal of Production Economics* | Two bootstrapping schemes against a parametric approach | For highly intermittent demand and short lead times, the parametric approach outperformed interval-and-size bootstrapping | A |
| Boylan and Babai (2022), *International Journal of Production Economics* | Bootstrap with and without replacement, 3,000 automotive parts | Without replacement was better at low service, long lead times and short histories; with replacement was better at 95–99 per cent service | A |
| Teunter and Duncan (2009), *Journal of the Operational Research Society* | Royal Air Force data | On service and stock-holding criteria, Croston variants and bootstrapping beat moving average and simple exponential smoothing | A |

The pattern is not a contradiction; it is a statement about scope. The bootstrap wins when the
history contains the tail, the target is high, and the family is wrong in the tail; it loses when
the history is short, the lead time short, the intermittency severe, and the resampling scheme
biased. The practical rule is to run both on your own data at your own service target.

### 9.6 Machine-learning two-part models
A recent strand treats the hurdle as a supervised learning problem: a classifier for occurrence
and a regressor for size. Rožanec et al. (2022), "Reframing Demand Forecasting: A Two-Fold Approach
for Lumpy and Intermittent Demand" in *Sustainability* 14(15), article 9295, frames the problem as
separate classification and regression tasks and reports a gradient-boosted classifier with an area
under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.97 on their data. A 2026 *PLOS One* paper, "A
two stage statistical framework for cold start spare part demand forecasting", DOI
10.1371/journal.pone.0350729, combines a classifier, a Gamma size model and Monte Carlo simulation,
and reports a continuous ranked probability score of 3.27 against 15.41 for a deep autoregressive
benchmark, with a 6.4 per cent mean absolute error improvement over a single-stage gradient-boosted
model.

These are promising and not yet a settled alternative. The claims are on specific datasets with
specific baselines, and the structural insight — separating occurrence from size — is the same one
this topic has been making since 1959. What the machine-learning versions add is covariates and
non-linear occurrence models, not a new decomposition. Grade A for the published papers.

## 10. Failure modes and red flags
| Failure | Red flag visible in a meeting or a dataset | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Non-zero periods treated as demand events | The number of "demand occurrences" equals the number of non-zero weeks exactly | Multiple arrivals in one period are collapsed | Arrival rate understated by the multi-arrival probability | Estimate `λ` from the zero fraction; state the clock |
| Mean size taken as the mean of non-zero periods | Mean size times arrival rate does not equal mean demand | The conditional mean of a period is not the mean of an event | Mean size overstated by `λ/(1−e^−λ)`; order-up-to level too high | Use `μ̂ = x̄/λ̂`; check the identity |
| Occurrence probability used as an arrival rate | A parameter between 0 and 1 is used in `λL` | The two differ by `−ln(1−p)/p` | Understated lead-time demand; under-stocking | Name the parameter in the code and the report |
| Arrival rate reported without an interval | A four-significant-figure rate from 60 observations | Sampling error is large for rare events | False precision; the decision is treated as certain | Report the interval; propagate it to the service level |
| Size distribution fitted to period totals | The fit reports `n` equal to the number of periods | Period totals are compound sums, not sizes | Parameters estimate a different object | Fit to events, or fit the compound likelihood |
| A family chosen for convenience | "We use Gamma everywhere" | Families differ in the tail, the decision-relevant part | Systematic bias in the 90th percentile and above | Choose per item class; report the tail diagnostic |
| Goodness of fit treated as adequacy | A KS p-value above 0.05 is the only diagnostic shown | Fit and utility are different properties | A well-fitting family can still produce bad service | Add a service or cost backtest |
| Independence assumed and never tested | No correlation statistics in the method note | Correlation biases both parameters | Errors whose sign depends on the correlation | Run the three tests in step 4; report them |
| Normal approximation used at low `λL` | A negative demand percentile appears in a table | The normal has support on the whole line; the compound sum does not | Materially wrong tail probabilities | Use the exact or recursive distribution below `λL` of about 10 |
| Lead time rounded to a whole period | `L = 3.7` weeks becomes `L = 4` | Rounding changes the rate-times-horizon product | A 13 per cent error at `λ = 0.62` | Scale the rate, not the horizon |
| CSL and fill rate reported interchangeably | One number is labelled "service" with no definition | The two functionals differ by several points | A target met on one measure, missed on the other | Compute both; name the one the contract uses |
| Bootstrap applied to a short history | The resampled maximum equals the observed maximum | The empirical distribution cannot exceed the sample | Tail truncated exactly where the service target lives | Use a parametric tail above the sample maximum |
| Promotion periods left in the history | Arrivals cluster in a known calendar window | The arrival rate is an average over two regimes | Rate estimated for neither regime | Segment; model the promotion separately |
| The model is fitted, then only the mean is used | The distribution is computed and discarded in the report | The decision needs a quantile | Work done, value not captured | Report the decision functional, not the parameters |

Two deserve expansion. **Mean size taken as the mean of non-zero periods** is the failure Prak et
al. measured, and it is invisible because the model still reproduces mean demand exactly — the two
errors offset. What it does not reproduce is the variance, and therefore the service level. **The
normal approximation at low arrival counts** survives longest, because at moderate `λL` the
approximation is fine and nobody rechecks it when the catalogue's slow items are added.

## 11. Assumptions and limits
| Assumption | Why it is needed | What breaks if it fails | How to test it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrivals follow a Poisson process | Gives `N(L) ~ Poisson(λL)` and the zero-probability estimator | Clustering, trend or seasonality biases `λ` and invalidates the convolution | Dispersion test; runs test on zero/non-zero; interarrival histogram against exponential |
| Sizes are independent of arrival times | Makes the compound sum separable | Emergency orders are small, accumulation orders large; the mean size is a mixture | Correlation between size and preceding interval |
| Sizes are i.i.d. over time | Makes the size law a single object | A customer who batch-buys quarterly produces a multimodal size law | Autocorrelation of sizes; Ljung–Box |
| Sizes are independent across items, or dependence is modelled | Justifies fitting each series separately | Substitutes and complements contaminate each other's histories | Cross-correlation of residuals at a location |
| The process is stationary over the window | The historical rate is the future rate | A listing change, competitor exit or redesign shifts the rate | Split the window; test for a break; join to installed base |
| The size law is right, including its tail | The tail drives every service level above about 80 per cent | The quantile is wrong by several units even when the fit looks good | Tail-weighted KS; P-P plot above the 90th percentile |
| Demand is observed, not censored | The arrival rate must reflect the market, not the stock policy | Stock-outs suppress events; `λ` is understated and the model reproduces the stock-out | Availability flags; compare demand in available and unavailable periods |
| The bucket is the decision period | The parameters are properties of the bucket | Class and rate change with aggregation; a weekly model for a daily decision is wrong by the ratio | Compare the forecasting bucket with the replenishment cycle |
| Lead time is fixed, or its randomness is modelled separately | The convolution is over demand only | Mixing a fixed-`L` demand distribution with a variable `L` understates the tail | Compare lead-time variance with demand variance; if comparable, model the mixture |
| The history is long enough to estimate two parameters | `λ` and the size law both need events, not periods | Wide intervals and unstable fits; the initialisation dominates | Count events; report the interval width |

Three limits deserve statement. **The model describes a process, not a law of the item.** A compound
Poisson fit says the history is consistent with Poisson arrivals and an i.i.d. size law; it does
not say the future will be, and the arrival rate is the most fragile parameter because a listing
change, a promotion or a competitor exit moves it first. **Identification depends on the clock
assumption.** With weekly data you cannot see individual arrivals; the decomposition into `λ` and
`μ` is identified by the Poisson zero probability, which is an assumption, not an observation. If
arrivals are not Poisson the two parameters are not separately identified from the marginal
distribution, and any decomposition is a modelling choice rather than an estimate. **A distribution
is not a decision.** It is an input to a policy that also needs a review period, a replenishment
rule, a lead-time distribution, a cost structure and a service definition, and errors in any of
those can dominate the error in the distribution. Module 10 treats the policy; this topic stops at
the distribution on purpose.

### Exercise 6
The models are arithmetically consistent because they agree on the weekly marginal. Under the
hurdle, `P(Y = 0) = 1 − p = 0.54` and `E[Y] = 0.46 × 5.37 = 2.47`. Under the compound Poisson,
`P(Y = 0) = e^−0.616 = 0.540` and `E[Y] = 0.616 × 4.01 = 2.47`. The identity `p = 1 − e^−λ` makes
them match on both the zero probability and the mean.

They differ on a week with two orders. The hurdle cannot produce one: its maximum weekly demand is
the maximum of the size law. The compound Poisson produces one with probability
`1 − e^−λ(1 + λ) = 0.127`, about 13 weeks in 100, and in those weeks demand is the sum of two sizes.
The hurdle compensates by giving each single event a larger mean, 5.37 against 4.01, which is
exactly the naive estimator from step 8. The hurdle's size law is the *period-conditional* law, not
the *event* law, and reading it as the event law is the error.

The difference matters at any horizon longer than one bucket, and for any question about the number
of orders rather than the quantity. An order-frequency or capacity question — how many picks, how
many shipments — needs the arrival rate, which the hurdle does not have.

### Exercise 7
A strong protocol names three null hypotheses. Arrivals are Poisson: index of dispersion on weekly
counts, `(n−1)s²/x̄` against chi-square with `n−1` degrees of freedom, plus a runs test on the
zero/non-zero sequence. Sizes are independent of the preceding interval: Pearson correlation
between size and elapsed time, with a bootstrap interval because the distribution is skewed. Sizes
are serially independent: autocorrelation at lags 1 to 3 and a Ljung–Box statistic.

The promotion calendar is used two ways. Segment into promotion and non-promotion weeks and fit the
parameters separately; if the arrival rates differ materially, the pooled fit describes neither
regime. Then test whether promotion weeks alone show a size–interval correlation, since a promotion
bringing forward both small and large orders will.

The protocol must say what happens on rejection: segment, add a covariate to the occurrence model,
model the correlation explicitly, or fall back to an empirical route that does not require
independence. It must not say "investigate further", which is not a rule. The reporting format is a
table with one row per test, the statistic, the threshold, the decision, and the action taken.

### Exercise 8
A strong algorithm runs in four stages: draw a lead time `L` from its fitted distribution; draw the
number of arrivals `N` over `L`, jointly with `L` if arrivals correlate with it; draw `N` sizes
from the size law, using a copula or a conditional law if size depends on the interval; sum and
store. Repeat `M` times.

The replication count follows from a standard error argument. The Monte Carlo standard error of an
estimated 95th percentile is roughly `sqrt(p(1−p)/M)/f(q)`, where `f(q)` is the density at the
quantile. For a target precision of 0.1 units on a quantile with local density 0.02 per unit,
`M ≈ 0.95 × 0.05 / (0.01 × 0.0004) = 118,750`, so 100,000 to 200,000 replications is the right
order; reporting 10,000 and a two-decimal quantile is false precision.

The three checks: simulated mean against the analytic mean; simulated zero probability against
`e^−λL`; stability of the 95th percentile across two independent seeds. The third catches an
inadequate replication count, which the first two cannot.

## 14. Interview questions
**1. "What is a compound Poisson demand process?"**
A strong answer describes two stages: Poisson arrivals at rate `λ`, and an independent size drawn
from `F` per arrival, with period demand the sum of the sizes that arrived. It gives `λμ`, `λE[Z²]`
and `e^−λL`, and says the value is a distribution rather than a rate. A weak answer says "Poisson
demand with variable order sizes" without saying what is independent of what.

**2. "Why can't you use the mean of the non-zero periods as the mean order size?"**
A strong answer gives the identity: the expected non-zero period is `μλ/(1 − e^−λ)`, exceeding `μ`
whenever a period can hold more than one arrival, with the factor at a concrete `λ`, and notes the
error is largest at moderate rates rather than for the slowest items. A weak answer says "biased"
without direction or magnitude.

**3. "How do you estimate the arrival rate from weekly data?"**
A strong answer says you cannot count arrivals, so you use the Poisson zero probability,
`λ̂ = −ln(n₀/n)`, attach a confidence interval, note the estimator is distribution-free with respect
to the size law, and mention the upward finite-sample bias of order `1/n`. A weak answer divides
non-zero weeks by weeks, which estimates the occurrence probability and understates `λ`.

**4. "What does a hurdle model assume that a compound Poisson model does not?"**
A strong answer says the hurdle assumes at most one event per period, with Bernoulli occurrence and
a zero-truncated size law, and identifies an occurrence probability rather than an arrival rate. It
notes the two coincide at the fitted bucket when `p = 1 − e^−λ` and diverge at fractional horizons.
A weak answer calls the hurdle a computational convenience with no substantive difference.

**5. "Why does the choice of size distribution matter if the mean is the same?"**
A strong answer says the mean depends on `μ` alone, the variance on `E[Z²]`, and the tail on the
whole law, so two families matched on the mean can differ by several units at the 95th percentile,
and it gives a concrete gap. A weak answer says the choice matters "for accuracy", which is not a
mechanism.

**6. "How would you compute a fill rate from a demand distribution?"**
A strong answer gives `1 − E[(D−S)⁺]/E[D]`, distinguishes it from `F_S(S)`, notes the
expected-shortfall term needs the distribution above the stock level rather than its mean, and says
fill rates are often overestimated when the family is wrong. A weak answer equates fill rate with
the probability of not stocking out.

**7. "A model fits the data well by Kolmogorov–Smirnov. Is it good enough to set stock?"**
A strong answer says no, because fit and utility are different properties: a family can pass a KS
test and still misestimate the service-determining tail, and KS is not tail-weighted. It proposes a
tail check and a service backtest. A weak answer treats the p-value as an answer.

**8. "What breaks if arrivals are correlated with sizes?"**
A strong answer names the mechanism: promotions, allocation rules and batch buying produce both more
events and larger ones, so `λ` and `μ` estimate the wrong quantities. It cites the prevalence
evidence — roughly a third of one large military parts population — and the three tests. A weak
answer says the model "becomes inaccurate" without a direction or a test.

**9. "Parametric, bootstrap or simulation: which do you choose?"**
A strong answer states the trade-off in history length, service target and horizon, and cites the
disagreement rather than picking a winner: bootstrap wins when the sample holds the tail and the
target is high, parametric at short lead times and severe intermittency, simulation when lead time
is random or arrivals are correlated. A weak answer names one method as best.

**10. "How would you report a compound Poisson fit to a planning director?"**
A strong answer gives the seven-line card — window and bucket, arrival rate with interval, size law
with parameters and event count, fit diagnostics with a tail check, independence results, route, and
decision outputs — and says why each line is there. A weak answer reports the mean and the software
name.

**11. "What is the difference between CSL and fill rate, and why does it matter here?"**
A strong answer defines both as functionals of one distribution, gives the expected-shortfall
formula, notes fill rate is usually higher at the same stock level because a stock-out is weighted
by its size, and says the contract decides which is computed. A weak answer treats them as two names
for the same service number.

**12. "When would you not use a compound Poisson model?"**
A strong answer names the conditions: seasonal or promotional arrivals with a non-stationary rate;
sizes correlated with intervals and the correlation unmodelled; too short a history for two
parameters; a target low enough that only the body matters; a simpler empirical route performing as
well. It notes the model is a description, not a requirement. A weak answer says it is always
better because it is more sophisticated.

## 15. Key terms
- **Compound Poisson process** — Poisson arrivals, each carrying an independent random size.
- **Arrival rate (λ)** — expected demand events per period; not the occurrence probability.
- **Demand rate** — expected demand per period, `λ · μ`.
- **Size law (F)** — the distribution of the quantity per event.
- **Compound sum** — the sum of a random number of random sizes.
- **Stuttering Poisson** — the compound Poisson with geometric sizes; the historical name.
- **Closure under convolution** — a sum of independent compound Poissons is compound Poisson.
- **Lead-time demand** — demand over lead time plus review period.
- **Zero fraction** — share of periods with no demand; the input to the arrival-rate estimator.
- **Occurrence probability (p)** — probability a period contains at least one event.
- **Fraction-of-zeros estimator** — `λ̂ = −ln(n₀/n)`, from the Poisson zero probability.
- **Hurdle model** — Bernoulli occurrence plus a zero-truncated size law, one event per period.
- **Zero-inflated model** — a mixture adding mass at zero without excluding zeros from the count law.
- **Gamma distribution** — continuous right-skewed family with a mode above zero.
- **Lognormal distribution** — a family whose logarithm is normal; heavy right tail.
- **Negative binomial** — an overdispersed count family; a Gamma-mixed Poisson.
- **Variance-to-mean ratio (VMR)** — variance divided by the mean; 1 for Poisson.
- **Index of dispersion** — the sample VMR times `n − 1`; a chi-square statistic under Poisson.
- **Overdispersion** — variance above the mean; the signature of compounding.
- **Method of moments (MM)** — matching sample moments to theoretical ones.
- **Maximum likelihood (MLE)** — maximising the probability of the observed data.
- **Consistency** — convergence to the true parameter as the sample grows.
- **Panjer recursion** — a linear-time recursion for the compound sum with discrete sizes.
- **Probability integral transform (PIT)** — `F(y)` at the realised value; uniform when calibrated.
- **Continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)** — a proper score for a full distribution.
- **Cycle service level (CSL)** — `F_S(S)`, the probability demand is met from stock in a cycle.
- **Fill rate** — expected fraction of demand units served, `1 − E[(D−S)⁺]/E[D]`.
- **Expected shortfall** — expected units of unmet demand above a stock level.
- **Critical ratio** — underage cost over underage plus overage cost; the target quantile.
