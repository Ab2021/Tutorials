## 1. Why this topic exists

A manufacturer can have a credible finished-goods plan and still stop production for lack of a low-cost component.
The buyer sees a purchase order due next month.
The production supervisor needs the component at the start of a subassembly order next week.
Both dates may be recorded correctly; the problem is that no one has connected them through the bill of materials and the lead time.
Material requirements planning (MRP) makes that connection explicit.

MRP translates dated parent requirements into dated component requirements.
It nets available stock and firm receipts before proposing further replenishment.
It then offsets each proposed receipt by an assumed lead time to expose the date on which action must begin.
A late release is a decision requiring escalation, not an order that magically traveled back in time.

Consider a plant building a diagnostic pump.
A housing is used both directly in the finished pump and inside a cartridge.
The plant has some housings on hand and a shipment already due.
If the planner orders against each use separately without sharing the same stock balance, the result can double-count inventory.
If the planner ignores the cartridge path, the result can omit a large fraction of demand.
If the planner explodes a parent receipt date rather than its start date, components may arrive after assembly has begun.

The business decisions are specific.
A buyer must decide whether to release, expedite, split, or renegotiate a purchase.
A material planner must decide whether an exception is a genuine shortage or a data defect.
A production planner must determine whether a proposed finished-goods date remains plausible.
A customer-service team may need to revise a promise if the shortage cannot be recovered.
Engineering and quality may need to approve a substitute before any replacement is used.

The term *plan* matters.
A forecast estimates future demand; it is not necessarily a customer commitment.
A master production schedule (MPS) is an authorized statement of intended finished-goods production, although its firmness varies by time fence.
A target is a desired outcome, not an inventory receipt.
A commitment is an accepted obligation, such as a confirmed customer order or an accepted supplier purchase order.
A planned order generated by MRP is a proposal until the appropriate owner releases and accepts it.

This topic follows one fictional product through a complete, time-phased, multi-level explosion.
The numbers are instructional, not a claim about a named manufacturer.
You will calculate the impact of a shared component, an existing receipt, fixed lot multiples, and a release before the planning horizon.
You will also learn why an internally consistent MRP output is not proof that a supplier, machine, or customer can meet the resulting dates.

## 3. The core mental model

MRP is a dated dependency calculation, not a forecast engine and not a finite-capacity schedule.
It starts with demand or an MPS at one level, applies a supply ledger, and creates order proposals.
The proposed **release** of a parent is then a dated source of demand for its components.
The same steps repeat down the product structure.

```text
Forecast + customer orders + inventory policy
                    |
                    v
       authorized parent MPS / independent demand
                    |
                    v
     gross demand + beginning stock + firm receipts
                    |
                    v
             gross-to-net ledger
                    |
                    v
       lot rule -> planned order RECEIPT
                    |
       offset by working lead time and calendar
                    v
       planned order RELEASE / late exception
                    |
       multiply parent release by component usage
                    v
       combine all parent paths at the component
                    |
                    v
       net once -> lot size -> offset -> repeat
                    |
                    v
    capacity and supplier checks -> authorized action
```

The arrows have different meanings.
Netting is a stock-flow accounting operation.
Offsetting is a date calculation using a lead-time convention.
Explosion is a dependency operation using an effective component relationship.
Authorization is a business control performed after a proposal has been generated and checked.
None of these operations is a substitute for another.

Imagine one unit of a pump needing two cartridges.
If the pump order must start in week 3, those two cartridges are required in week 3 under a start-of-order issue convention.
If the cartridge order requires one week of manufacturing lead time, the cartridge order must start in week 2.
If each cartridge needs three housings, that cartridge start creates a requirement for six housings in week 2.
The housing demand is associated with the cartridge **release**, not the later cartridge receipt.

When several parents consume the same item, their dated gross requirements must be collected before the item's stock is netted.
Stock is a shared pool, not a separate balance for each BOM path.
When the same item appears at different levels, a low-level-code or an equivalent topological process ensures that all parent requirements have arrived before the item is planned.
A system may use incremental regeneration, but it still needs dependency-consistent results.

The ledger moves forward in time because each period's ending stock becomes the next period's opening stock.
The BOM is traversed from parents toward components because parent releases create component needs.
The lead-time offset moves the proposed receipt backward in time because an order must begin before it can finish.
These three directions are easy to confuse.
Write them down before implementing a model.

An MRP exception describes a conflict between the model and a rule or date.
It does not automatically diagnose the cause.
A past-due release may mean an unexpectedly late MPS change, an inaccurate lead time, a missing scheduled receipt, or a genuine supply problem.
The correct response depends on which of those explanations is supported by evidence.

## 4. Definitions and vocabulary

| Term | Definition and unit | Common misuse |
|---|---|---|
| Independent demand | Requirement originating outside the item's parent BOM, in item units by date. | Assuming every requirement is independent. |
| Dependent demand | Requirement derived from the release of a consuming parent, in component units by date. | Using the parent's customer due date without its manufacturing offset. |
| Forecast | Estimate of future demand, in units per time bucket. | Treating an estimate as a confirmed customer order. |
| MPS | Dated intended production or supply for a selected item, in finished units per bucket. | Adding the same customer order and MPS quantity as two independent demands. |
| Time bucket | Reporting interval, such as a working week. | Assuming all events within a bucket occur simultaneously. |
| BOM | Approved item relationships specifying component usage per parent. | Treating a conceptual product drawing as an effective production BOM. |
| Parent | Item whose production consumes another item. | Assuming a parent must be a finished good. |
| Component | Item consumed by a parent, in its stocking unit. | Assuming a component cannot also be a parent. |
| Usage quantity | Component units required per parent unit, before any separately modeled loss. | Multiplying by an unconverted case quantity. |
| Shared component | Component used by more than one parent or at more than one level. | Netting the same on-hand stock once for each path. |
| BOM effectivity | Date, serial, location, or other condition under which a relationship applies. | Exploding the current revision against every historical order. |
| Gross requirement | Total dated demand before available stock and receipts are netted, in item units. | Calling a gross requirement an order. |
| Beginning available balance | Eligible unallocated inventory at the start of the horizon, in item units. | Including quarantined or already allocated stock without adjustment. |
| Scheduled receipt | Existing firm supply expected in a bucket, in item units. | Treating a proposed planned receipt as an existing order. |
| Projected available balance | Calculated ending available stock after the period's requirements and modeled supply, in item units. | Treating projection as a physical count. |
| Net requirement | Shortage remaining after available stock and scheduled receipts, plus any modeled protection target, in item units. | Ignoring the prior period's residual balance. |
| Planned order receipt | Proposed replenishment quantity and completion date, in item units. | Assuming the supplier or shop has accepted it. |
| Planned order release | Proposed quantity and start or ordering date, in item units. | Treating release as the same date as receipt despite a nonzero lead time. |
| Lot-for-lot | Rule ordering exactly the net requirement, subject to the model's rounding convention. | Calling it universally cheapest. |
| Fixed multiple | Rule rounding a positive net requirement up to a multiple of a specified number of units. | Confusing a multiple with a fixed order quantity. |
| Safety stock | Explicit inventory protection target in units under a stated policy. | Adding it independently at every period without specifying how it is protected. |
| Lead time | Modeled interval between order release and receipt, in working days or buckets. | Assuming it contains no queue, transport, inspection, or calendar effects. |
| Offset | Mapping a receipt date backward through a lead-time calendar to a release date. | Subtracting calendar weeks when the supplier works a different calendar. |
| Past-due release | Required release earlier than the current planning origin. | Entering a fictional past transaction rather than escalating. |
| Low-level code | Deepest level at which an item appears in the relevant BOM graph. | Planning a shared item before every parent path contributes. |
| Pegging | Linkage explaining which parent or demand contributes to an item's requirement or supply allocation. | Treating a model's allocation as a physical reservation without confirmation. |
| Time fence | Boundary governing how proposed changes are treated over a defined horizon. | Assuming a fence physically prevents shortages. |
| Firm planned order | Planned supply protected from ordinary automatic regeneration under an explicit system rule. | Treating it as a supplier-confirmed purchase order. |
| Nervousness | Unwanted changes in planned order dates or quantities between comparable planning runs. | Labeling every justified change as waste. |
| Reschedule-in / out | Recommendation to move an existing receipt earlier / later. | Executing the recommendation without supplier or shop agreement. |
| Planning horizon | Date range covered by the planning run. | Treating absence beyond the horizon as absence of future demand. |
| Cumulative lead time | Time along a dependent supply path under specified parallel/serial assumptions. | Summing every component lead time even when paths run in parallel. |
| Unit of measure | Stocking, purchasing, or BOM quantity unit and conversion. | Mixing pieces, cases, and kilograms without conversion. |
| Yield | Accepted output divided by input under a defined process and period, often a proportion. | Applying a scrap factor twice. |
| Regeneration | Recalculation of the plan from a defined input snapshot. | Comparing runs whose data cutoff and scope differ without adjustment. |

## 5. The mechanics, step by step

The procedure below uses a simple periodic, deterministic MRP convention.
Commercial systems differ in their treatment of receipts, safety stock, calendars, order status, and time fences.
The convention must be explicit before you compare their outputs.

### Step 1 — Fix the business question and run boundary

**Input:** the plant, item scope, planning origin, horizon, data cutoff, and decision owner.
**Output:** a reproducible run definition.

1. Identify the plant and stock pools that are eligible to satisfy the demand.
2. Record the exact timestamp of the input snapshot.
3. Define the first and last planning buckets.
4. Specify whether periods represent working days, calendar days, or aggregated weeks.
5. Identify which requirements are already represented in the MPS.
6. Record which existing orders qualify as scheduled receipts.
7. Decide whether quantities represent gross physical stock or available stock after holds and allocations.
8. Identify the planner who can resolve each exception.

An eight-week material view may be sufficient for this exercise but not for a product whose longest component path takes twenty weeks.
A horizon that ends too early can understate future replenishment needs and hide the consequences of lot accumulation.
A rolling horizon also changes the visible set of orders between snapshots.
Measure nervousness only over a common comparison window.

### Step 2 — Select the independent parent demand

**Input:** approved production plan, demand policy, and open commitments.
**Output:** a dated gross-requirement series for the first planned item.

The worked example treats the MPS quantities as requirements for finished-goods availability in specified weeks.
It does not also load customer orders or forecast against those same quantities.
That boundary prevents double counting.
The definition could instead use firm MPS planned receipts as fixed supply and model customer orders as demand, but then the remaining ledger must follow that convention consistently.

Ask the production planner which numbers are commitments and which can change.
Ask whether an accepted customer order is already included in the displayed MPS.
Confirm the units and the bucket date convention.
Do not replace a weak MPS with an unreviewed sales forecast merely because the forecast is available.

### Step 3 — Build the eligible beginning balance

**Input:** inventory snapshot, locations, holds, allocations, and ownership rules.
**Output:** available opening stock for each item and pool.

Physical stock and MRP-available stock can differ.
Quarantined units may be unavailable until quality disposition.
A customer-owned component may not be available for another customer's order.
Stock in transit may be a dated receipt rather than opening stock.
A reservation may already be excluded from available balance, depending on the system.
Document that treatment so you do not subtract the reservation twice.

For this example, each opening balance is already eligible and unallocated.
There are no transfers, allocations, expiry dates, or quality holds.
A real run should reconcile the starting balance with its source system and investigate material differences.

### Step 4 — Place existing scheduled receipts

**Input:** open purchase and production orders with remaining quantities and expected receipt dates.
**Output:** a time-phased receipt series, separate from newly proposed orders.

Use remaining open quantity, not the original order quantity when partial receipts have occurred.
Place the receipt on the expected availability date after any modeled inspection or handling delay.
Distinguish an agreed supplier confirmation from an internal requested date.
A scheduled receipt can still be late or uncertain; its inclusion is a planning assumption, not a service guarantee.

Avoid counting the same purchase order line both as in-transit stock and as a future receipt.
If an order has already been released, its component requirements may need to originate from the actual open order rather than a newly generated parent release.
This teaching example has no pre-existing parent production orders requiring additional component explosion.
The scheduled receipts are externally supplied quantities that will be available in their listed weeks.

### Step 5 — State within-period timing and safety-stock policy

**Input:** receipt and issue timing, balance definition, safety-stock policy.
**Output:** an unambiguous ledger convention.

In this example, all scheduled receipts and planned receipts in a week are available before that week's gross requirements are issued.
The balance shown is the end-of-week available quantity.
A release in week 0 is retained as an exception even though the visible horizon begins in week 1.
There is no safety stock, loss, scrap, or demand cancellation.
All units are whole pieces.

This convention can hide a Monday shortage if a scheduled receipt arrives on Friday.
Use daily or event-level planning when that distinction is material.
Safety stock could be handled as a minimum end-of-period projected balance, but the exact policy must be declared.
For a minimum balance of `SS`, netting can use `max(0, gross + SS - prior_balance - scheduled)`.
If the planned receipt is large enough, the resulting ending balance is at least `SS`.
Do not add `SS` to the physical gross demand column and then again as a minimum balance.

### Step 6 — Net one period at a time

**Input:** previous balance `B`, current gross requirement `G`, and current scheduled receipt `S`.
**Output:** net requirement `N`, planned receipt `R`, and ending balance `E`.

For the example's zero-safety-stock convention:

```text
N = max(0, G - B - S)
R = lot_rule(N)
E = B + S + R - G
```

All quantities in these equations are item units in one bucket.
When `N` is zero, `R` is zero.
When `N` is positive, the lot rule produces at least `N` units.
Carry `E` forward as the next period's `B`.
This carryforward is essential: an oversized receipt can protect a later period.

A negative projected balance in this convention signals a failed calculation or an intentionally unfilled shortage.
If a system permits backorders or shortage carryover, write that different convention explicitly.
The example does not create an artificial negative balance merely because a planned release is late; it flags the late release separately.
The planned receipt is a mathematical proposal whose feasibility is still unresolved.

### Step 7 — Apply the item-specific lot rule

**Input:** positive net requirement and the item's approved replenishment policy.
**Output:** planned order receipt quantity.

Lot-for-lot (L4L) makes `R = N`.
A fixed multiple `m` makes `R = m * ceil(N / m)` for `N > 0`.
For `N = 62 pieces` and `m = 50 pieces`, the proposed receipt is `100 pieces`.
The extra `38 pieces` are projected inventory, not new customer demand.
A fixed multiple may reflect packaging, a machine fixture, or a supplier minimum negotiated as a multiple.
It should not be assumed merely because the resulting quantity looks convenient.

A minimum order quantity and a fixed multiple are different constraints.
An order may need to be at least 75 pieces and also a multiple of 25 pieces.
A maximum order quantity may split one replenishment into multiple orders and create multiple setups.
A period-of-supply policy intentionally covers future periods and requires a different calculation.
The worked example only uses L4L and fixed multiples; it does not solve an optimal lot-sizing problem.

### Step 8 — Offset each receipt to a release

**Input:** planned receipt week, item lead time, and calendar.
**Output:** planned release week and any past-due exception.

The simplified example uses whole working-week lead times.
Subtract one week for an item with a one-week lead time and two weeks for an item with a two-week lead time.
For a receipt in week 4 and one-week lead time, the proposed release is week 3.
For a receipt in week 2 and two-week lead time, the proposed release is week 0.
Week 0 is before the planning origin and cannot be executed now.
The release remains visible as a past-due requirement instead of being silently moved into week 1.

In a daily model, use the relevant plant, supplier, transport, and receiving calendars.
Clarify whether lead time includes inspection, transit, queue, setup, and review intervals.
An order placed on a holiday may not start until the next working day.
Different date conventions can create an apparent discrepancy even when both systems use the same nominal lead time.
Trace one order through the calendar before escalating a model difference.

### Step 9 — Explode parent releases into component demand

**Input:** parent planned releases, valid BOM lines, usage quantities, and issue timing.
**Output:** dated dependent gross requirements for each component.

For each parent release, multiply the released parent quantity by the effective usage quantity.
Assign the result to the component's issue date.
Here the issue date is the parent's release week.
For example, 18 pump units released in week 3 create 36 cartridge units at two cartridges per pump.
They also create 18 direct housing units at one housing per pump.

The parent's planned receipt does not automatically create a component requirement on that same receipt date.
The component is needed when the parent production operation consumes it.
Some real routings issue components later than order start; in that case an operation offset is required.
The simple start-of-order convention is conservative only in some circumstances and is not a universal shop-floor rule.

When a BOM has effective-date changes, select the approved line valid under the plant's policy for the order or issue date.
A configurable product may require an order-specific BOM rather than a standard BOM.
An alternate component requires an approved substitution rule and sufficient available supply.
The presence of a substitute in a catalog does not by itself authorize its use.

### Step 10 — Aggregate shared component paths before netting

**Input:** all dated requirements arriving from every parent, plus any independent demand for the component.
**Output:** one gross-requirement series per item and stock pool.

The housing in the example appears directly under the pump and under the cartridge.
The pump's release creates direct housing demand.
The cartridge's release creates additional housing demand.
Both are collected into a single housing ledger before using the opening housing balance.
This prevents allocating the same 40 opening housings to two different paths.

A low-level code is one implementation aid.
The housing appears at depth 1 and depth 2, so its deepest occurrence is depth 2.
Plan the pump and cartridge first, then plan the housing after contributions from both are known.
A topological traversal of the item dependency graph can provide the same ordering.
Cycles must be detected and corrected or explicitly handled as a special manufacturing process; a naive recursive explosion may never terminate.

### Step 11 — Flag exceptions without pretending to resolve them

**Input:** proposed releases, dates, quantities, existing order status, and planning rules.
**Output:** prioritized exception queue.

Common exceptions include releases before the planning origin, overdue existing receipts, and recommendations to reschedule or cancel existing supply.
A model can identify that a quantity is needed in week 2 and the normal release belonged in week 0.
It cannot establish that an expedite is available unless the supplier or shop confirms it.
Likewise, a reschedule-out recommendation does not automatically revise a supplier commitment.

For a late release, verify the underlying stock, receipt, BOM, and lead-time data first.
Then evaluate expedite, alternate supply, approved substitution, resequencing, allocation, or a customer promise change.
Record the decision owner, cost, impact, and next review time.
Do not delete the exception merely to make the dashboard green.

### Step 12 — Check capacity and execute only authorized actions

**Input:** proposed material plan, supplier response, routing, work-center calendar, and operating constraints.
**Output:** feasible or explicitly risk-accepted orders and revised commitments.

Basic MRP normally assumes planned orders can be supplied at the modeled lead time.
It does not reserve machine capacity, labor, tooling, transport, or supplier material.
A capacity requirements check converts the order proposals into a dated load and compares that load with available resources.
Finite scheduling or a supplier confirmation may be needed before accepting a customer date.

A planner should release an order only under the organization's approval rules.
The buyer confirms a purchase order with the supplier.
The production supervisor accepts a feasible shop order under the current operating conditions.
Customer service communicates any revised promise after an authorized decision.
Track actual receipts and completions so the next run starts with current evidence.

### Step 13 — Control change and nervousness

**Input:** comparable plan snapshots, order identities, time fences, and materiality thresholds.
**Output:** an explainable change list and a decision on whether each change needs action.

A demand change can propagate through several BOM levels and change many planned orders.
A lot boundary can amplify a small demand change into a large replenishment change.
A delayed receipt can create an earlier planned order even if total horizon demand is unchanged.
Not every change is avoidable; a genuine customer cancellation should update the plan.
The objective is to avoid unnecessary disruption while preserving timely response to real information.

Compare the same item, site, order identity or matching rule, bucket granularity, and horizon.
Separate new orders, cancellations, date moves, quantity changes, and orders leaving the comparison window.
Report changes within the action horizon separately from distant changes.
Record whether a change was driven by demand, inventory, master data, a receipt update, or a manual override.

A planning time fence can limit automatic changes close to execution.
A firm planned order can protect a proposal from ordinary replanning under a documented rule.
Neither measure creates missing material or authorizes an impossible promise.
The exception workflow must remain able to surface a shortage inside the fence.

### Step 14 — Interpret order changes across a run

**Input:** an earlier and a later planning snapshot with stable order identifiers.
**Output:** a classified list of changes and a traceable explanation.

An order can change quantity without changing date.
It can change date without changing quantity.
It can change both quantity and date, or it can disappear from the later snapshot.
An order that disappears may have been canceled, executed, or moved beyond the comparison horizon.
Those are different events and should not be collapsed into a single cancellation count.

First, select the common planning window.
Second, match order identities where stable identifiers exist.
Third, examine unmatched records using execution and cancellation transactions.
Fourth, classify each matched difference by date, quantity, status, and action horizon.
Fifth, link the change to a source event where the data support that conclusion.
Finally, separate confirmed causal explanations from unresolved hypotheses.

For example, a reduction in customer demand could reduce a parent's proposed release.
That reduction could lower component gross demand on the corresponding issue date.
The component receipt might remain unchanged if the new net requirement is still in the same fixed-multiple interval.
Its projected ending balance would instead increase.
Alternatively, a one-piece change across a multiple boundary could remove an entire lot from the plan.
These outcomes follow from the policy and should be visible in the trace.

### Step 15 — Reconcile pegging without confusing it with reservation

**Input:** parent requirements, component requirements, and any allocation or reservation records.
**Output:** a defensible explanation of what is affected by a shortage.

Pegging answers a causal or allocation question within the planning model.
For a housing shortage, it can identify which pump and cartridge releases contribute to demand.
An individual pegging relationship may be recalculated as supply and demand change.
It should not automatically be treated as a legal or physical reservation.

A physical reservation normally requires an explicit transaction and an operational rule.
For example, a quality-released batch may be allocated to a specific customer program.
If that batch is reserved, another demand stream may not be eligible to consume it.
The planner must ensure that the availability pool and the pegging logic reflect that restriction.
Otherwise, a useful explanatory link can be mistaken for stock that is actually available.

When a component cannot cover all parents, establish the allocation authority before promising recovery.
A customer priority may be based on contractual obligations, safety, clinical need, or another approved criterion.
The priority rule is a business decision, not an automatic consequence of BOM sequence.
Record the allocation, the affected commitments, and when the decision will be reviewed.

### Step 16 — Review the plan after an exception decision

**Input:** an approved recovery action and its confirmed dates, quantities, and conditions.
**Output:** updated source records and an auditable next plan.

If the supplier confirms an earlier delivery, update the appropriate order line according to the order-management process.
If a substitute is approved, record its scope, revision, quantity, validity, and required quality checks.
If the production date changes, update the authorized production plan and assess downstream commitments.
Do not enter a hypothetical receipt as firm supply merely to make the exception disappear.

After execution, compare the actual usable receipt with the confirmed date and quantity.
Record partial deliveries, inspection holds, and remaining open quantities.
The next MRP run should use those updated facts rather than retaining the original proposal unchanged.
Close the case only when the agreed outcome or an authorized change to the commitment is evidenced.

The objective is a closed loop.
The ledger identifies a need.
An owner evaluates and authorizes an action.
Execution produces evidence.
The next planning snapshot incorporates that evidence.

## 6. Worked example

### Case and decision

A fictional plant plans portable diagnostic pumps for eight working weeks.
The planner needs a material proposal and must identify any component that cannot meet its normal lead time.
The example is intentionally deterministic so the arithmetic can be reproduced.
It does not claim a particular service level or an optimal total cost.

The commercial forecast is an input to the planning process, but its individual quantities are not needed for this exercise.
The approved MPS is the dated finished-goods requirement shown below.
It is a plan, not automatically a customer commitment.
No separate target quantity is inserted into the ledger.
Existing scheduled receipts are assumed to be firm open supply, but their dates are still assumptions until execution confirms them.
All newly computed receipts and releases are proposals.

### BOM and item parameters

```text
P: finished pump
├── 2 A: cartridges per P
│   ├── 3 C: housings per A
│   └── 2 D: seals per A
└── 1 C: housing per P
```

The housing `C` is shared across two levels.
All component usage quantities are pieces per parent piece.
All BOM lines are effective for the entire eight-week horizon.
All issues occur in the parent release week.
There is no scrap, yield loss, safety stock, or capacity constraint in the calculation.

| Item | Description | Beginning available | Scheduled receipt | Lead time | Lot policy |
|---|---|---:|---|---:|---|
| P | Finished pump | 8 pieces | 10 pieces in week 2 | 1 week | L4L |
| A | Cartridge | 12 pieces | 8 pieces in week 2 | 1 week | Multiple of 50 pieces |
| C | Housing | 40 pieces | 60 pieces in week 2 | 1 week | Multiple of 100 pieces |
| D | Seal | 25 pieces | 15 pieces in week 1 | 2 weeks | L4L |

The planning origin is the beginning of week 1.
A weekly receipt is assumed available before all requirements in that week.
A release in week 0 is outside the actionable horizon and must be escalated.

### 6.1 Finished pump P

The MPS requires 36 pumps in week 4, 48 pumps in week 6, and 30 pumps in week 8.
Other weeks have zero pump requirement.
The week-2 receipt is not a new MRP proposal.

| P, pieces | W1 | W2 | W3 | W4 | W5 | W6 | W7 | W8 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Gross requirement | 0 | 0 | 0 | 36 | 0 | 48 | 0 | 30 |
| Scheduled receipt | 0 | 10 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Projected ending balance | 8 | 18 | 18 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Net requirement | 0 | 0 | 0 | 18 | 0 | 48 | 0 | 30 |
| Planned receipt | 0 | 0 | 0 | 18 | 0 | 48 | 0 | 30 |
| Planned release | 0 | 0 | 18 | 0 | 48 | 0 | 30 | 0 |

In week 4, `36 - 18 = 18 pumps` are required after available stock.
L4L creates a receipt of 18 pumps in week 4 and the one-week offset creates a release of 18 pumps in week 3.
The week-6 demand creates a 48-pump release in week 5.
The week-8 demand creates a 30-pump release in week 7.
The sum of pump planned receipts is 96 pieces; opening stock plus scheduled supply adds 18 pieces, matching 114 pieces of total requirement.

### 6.2 Cartridge A

Each pump release consumes two cartridges.
Gross cartridge requirements are therefore 36 pieces in week 3, 96 pieces in week 5, and 60 pieces in week 7.
The fixed-multiple rule applies to the net requirement, not to the gross requirement.

| A, pieces | W1 | W2 | W3 | W4 | W5 | W6 | W7 | W8 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Gross requirement | 0 | 0 | 36 | 0 | 96 | 0 | 60 | 0 |
| Scheduled receipt | 0 | 8 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Projected ending balance | 12 | 20 | 34 | 34 | 38 | 38 | 28 | 28 |
| Net requirement | 0 | 0 | 16 | 0 | 62 | 0 | 22 | 0 |
| Planned receipt | 0 | 0 | 50 | 0 | 100 | 0 | 50 | 0 |
| Planned release | 0 | 50 | 0 | 100 | 0 | 50 | 0 | 0 |

At the start of week 3, 20 cartridges are available.
The net requirement is `36 - 20 = 16 pieces`.
Rounding to a multiple of 50 gives a planned receipt of 50 pieces and an ending balance of `20 + 50 - 36 = 34 pieces`.
The one-week lead time places the 50-piece release in week 2.

In week 5, the net requirement is `96 - 34 = 62 pieces`.
The next 50-piece multiple is 100 pieces, so the ending balance is `34 + 100 - 96 = 38 pieces`.
The release is 100 pieces in week 4.
In week 7, the net requirement is `60 - 38 = 22 pieces`, the receipt is 50 pieces, and the ending balance is 28 pieces.
The corresponding release is 50 pieces in week 6.
Across the horizon, `12 + 8 + 200 - 192 = 28 cartridges`, which reconciles the ledger.

### 6.3 Housing C: combine both paths

The direct pump path contributes one housing for each pump released.
The cartridge path contributes three housings for each cartridge released.
Compute both paths before netting the 40-piece opening housing balance.

The week-2 cartridge release is 50 pieces, so it creates `50 × 3 = 150 housings` in week 2.
The week-4 cartridge release of 100 pieces creates `100 × 3 = 300 housings` in week 4.
The direct P release in week 3 creates 18 additional housings in week 3.

In week 2, `150 - 40 - 60 = 50 housings` are net required.
The 100-piece multiple yields a receipt of 100 pieces and an ending balance of 50 pieces.
The receipt's release belongs in week 1.
In week 3, the direct use of 18 pieces reduces that balance to 32 pieces.

### 6.4 Seal D and the past-due release

Each cartridge release consumes two seals.
The cartridge releases of 50, 100, and 50 pieces produce seal requirements of 100, 200, and 100 pieces in weeks 2, 4, and 6.
The seal has a two-week lead time and L4L replenishment.

### 6.5 Decision and sensitivity

The mathematical ledger shows no negative ending balance because it includes a proposed 60-piece week-2 receipt.
That receipt is not feasible under the stated normal lead time unless an exception is resolved.
First the buyer verifies the 15-piece week-1 receipt and the 25-piece eligible opening balance.
Then the buyer asks the supplier whether 60 additional seals can arrive by the required week-2 issue date.
The planner checks whether the cartridge release can move without jeopardizing the pump requirement.
Quality and engineering must approve any alternative seal; procurement must confirm availability and cost.
If none of those actions works, the production and customer-service owners assess a revised pump commitment.

A one-week earlier pump requirement can shift releases at several levels.
The resulting change can also cross a lot-size boundary, so the quantity change may not be proportional to the demand change.
For example, changing an A net requirement from 50 to 51 pieces raises its fixed-multiple receipt from 50 to 100 pieces.
That extra 50-piece A release creates 150 additional C pieces and 100 additional D pieces under this BOM.
Those are model consequences, not evidence that demand itself grew by 50 pumps.
Before changing a firm order, compare the new plan to the previous snapshot and identify the driver.

## 7. Code, query, or calculation

The following Python 3 program reproduces the example using only the standard library.
It uses whole pieces and whole working-week buckets.
The modeled BOM is acyclic and is processed in the explicit dependency order `P`, `A`, `C`, `D`.
The code combines all component requirements before planning a shared item.
It retains week-0 releases separately rather than moving them to week 1.
It does not model execution, allocation, safety stock, capacity, substitution, or dynamic effectivity.

```python
from collections import defaultdict

HORIZON = 8

# (initial available pieces, scheduled receipts by week, lead weeks, lot multiple)
# A multiple of 1 implements L4L because all quantities are integral pieces.
ITEMS = {
    "P": (8, {2: 10}, 1, 1),
    "A": (12, {2: 8}, 1, 50),
    "C": (40, {2: 60}, 1, 100),
    "D": (25, {1: 15}, 2, 1),
}

# Each pair is (component, pieces per released parent piece).
BOM = {
    "P": [("A", 2), ("C", 1)],
    "A": [("C", 3), ("D", 2)],
    "C": [],
    "D": [],
}

# This order puts every parent before each of its components.
ORDER = ("P", "A", "C", "D")

# The MPS is modeled as P gross demand, not additionally as a receipt.
gross = defaultdict(lambda: defaultdict(int))
gross["P"].update({4: 36, 6: 48, 8: 30})
results = {}

for item in ORDER:
    opening, scheduled, lead, multiple = ITEMS[item]
    balance = opening
    rows = {name: [0] * HORIZON for name in
            ("gross", "scheduled", "balance", "net", "receipt", "release")}
    outside = defaultdict(int)

    for week in range(1, HORIZON + 1):
        demand = gross[item][week]
        firm_supply = scheduled.get(week, 0)
        net = max(0, demand - balance - firm_supply)
        receipt = ((net + multiple - 1) // multiple) * multiple
        balance += firm_supply + receipt - demand
        release_week = week - lead
        if receipt:
            if 1 <= release_week <= HORIZON:
                rows["release"][release_week - 1] += receipt
            else:
                outside[release_week] += receipt
        i = week - 1
        rows["gross"][i] = demand
        rows["scheduled"][i] = firm_supply
        rows["balance"][i] = balance
        rows["net"][i] = net
        rows["receipt"][i] = receipt

    results[item] = (rows, dict(outside))

    # Explode all releases, including those before the horizon.
    # The present dataset has no components below the late D release.
    releases = {week: qty for week, qty in enumerate(rows["release"], 1) if qty}
    releases.update(outside)
    for component, usage in BOM[item]:
        for week, qty in releases.items():
            gross[component][week] += qty * usage

for item in ORDER:
    rows, outside = results[item]
    print(item)
    for name in ("gross", "scheduled", "balance", "net", "receipt", "release"):
        print(f"  {name:9s}: {rows[name]}")
    if outside:
        print(f"  releases outside weeks 1-{HORIZON}: {outside}")
```

  net      : [0, 0, 0, 18, 0, 48, 0, 30]

## 8. Data, standards, and regulatory context

### Records and grain

An MRP run depends on several independently governed records.
A calculation is reproducible only if the version and timestamp of each relevant record can be reconstructed.
The minimum practical question is not merely “what was the item master?” but “which approved item, site, BOM, inventory, order, and calendar records were used at the run cutoff?”

| Record | Useful grain | Decision affected | Control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Item | Item and plant, with validity interval | Replenishment policy and lead time | Authorized owner and effective date |
| BOM line | Parent, component, plant, revision, validity, line | Component quantity and issue timing | Engineering approval and overlap check |
| Inventory | Item, site, location, status, snapshot time | Eligible opening stock | Reconcile with physical and transaction records |
| Open receipt | Order line, item, remaining quantity, expected date | Net requirement | Reconcile open quantity and receipt status |
| MPS | Item, site, bucket, version | Parent gross requirement | Demand/production approval and inclusion policy |
| Calendar | Resource or partner, date, working status | Release date | Versioned holiday and shutdown changes |
| Lot rule | Item, plant, supplier or production context | Proposed receipt size | Review against actual constraints and cost |
| Snapshot | Run ID, cutoff, horizon, configuration | Reproducibility and change attribution | Immutable run metadata and lineage |

Record both effective time and transaction time where practical.
An engineering change can be entered today but become valid next month.
A historical planning analysis needs the records available to the planner at the historical run, not a retroactive application of today's BOM.
Corrections may be legitimate, but they must remain distinguishable from the original decision context.

### Units, packaging, and yield

A supplier may sell seals by the carton while the BOM consumes pieces.
If one carton contains 40 pieces, a proposed receipt of 60 pieces cannot be ordered as exactly 1.5 cartons when only whole cartons are permitted.
A purchase unit conversion and an order-multiple rule should express the actual constraint.
The model must also specify whether inventory is maintained in pieces, cartons, or another base unit.

Scrap and yield need a separate, approved convention.
If a process consumes more input than its nominal output because only 95% of input passes inspection, one possible deterministic approximation is to divide the required good quantity by 0.95 and round up.
That approximation assumes a stable known yield and ignores stochastic variability.
If a BOM usage quantity already includes the same allowance, applying the yield again overstates demand.
Actual batch yield and traceability requirements can require more detailed calculations.

### Interoperability standards

GS1 identifies trade items and locations using globally standardized identifiers such as the Global Trade Item Number (GTIN) and Global Location Number (GLN).
The identifiers can reduce ambiguity when exchanging item and location data across organizations.
They do not, by themselves, validate a BOM, guarantee a lead time, or decide which stock is available.
Use the relevant GS1 specifications and trading-partner agreements when exchanging identifiers and packaging hierarchies.

ANSI/ISA-95 describes models and terminology for integrating enterprise and control systems.
It can help organize exchanges between planning, manufacturing operations, and execution systems.
It does not prescribe this lesson's specific netting formula, lot policy, or automatic order authorization.
The standard's applicability depends on the integration and the parties' implementation choices.

Electronic data interchange (EDI) messages can communicate orders, acknowledgments, and shipment information.
A message format alone does not establish that a requested date is confirmed or that the shipment will arrive on time.
Map each message status to the planning ledger and reconcile late, partial, canceled, and duplicate transactions.
An implementation guide and commercial agreement determine the particular transaction interpretation.

### Regulatory context

MRP itself is a planning method, not a universal regulatory approval.
For regulated medical devices, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Quality Management System Regulation became effective on February 2, 2026, incorporating ISO 13485:2016 by reference with specified requirements and modifications.
An organization must evaluate its own products, jurisdiction, and applicable quality-system obligations.
This educational pump example is fictional and does not establish compliance.

Controlled specifications, change decisions, supplier controls, and traceability may be relevant to an actual regulated manufacturer.
The exact records and controls depend on the applicable regulation and the organization's quality system.
A planned substitution must not be treated as approved simply because it satisfies the numerical MRP shortage.
Consult the controlling regulatory text and qualified quality and legal personnel for implementation.

### Data quality checks before release

1. Confirm that the open quantity on each scheduled receipt excludes already received units.
2. Confirm that the expected date means usable availability, not merely supplier dispatch.
3. Reconcile opening available stock to inventory status and allocation rules.
4. Validate that every BOM component has an item record and a stocking unit.
5. Check BOM effectivity for gaps and overlapping active alternatives.
6. Verify that the parent release date is the intended component issue date or apply an operation offset.
7. Check that lead times use the correct working calendar and include the intended stages.
8. Confirm that the lot rule represents a current commercial or production constraint.
9. Retain order identifiers and the source of each dependent requirement for investigation.
10. Compare the run cutoff and horizon before attributing differences between plans.

A data-quality score is useful only when its denominator and consequence are defined.
For example, `open purchase-order lines with a supplier-confirmed usable receipt date / open purchase-order lines` measures confirmation coverage.
It does not prove that the confirmed date is reliable.
Pair it with receipt-date performance and a review of high-impact exceptions.

### Example data contract for a supplier receipt

A practical interface should state what each field means, not only its datatype.
The following fields are illustrative and should be adapted to the actual source and contractual process.

| Field | Interpretation | Quality question |
|---|---|---|
| `purchase_order_id` | Identifier for the commercial order. | Does it uniquely identify the order in this source? |
| `line_id` | Identifier for the ordered item line. | Can partial receipts be linked to this line? |
| `item_id` | Identifier of the purchased component. | Does it map to the plant's inventory item? |
| `site_id` | Intended receiving site or stock pool. | Can the quantity actually satisfy demand at this site? |
| `remaining_qty` | Open quantity not yet received or canceled. | Has each receipt already reduced this amount? |
| `unit` | Unit for the quantity. | Is its conversion to the stock unit approved? |
| `requested_date` | Date requested by the buyer. | Is it being mistaken for a supplier confirmation? |
| `confirmed_date` | Date accepted by the supplier, if any. | Is the confirmation current and for the remaining quantity? |
| `available_date` | Date the units are expected to be usable. | Does it include transit, receiving, and quality release? |
| `status` | Order state according to the source system. | Does this status qualify as scheduled supply? |
| `updated_at` | Time the source record last changed. | Was it available at the planning snapshot cutoff? |

A requested date can be useful for measuring supplier responsiveness.
It is not necessarily a reliable expected receipt date.
A dispatch date can be useful for tracking transit.
It is not necessarily the date on which stock can be issued to production.
An order acknowledgment can confirm a different quantity and date from the request.
The reconciliation process must preserve these distinctions.

Suppose 100 seals were ordered and 35 seals have been received and accepted.
If none were canceled, the remaining open quantity is 65 seals.
The initial inventory snapshot may already include the accepted 35 seals.
Loading the original 100 seals as a future receipt would count those 35 twice.
If 10 of the 35 received seals remain on quality hold, their eligibility must be evaluated separately.

### Source lineage for an exception

For a material shortage, retain enough information to reconstruct the calculation.
Record the planning-run identifier and input cutoff.
Record the item, site, unit, bucket, opening eligible stock, and scheduled supply.
Record the parent releases and BOM versions that contributed to gross requirement.
Record the lot policy, lead-time value, and calendar used to calculate the release.
Record the planner's manual adjustments and their authorizing owner.

This lineage makes a shortage explainable to the buyer and production team.
It also enables a later reviewer to distinguish a data correction from a demand change.
If the BOM revision changes after the run, a historical snapshot remains necessary to reproduce the original result.
If the data platform retains only current values, an apparent historical discrepancy may be impossible to resolve reliably.

### Minimum monitoring set

One measure can track the percentage of actionable purchase proposals whose normal release date is already past.
Define the numerator as past-due actionable proposals and the denominator as all actionable purchase proposals in the same run.
Report the measure by criticality and planner as well as in aggregate.
Exclude proposals outside the action horizon only if the rule is documented.

A second measure can track supplier-confirmation coverage for open purchase-order lines.
Its denominator should exclude closed and canceled lines consistently.
Its numerator should require a usable confirmed quantity and date, not merely an acknowledgment message.
Pair coverage with actual usable receipt performance so a high confirmation rate is not mistaken for reliability.

A third measure can track the age of unresolved shortages affecting firm customer commitments.
Define age from the first recorded exception, not from the most recent regeneration.
Preserve the original date if the same exception reappears in consecutive runs.
Review high-impact outliers with the owner who can change supply, production, or customer commitments.

## 9. Industry practice and case evidence

### What the evidence supports

The MRP method has a long operational history, but implementation results are context-dependent.
The published sources below include research on schedule instability, official descriptions of standards, and official software documentation.
They support the mechanics and limitations discussed here; they do not certify the performance of the fictional pump plan.

The classic research by Blackburn, Kropp, and Millen studies changes and stability in MRP systems.
The planning literature shows that different approaches to dampening nervousness trade stability against responsiveness.
A time fence, firming rule, or lot policy must therefore be assessed against the costs and service risks of the particular environment.
It is not evidence that every near-term change should be suppressed.

The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) describes manufacturing systems integration and the role of standards in connecting enterprise and manufacturing operations.
That evidence supports treating data exchange and operational execution as separate concerns.
It does not mean that an ISA-95-compliant interface automatically produces a feasible material plan.

### Named public illustration: Toyota and supplier risk

Toyota publicly describes the effect of the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake on its supply chain and its subsequent efforts to strengthen supplier risk management.
The event exposed disruption risk across a multi-tier supplier network.
The documented fact is the occurrence of the disruption and Toyota's reported countermeasures; the sources do not provide the exact fictional MRP ledgers in this lesson.

**Inference:** a time-phased dependent-demand view and reliable multi-tier supplier information can help identify where a component disruption may affect parent products.
That inference does not establish that a standard MRP run would have prevented the disruption.
Supplier mapping, inventory buffers, alternate capacity, recovery planning, and validated execution data address risks beyond a deterministic BOM explosion.
A planner should avoid turning a public resilience case into a claim of quantified MRP benefits without evidence.

### Named public illustration: ERP planning logic

Oracle's published planning documentation describes gross-to-net planning, existing supply, and planned orders in the context of its planning products.
Microsoft's Dynamics 365 documentation describes master planning and the generation of planned orders.
These are official vendor descriptions of product behavior, not independent proof that a deployment is successful.
Actual behavior depends on the installed product, version, configuration, and data.

**Inference:** a cross-functional review of configuration and source records is necessary before comparing a hand calculation to an enterprise-resource-planning (ERP) result.
A vendor may handle firm orders, negative days, safety stock, working calendars, or forecast consumption differently from the simplified method here.
Start with a single-item, single-period reconciliation, then add the next rule.
A mismatch can be an intentional configuration difference rather than a software defect.

### A practical operating rhythm

A material planner typically reviews the highest-impact exceptions after a run, rather than manually checking every item equally.
A useful queue identifies the impacted customer or parent, the latest actionable release date, the shortage quantity, and the decision owner.
The queue should also show the evidence behind each proposed receipt and the time at which that evidence was last confirmed.

The buyer investigates purchased components and obtains a supplier commitment or records that one is unavailable.
The production planner checks whether the related shop order is feasible against materials and capacity.
The inventory or warehouse owner resolves discrepancies in stock status and location.
Engineering and quality review controlled changes or substitutions.
Customer service handles revised external commitments under the organization's authority model.

A daily exception review can reduce the time between a late signal and a response.
It can also generate noise if the MPS changes repeatedly without decision discipline.
Set an explicit materiality threshold and preserve a route for critical low-quantity shortages.
A single seal may be inexpensive but still stop a high-value pump.

### Nervousness: an illustrative comparison

Suppose the same open cartridge proposal is 100 pieces due in week 5 in snapshot A and 100 pieces due in week 4 in snapshot B.
That is one order with a one-week earlier date, not a 100-piece increase in total order quantity.
Suppose another matched order changes from 50 to 100 pieces in the same week.
That is one quantity increase of 50 pieces, not necessarily a new independent demand of 50 pieces.

If 20 matched actionable orders are compared and 6 changed either date or quantity, the change incidence is `6 / 20 = 30%`.
If the total absolute quantity change over those matched orders is 120 pieces and the baseline matched quantity is 1,000 pieces, the normalized quantity movement is `120 / 1,000 = 12%`.
These measures answer different questions and can overlap.
They do not include unmatched new or canceled orders unless the metric explicitly says so.

A useful report also records the direction and timing of a change.
Moving a distant proposal by a week may have little immediate cost.
Changing an already released supplier order the day before shipment can cause premium freight or excess stock.
The decision threshold should reflect commitments and operational cost, not just the number of changed fields.

### Choosing a stabilizing action

A time fence protects an agreed near-term decision window from automatic regeneration under defined rules.
Firming selected planned orders can retain quantities or dates during ordinary replanning.
A minimum change threshold can suppress small recommendation noise when that suppression is explicitly approved.
These controls may make the plan easier to execute but can also conceal a real shortage if exceptions are suppressed.

A better workflow separates a stable execution instruction from a visible risk signal.
Keep the firm order unchanged until an authorized owner decides otherwise.
Show the shortage, its pegged parents, and the impact of not changing the order.
When a genuine customer change arrives, compare the cost of accepting it with the cost of preserving the old plan.
Record the decision and update both the execution order and the planning assumptions as appropriate.

### Operating example: a controlled expedite

The following is an illustrative decision sequence, not a report of an actual company's outcome.
At the start of week 1, the fictional buyer receives the seal exception for 60 pieces.
The buyer verifies the 25-piece opening balance with the warehouse.
Quality confirms that the opening balance is eligible for the relevant production program.
The buyer then verifies whether the 15-piece week-1 scheduled receipt is still expected on time.

The supplier is asked for a specific alternative: 60 usable pieces before the week-2 cartridge issue.
The supplier's response must distinguish its ship date from the plant's usable receipt date.
The buyer records any premium freight charge and the duration for which the quote is valid.
The planner checks whether the proposed delivery covers the actual issue timing within week 2.

If the supplier confirms the quantity but not the arrival date, the material risk remains open.
If the supplier confirms the date but requires a 100-piece minimum, the extra 40 pieces affect inventory and cost.
If the supplier can deliver only 40 pieces, production must determine whether partial cartridge production is possible.
The model cannot infer that a partial quantity is useful without knowing the physical order and issue constraints.

If the normal supplier cannot recover, procurement may investigate an alternative source.
Engineering and quality determine whether the alternative is approved for this pump and revision.
The buyer must not place an order for an unapproved substitute as though it were interchangeable.
The decision record should identify who accepted any additional cost and residual service risk.

The production planner then checks whether the pump's planned dates remain feasible.
The customer-service owner receives a clear distinction between confirmed recovery and an open risk.
The exception closes only when the agreed recovery or revised promise has been recorded.
After receipt, the warehouse and quality teams confirm the usable quantity and date.

### Diagnosing a lot-boundary change

Suppose an item has a 50-piece fixed multiple and 20 pieces available before a requirement.
With a requirement of 70 pieces, the net requirement is 50 pieces.
The proposed receipt is therefore 50 pieces and the projected balance is zero.
If the requirement increases by one piece to 71, the net requirement becomes 51 pieces.
The proposed receipt rises to 100 pieces and the projected balance becomes 49 pieces.

The underlying demand increase is only one piece.
The proposed receipt increase is 50 pieces because the lot constraint is discrete.
The extra 49 pieces remain projected inventory, not additional consumption.
If the item is a parent, the larger release can create correspondingly larger component requirements.
The resulting component-order changes should be attributed to the combination of demand movement and the lot rule.

The right response depends on whether the multiple is a real constraint.
If it reflects a mandatory supplier package, overriding it may be impossible or expensive.
If it is an old default and the supplier accepts smaller lots, the buyer may update the policy through the normal approval process.
If it reflects a setup trade-off rather than a hard constraint, a cost comparison may be appropriate.
Do not remove a genuine production constraint merely to improve a nervousness metric.

### Balancing stability and responsiveness

A plan must respond to actual changes in customers, suppliers, and available inventory.
It also needs enough stability for people to buy materials and operate equipment.
Those objectives can conflict when every small input change causes an order recommendation.
The appropriate balance differs by lead time, order status, contract, and consequence of a shortage.

Use a short action horizon for recommendations that require immediate human attention.
Use a longer visibility horizon to expose emerging risk before action becomes urgent.
Allow planners to firm selected orders under an explicit policy.
Retain a visible exception when the firmed plan no longer covers updated requirements.
Require an authorized decision when changing a released order or an external commitment.

Measure both stability and service.
A lower count of changed orders is not success if shortages are simply hidden.
A lower shortage count is not success if excess and obsolete stock rise without limit.
Review the measures together with supplier expedites, inventory, and customer outcomes.
Document the period, population, and denominator for every comparison.

## 10. Failure modes and red flags

| Failure | Early warning sign | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Double-counted independent demand | MPS and order totals both rise for the same commitment | Same obligation enters as forecast, order, and MPS demand | Excess planned supply | Document demand consumption and reconcile to the MPS. |
| Omitted parent path | Component demand is unexpectedly low | Shared component is exploded from only one parent | Shortage at assembly | Combine all effective paths before netting. |
| Inventory netted twice | Each product plan seems covered by the same stock | Separate ledgers reuse one common pool | Overpromising and late orders | Net once at item and pool grain. |
| Wrong release basis | Component need aligns with parent completion | Explosion uses parent receipt rather than issue/start | Components arrive after production begins | Use parent release plus operation offset. |
| Receipt double counted | In-transit and open-order totals disagree | One shipment appears as stock and scheduled supply | False availability | Reconcile transaction identity and remaining quantity. |
| Unusable stock included | Available balance is high but warehouse cannot issue | Holds, ownership, or expiry are ignored | Line stop despite a green plan | Define eligible status and reconcile exceptions. |
| Incorrect BOM revision | Unexpected substitutions or pick shortages | Current revision applied outside effectivity | Wrong parts ordered or used | Version BOMs and validate the order context. |
| Unit conversion defect | Requirements differ by a package-size factor | Pieces and cartons are mixed | Severe over- or under-order | Govern base units, conversions, and rounding. |
| Outdated lead time | Persistent past-due releases or expedites | Actual supplier or shop time exceeds the model | Chronic lateness | Compare dated actuals with modeled components of lead time. |
| Calendar mismatch | Release dates fall on shutdown days | Calendar weeks substituted for working days | Late starts and missed receipts | Use appropriate supplier, transport, and plant calendars. |
| Lot rule mismatch | Repeated manual quantity changes | Lot multiple no longer matches constraints | Excess stock or rejected order | Review lot parameters with buyer and production. |
| Hidden past-due release | No exception despite week-0 requirement | Model clips dates to the planning origin | Unachievable receipt appears feasible | Preserve and escalate outside-horizon releases. |
| Bucket timing error | Weekly plan balances but Monday line stops | Friday receipt covers Monday demand in same bucket | Intraperiod shortage | Use daily or event-level dates for critical items. |
| Capacity ignored | Every item is green but resource load exceeds availability | MRP assumes infinite capacity | Orders compete for the same machine | Run capacity review and resolve overload. |
| Unapproved substitute | Suggested replacement exists but quality blocks issue | Numerical equivalence mistaken for approval | Compliance or quality incident | Require documented engineering and quality authorization. |
| Excessive nervousness | Frequent order changes without demand explanation | Lot thresholds, volatile inputs, or unstable rules | Supplier cost and planner workload | Compare snapshots and address the actual driver. |
| Over-firming | Few order changes but shortage exceptions rise | Fence suppresses needed response | Late customer orders | Keep shortages visible and allow authorized exceptions. |
| Horizon truncation | Low demand near the horizon end | Future requirements are not visible | Misleading lot and inventory decisions | Cover cumulative lead time and decision horizon. |
| Circular BOM | Explosion does not terminate or repeatedly expands | Invalid dependency cycle | Run failure or inflated demand | Detect cycles and govern special rework structures. |
| Unexplained manual override | Plan cannot be reproduced from source data | Edits lack reason, owner, or expiry | Loss of trust and inconsistent decisions | Log overrides and review their continuing need. |

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why it is needed here | What breaks it | How to test or respond |
|---|---|---|---|
| BOM is complete and acyclic | Parent releases must reach all required components in a finite sequence. | Missing line, unapproved alternate, or circular relationship. | Compare to approved engineering structure and run cycle checks. |
| BOM is effective for all eight weeks | One constant usage applies to each release. | Engineering change or configuration-specific option. | Check plant and date effectivity for every order. |
| Component issue is at parent release | Gives a simple dated dependency. | Components are consumed at later routing operations. | Compare pick/issue timestamps to routing operations. |
| Whole-week buckets are adequate | Allows arithmetic with integer week offsets. | Receipt and consumption occur in the wrong order within the same week. | Inspect critical items at daily or event resolution. |
| Receipts precede requirements within a bucket | Makes the ledger convention deterministic. | Receipt is physically available after an issue. | Compare delivery, inspection, and issue timestamps. |
| Opening stock is eligible and unallocated | Makes one opening value sufficient. | Quality hold, reservation, expiry, or customer ownership. | Reconcile by location, status, ownership, and allocation. |
| Scheduled receipts arrive as listed | Allows the model to count existing supply. | Supplier delay, partial delivery, or quality failure. | Obtain confirmation and monitor actual usable receipts. |
| Lead times are fixed whole weeks | Makes backward offsetting reproducible. | Variability, holidays, queue, transport, or inspection. | Compare stage-level actuals and use relevant calendars. |
| No safety stock is required | Keeps the zero-minimum ledger easy to audit. | Volatile supply or demand requires explicit protection. | Review service policy and model a stated minimum balance. |
| Yield is 100% | Nominal BOM usage equals input needs. | Scrap, test failures, or variable process yield. | Measure good output and apply a nonduplicated yield policy. |
| Quantity is integral in pieces | Makes ceiling to lot multiples well defined. | Weight-based or fractional consumption. | Use a suitable base unit and precision policy. |
| Planned supply has no capacity limit | Isolates the material dependency calculation. | Supplier, labor, tool, or machine is overloaded. | Run capacity and supplier feasibility checks. |
| No existing parent orders need further explosion | Avoids double counting or omitting open-order components. | Released parent order has unissued components. | Include its remaining component requirements once. |
| No reservations or allocation priorities | A common stock pool can be netted in date order. | Dedicated customer, program, or project stock. | Segment pools and apply explicit allocation rules. |
| Horizon is sufficient for the illustration | All shown finished-goods needs are within eight weeks. | Long cumulative lead time or important future demand. | Extend the horizon and identify beyond-horizon dependencies. |
| Demand and supply are deterministic | Produces one reproducible plan. | Forecast error, uncertain yield, or supplier disruptions. | Evaluate scenarios, buffers, and recovery actions separately. |
| Lot multiples are binding | Explains the planned excess receipts. | Actual minimum, multiple, or supplier agreement differs. | Validate current contract and process constraints. |
| A late proposal remains a proposal | Prevents interpreting ledger balance as proof of delivery. | Automated order release treats it as executable. | Require exception review and confirmation. |

## 12. Exercises

Use the worked-example conventions unless an exercise states otherwise.
Each exercise identifies the required deliverable and the evidence needed for acceptance.
Show quantities in pieces and dates in working weeks.

### Exercise 1 — Trace a dependency

**Time:** 10 minutes.
**Task:** Explain why a component's gross requirement is generally derived from the consuming parent's release rather than the parent's receipt.
Use pump P and cartridge A from the example.
State when 36 cartridges are needed and what generates that number.
**Deliverable:** Four to six sentences and one multiplication.
**Acceptance:** Identifies the week-3 pump release, the two-per-pump usage, and the week-3 component issue convention; distinguishes proposed release from actual order.

### Exercise 2 — Net a single item

**Time:** 15 minutes.
**Task:** An item starts with 17 pieces, has a scheduled receipt of 6 pieces in week 1, and has gross requirements of 14 pieces in week 1 and 28 pieces in week 2.
The lot policy is L4L, the lead time is one week, safety stock is zero, and receipts precede demand in each week.
**Deliverable:** A two-week table of gross, scheduled, ending balance, net requirement, planned receipt, and planned release.
**Acceptance:** Carries the week-1 ending balance forward and places the week-2 receipt's release in week 1.

### Exercise 3 — Combine shared paths

**Time:** 15 minutes.
**Task:** A pump release of 22 pieces and a cartridge release of 40 pieces both occur in week 3.
A pump consumes one housing and a cartridge consumes three housings.
The housing begins week 3 with 31 eligible pieces and has a scheduled receipt of 20 pieces in that week.
Its lot multiple is 100 pieces and safety stock is zero.
**Deliverable:** A trace of both gross-demand paths and a week-3 housing ledger.
**Acceptance:** Adds both requirements before using the shared stock, computes the minimum covering multiple, and reports ending balance.

### Exercise 4 — Resolve a past-due proposal

**Time:** 15 minutes.
**Task:** A receipt of 72 pieces is needed in week 2 and the normal lead time is three weeks.
The current planning origin is week 1.
Assume the requirement and eligible stock have already been validated.
**Deliverable:** Required release week, exception classification, and three possible responses with the responsible decision makers.
**Acceptance:** Does not move the historical release silently or represent the receipt as confirmed; includes supplier and customer/production consequences.

### Exercise 5 — Compare lot rules

**Time:** 20 minutes.
**Task:** An item starts with 10 pieces and has requirements of 35 pieces in week 1 and 25 pieces in week 2.
There are no scheduled receipts and safety stock is zero.
Compare L4L with a 40-piece multiple, assuming receipts can arrive before each week's requirement.
Ignore lead time for this exercise.
**Deliverable:** Two ledgers showing net requirement, receipt, and ending balance; a short trade-off statement.
**Acceptance:** Applies the lot rule to net rather than gross demand and reports both the number of positive planned receipts and final inventory.

### Exercise 6 — Measure plan changes

**Time:** 20 minutes.
**Task:** In a common action horizon, four matched planned orders have baseline quantities and dates `(40, W2)`, `(60, W3)`, `(50, W4)`, `(30, W4)`.
The next snapshot shows `(40, W3)`, `(80, W3)`, `(50, W4)`, `(20, W4)` for those same order identities.
A fifth new order of 25 pieces appears in the new snapshot.
**Deliverable:** Matched-order change incidence, sum of absolute quantity changes for matched orders, and a separate count of new orders; recommend one diagnostic before imposing a time fence.
**Acceptance:** Counts an order once even if more than one field changes; does not mix the new order into the matched denominator; identifies a plausible demand, supply, or master-data cause.

### Exercise 7 — Critique a planning proposal

**Time:** 20 minutes.
**Task:** A weekly plan shows a scheduled receipt of 100 seals in week 2 and a gross requirement of 90 seals in week 2.
The supplier says the receipt will arrive Friday; the line needs the seals Monday.
The stock report also includes 20 pieces on quality hold, and the buyer proposes an unapproved substitute.
**Deliverable:** Three distinct defects or risks, their consequences, and the owner of the next check.
**Acceptance:** Addresses intrabucket timing, stock eligibility, and substitute approval without assuming the substitute is usable.

### Exercise 8 — Design an exception workflow

**Time:** 25 minutes.
**Task:** Design a workflow for a past-due purchase release that threatens a customer commitment.
Include a calculation check, supplier response, production impact, controlled substitute decision, customer communication, and closure evidence.
**Deliverable:** A six-step decision record with owners, evidence, escalation trigger, and timestamps or service expectations.
**Acceptance:** Separates proposal from commitment, preserves the original exception, and assigns authority for changes to orders and promises.

## 13. Self-check answers

### Answer 1

The parent release represents the modeled start of the consuming order.
Under the worked example's convention, its components are issued in the same week.
The P release is 18 pumps in week 3, and each pump consumes two cartridges.
Therefore `18 pumps × 2 cartridges/pump = 36 cartridges` of gross requirement in week 3.
The planned receipt of P in week 4 is one week later and would create a late component requirement if used as the issue date.
The P release remains a proposal until the appropriate owner authorizes it.

### Answer 2

| Item, pieces | W1 | W2 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gross requirement | 14 | 28 |
| Scheduled receipt | 6 | 0 |
| Ending balance | 9 | 0 |
| Net requirement | 0 | 19 |
| Planned receipt | 0 | 19 |
| Planned release | 19 | 0 |

Week 1 ends with `17 + 6 - 14 = 9 pieces`.
Week 2 needs `28 - 9 = 19 pieces` after that carryforward.
L4L makes the week-2 receipt 19 pieces.
A one-week lead time places the proposed 19-piece release in week 1.

### Answer 3

The direct pump path contributes `22 × 1 = 22 housings` in week 3.
The cartridge path contributes `40 × 3 = 120 housings` in week 3.
Combined gross requirement is `22 + 120 = 142 housings`.
The eligible stock and scheduled receipt together cover `31 + 20 = 51 pieces`.
Net requirement is `142 - 51 = 91 pieces`.
The 100-piece multiple produces a 100-piece planned receipt and an ending balance of `31 + 20 + 100 - 142 = 9 pieces`.
The opening stock is used once, after both paths are combined.

### Answer 4

The release week is `2 - 3 = -1`, two weeks before the week-1 planning origin.
The proposal is past due; the week-2 receipt is not a confirmed supply.
The buyer can seek a supplier expedite and obtain a dated quantity and cost confirmation.
The production planner can investigate resequencing or an approved alternate source with engineering and quality.
If recovery is impossible, the authorized customer-service and production owners can revise the affected promise after assessing impact.
The exception remains open until the recovery action or revised commitment is documented.

### Answer 5

| Policy | W1 net | W1 receipt | W1 end | W2 net | W2 receipt | W2 end |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| L4L | 25 | 25 | 0 | 25 | 25 | 0 |
| 40-piece multiple | 25 | 40 | 15 | 10 | 40 | 30 |

Both policies create two positive planned receipts in these two weeks.
L4L ends with zero pieces; the fixed multiple ends with 30 pieces.
The fixed multiple may comply with packaging or setup constraints but ties up more inventory in this example.
There is not enough cost information to declare either policy economically optimal.

### Answer 6

Three of four matched orders changed: the first moved date, the second increased quantity, and the fourth decreased quantity.
The matched-order change incidence is `3 / 4 = 75%`.
The sum of absolute matched quantity changes is `0 + 20 + 0 + 10 = 30 pieces`.
The fifth order is one new order and is reported separately from the four matched orders.
Investigate whether the changes came from a real demand revision, a receipt update, a lot threshold, or a master-data correction before choosing a stabilizing control.
A fence may reduce automatic churn but must not hide a real shortage.

### Answer 7

A weekly balance may conceal a Monday shortage because Friday's receipt is too late for the issue.
The planner and buyer should confirm daily availability and recovery options.
The stock owner and quality team should identify whether the 20 held pieces were included in eligible inventory and correct the balance if needed.
Engineering and quality must evaluate and authorize any substitute before the buyer treats it as usable.
The consequence can be a line stop or a quality incident even if the weekly spreadsheet shows sufficient pieces.

### Answer 8 rubric

A strong design records the run ID, item, quantity, required date, original past-due release, and pegged impact.
It assigns the planner to verify the inputs and the buyer to obtain a supplier response by an explicit deadline.
It assigns production to assess resequencing and capacity, and engineering and quality to approve or reject a substitute.
It defines who may change the purchase order and who may revise a customer promise.
It retains the exception until an accepted recovery or revised commitment is evidenced.
It schedules a follow-up and captures the actual receipt or other closure event.
A weak design closes the exception when an email is sent, assumes the supplier agrees, or deletes the original late signal.

## 14. Interview questions

1. **How do you calculate a net requirement?**  
   Strong: states beginning balance, scheduled supply, gross demand, safety-stock policy, and timing.  
   Weak: subtracts all receipts without their dates or ignores carryforward.
2. **Why do components follow the parent release?**  
   Strong: explains dependent consumption and the issue-date convention.  
   Weak: assumes every component is needed at the parent's customer due date.
3. **How do you plan a component shared at two BOM levels?**  
   Strong: aggregates all parent contributions before a single netting pass and explains low-level ordering.  
   Weak: gives each parent the entire common inventory balance.
4. **What does a planned order release in the past mean?**  
   Strong: identifies an exception, validates inputs, and proposes recovery with owners.  
   Weak: changes the date to today and claims the original receipt is feasible.
5. **How does a fixed multiple change the ledger?**  
   Strong: rounds a positive net requirement up and carries excess stock into later periods.  
   Weak: rounds every gross requirement without netting.
6. **What is the difference between a planned order and a supplier commitment?**  
   Strong: distinguishes proposal, authorization, purchase order, and supplier confirmation.  
   Weak: assumes the system output binds the supplier.
7. **How would you measure nervousness?**  
   Strong: uses comparable snapshots, matching rules, horizon, date and quantity metrics, and new/canceled orders.  
   Weak: counts every changed field as an operational failure.
8. **What can a time fence protect, and what can it not do?**  
   Strong: describes a change-control boundary while keeping genuine shortages visible.  
   Weak: claims it creates inventory or guarantees service.
9. **Why can a weekly balanced plan still stop on Monday?**  
   Strong: explains within-bucket timing, quality release, and availability.  
   Weak: assumes every weekly receipt is available before every weekly issue.
10. **What checks precede release of the MRP proposal?**  
    Strong: mentions stock, BOM, dates, supplier and capacity feasibility, and decision authority.  
    Weak: treats mathematical consistency as a feasible schedule.
