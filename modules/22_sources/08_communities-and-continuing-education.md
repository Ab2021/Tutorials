## 1. Why this topic exists

Professional communities are where a field's working knowledge moves before it reaches journals. A conference talk, a society working group, a standards committee, and a mailing list can tell you what practitioners are actually doing months before a paper appears. They are also where the weakest evidence in a bibliography originates, because a conference abstract is not a peer-reviewed paper and a society's enthusiasm is not an appraisal.

The failures are specific:

- A slide cites "data presented at a conference" for a number that was never published and was later revised downward.
- A team treats a poster abstract as peer-reviewed evidence in a regulatory or investment document.
- A professional attends a large vendor-dominated conference, returns with product claims, and mistakes them for field consensus.
- A conference is assumed to be selective because it is large, when acceptance is broad and review is light.
- A predatory conference charges a fee, accepts in days, and issues a proceedings that looks citable.
- A continuing-education programme is counted in hours without anyone checking whether the content changed a practice.

The people affected are the analyst who cites a talk, the regulatory writer who needs a citable source, the clinician who needs to know what changed, and the learner who spends a budget on a meeting that does not improve their work.

This topic covers how to evaluate professional communities and their artefacts, how to read a conference programme critically, how to judge preprint servers, and how to build a continuing-education plan with hours and cost. It treats learning as a source-maintenance activity rather than as attendance.

The distinction matters because the cost of a professional community is not only the fee. It is the days away, the travel, and the attention, and those are scarce. A plan that spends them without an output is a plan that consumes the resource it was meant to build.

A reader who masters this topic will choose fewer venues, read their policies, and produce something from each. That is a smaller professional footprint and a larger return.

> **Note:** A conference abstract is a claim that something was done, not evidence that it was done well. The review standard varies from rigorous to nominal, and the artefact is often a 300-word summary without the analysis.

## 2. Learning outcomes

By the end of this topic you can:

1. **Classify** a community artefact — abstract, proceedings paper, journal paper, guideline, workshop, preprint — and state its evidence status. Assessed by Exercise 1 and self-check item 1.
2. **Evaluate** a conference or society for selectivity, review process, and commercial influence. Assessed by Exercise 2.
3. **Read** a conference programme critically and identify the sessions likely to contain settled versus preliminary work. Assessed by Exercise 3.
4. **Judge** a preprint server's moderation and versioning, and decide how a preprint can be used. Assessed by Exercise 4.
5. **Estimate** the abstract-to-publication rate for a topic and interpret it with an interval. Assessed by Exercise 5 and section 7.
6. **Detect** predatory conferences and journals using structural signals rather than reputation. Assessed by Exercise 6.
7. **Build** a twelve-month continuing-education plan with hours, cost, and expected output. Assessed by Exercise 7.
8. **Decide** which communities are worth the fee for a given role, and justify the choice. Assessed by Exercise 8.

The outcomes are ordered from classification to decision. A learner who can classify an artefact but cannot decide whether to pay for a venue has the vocabulary without the judgement.

Each outcome is observable in a document, and the exercises produce those documents rather than testing recall.

## 3. The core mental model

A community produces artefacts at different stages of maturity, and each stage carries a different evidential weight. The community's value is access to the stage, not the authority of the venue.

```text
                    COMMUNITY
        society | conference | working group | list | standards body
                              |
                              v
                    ARTEFACT TYPE
        abstract | proceedings | journal paper | guideline | preprint
                              |
                              v
                    REVIEW STANDARD
        full peer review | light review | moderation only | none
                              |
                              v
                    MATURITY
        preliminary | replicated | synthesised | recommended
                              |
                              v
                    USE DECISION
        signal | citation with caveat | evidence | practice change
```

Read the diagram downwards. The venue tells you which artefact type to expect, the artefact type tells you the likely review standard, and the review standard bounds the maturity claim.

**Venue prestige and review rigour are correlated, not identical.** A large conference can have a rigorous review process for full papers and a light one for abstracts. A small workshop can be more selective than a large meeting. The programme and the review policy are better guides than the name.

**Societies have positions.** A professional society advocates for its members, publishes guidelines, and sometimes sells credentials. Its educational content is useful and its policy statements are interested. The interest does not invalidate the content; it tells you what to check.

**Preprints are a distribution channel, not a review status.** Moderation checks scope and basic integrity; it does not check the analysis. The version history is the useful part, because it shows what changed before and after review.

**Continuing education is an input, not an outcome.** Hours attended are not competence gained. A plan that specifies hours, cost, and an output artefact is more useful than a list of meetings.

| Artefact | Typical review | Typical maturity | Permitted use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conference abstract | Light or none | Preliminary | Signal, hypothesis |
| Proceedings paper | Light to moderate | Preliminary to replicated | Citation with caveat |
| Journal paper | Full peer review | Replicated or not | Evidence with appraisal |
| Guideline | Structured review | Synthesised | Practice recommendation |
| Preprint | Moderation only | Preliminary | Signal, with version check |
| Workshop report | Variable | Consensus or opinion | Context |

The table is the practical reading key for a programme. A session's artefact type is usually visible in the programme, and the type bounds what the session can support before anyone hears it.

### 4.5 Signals of a weak venue

| Signal | What it suggests | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Acceptance in days | No substantive review | The stated review process |
| Broad, unrelated scope | A commercial operation | The programme committee |
| Fee required for acceptance | Pay-to-publish model | Refund and withdrawal policy |
| No named editors | No accountable board | The editorial page |
| Spam invitations | Mass solicitation | The organiser's history |
| Guaranteed publication | No selection | The acceptance criteria |
| Conference and journal bundled | A pipeline business | Indexing and review |
| No physical address | Unverifiable organiser | Registration details |
| Track record absent | No past editions | History check |
| Reviewers unnamed | No accountability | Committee page |
| Proceedings not indexed | No retrieval | Index search |
| Fee after acceptance | Pay-to-publish pipeline | Fee schedule |
| Unrelated topic list | Commercial harvesting | Scope check |
| Deadline pressure | Days to decide | Invitation timing |
| Generic acceptance letter | No substantive feedback | Review evidence |

The signals are structural because the alternative, judging by reputation, is unreliable. A new venue can be excellent and a familiar one can be weak, and the structure is what a learner can check before paying.

## 5. The mechanics, step by step

The procedure has 22 steps in five phases. It evaluates a community artefact and a learning plan with the same discipline: identify the object, establish the review standard, establish the maturity, decide the use, and record the decision.

The procedure is written for a learner who has limited time and budget. Its purpose is to prevent spending both on a venue that does not change practice.

### Phase A — Identify the community and the artefact

**Step 1. Name the community and its artefact.**
Input: the citation, the programme, or the invitation.
Output: the society or organiser, the event, and the artefact type.
Rationale: "presented at a conference" is not a source description, and the artefact type determines the review standard.
Branch: if the artefact is an abstract, record the abstract number and the meeting.
Edge case: a talk can summarise a paper that is already published, which changes the object.
Check: is the artefact type named.
Record: the community and artefact.

**Step 2. Establish the review process.**
Input: the venue's review policy or programme description.
Output: full peer review, light review, moderation only, or none.
Rationale: the review standard is the main determinant of how much weight the artefact can bear.
Branch: if the policy is not published, assume light review for abstracts and none for talks without a paper.
Edge case: a venue can review full papers rigorously and abstracts lightly.
Check: is the review process documented.
Record: the review standard.

**Step 3. Identify the programme committee and the track.**
Input: the programme and the committee list.
Output: the track, the chairs, and their affiliations.
Rationale: the committee indicates the field and the standard, and a track can differ from the main meeting.
Branch: if the track is industry-sponsored, treat the content as promotional until checked.
Edge case: a workshop can be more selective than the main track.
Check: are the chairs named.
Record: the committee and track.

**Step 4. Establish the artefact's maturity.**
Input: the abstract or paper.
Output: preliminary, replicated, synthesised, or recommended.
Rationale: maturity, not venue, determines whether the artefact can support a practice claim.
Branch: a single-centre preliminary result is a hypothesis, regardless of the venue.
Edge case: a late-breaking abstract is preliminary by design.
Check: does the artefact report replication or synthesis.
Record: the maturity.

### Phase B — Read the programme critically

**Step 5. Distinguish the session types.**
Input: the programme.
Output: a list of keynotes, oral sessions, posters, workshops, tutorials, and industry sessions.
Rationale: the session type signals the selection and the purpose, and industry sessions are not research.
Branch: if the programme does not distinguish industry sessions, look for sponsor logos and product names.
Edge case: a sponsored session can contain independent content, and it is still sponsored.
Check: are session types labelled.
Record: the session map.

**Step 6. Identify the work that is likely to be settled.**
Input: the programme and the abstracts.
Output: sessions reporting replication, multi-site results, or systematic reviews.
Rationale: settled work is more useful for a practice decision than a single-site preliminary result.
Branch: if a session is a "latest results" track, expect preliminary work.
Edge case: a methods session can be more durable than a results session.
Check: does the abstract report a design and a sample.
Record: the settled candidates.

**Step 7. Check the abstract's content against its claims.**
Input: the abstract text.
Output: whether it reports a design, a sample, an effect, and an uncertainty.
Rationale: an abstract without a design or an interval is a headline, not a result.
Branch: if the abstract reports only a p-value or a percentage, treat it as a signal.
Edge case: a structured abstract can still omit the analysis.
Check: are design, sample, effect, and uncertainty present.
Record: the assessment.

**Step 8. Check the proceedings for a full paper.**
Input: the proceedings or the venue's proceedings site.
Output: a full paper, an extended abstract, or nothing.
Rationale: the full paper is the artefact that can be appraised, and many abstracts never become one.
Branch: if there is a full paper, appraise it as a conference paper, not as a journal article.
Edge case: a proceedings paper can be a short version of a later journal article.
Check: is there a full paper.
Record: the proceedings status.

### Phase C — Check independence and interest

**Step 9. Identify sponsorship and commercial presence.**
Input: the programme, the sponsor list, and the exhibitor hall.
Output: the sponsors and their relationship to the content.
Rationale: a vendor-dominated meeting produces a view of the field filtered through products.
Branch: if a session is sponsored, the content is at least partly promotional.
Edge case: a society can be funded by vendors and still produce independent guidelines.
Check: is sponsorship disclosed.
Record: the sponsors.

**Step 10. Check speaker affiliations and disclosures.**
Input: the abstract and the speaker list.
Output: affiliations and disclosed conflicts.
Rationale: a talk by a vendor employee about the vendor's product is a product claim.
Branch: if disclosures are absent, treat the talk as interested.
Edge case: an academic with equity in a company is a disclosed conflict, not a disqualification.
Check: are disclosures present.
Record: the affiliations.

**Step 11. Check the society's position and advocacy.**
Input: the society's policy statements.
Output: the society's positions on the topic.
Rationale: a society advocates for its members, and its statements are interested.
Branch: if the society has a policy position, separate the educational content from the advocacy.
Edge case: a guideline can be both advocacy and evidence, and the methods section shows which.
Check: is there a position statement.
Record: the position.

**Step 12. Check whether the artefact is a preprint or a proceedings version.**
Input: the citation.
Output: the version and its status.
Rationale: a preprint or proceedings version can differ from the final paper, and the difference matters.
Branch: if the artefact is a preprint, check the version history.
Edge case: a conference paper can be superseded by a journal article with a different result.
Check: is the version identified.
Record: the version.

### Phase D — Decide the use

**Step 13. Decide whether the artefact is a signal or evidence.**
Input: the review standard and the maturity.
Output: signal, citation with caveat, evidence, or practice change.
Rationale: the use must match the artefact, and most conference content is a signal.
Branch: if the artefact is an abstract, it is a signal unless a full paper exists.
Edge case: a guideline presented at a conference is not the guideline until it is published.
Check: does the use match the artefact.
Record: the decision.

**Step 14. Check whether a journal version exists.**
Input: a search of the authors and title.
Output: the journal version, or a finding that none exists.
Rationale: the journal version is the citable object, and the conference version is a placeholder.
Branch: if a journal version exists, cite it and note the conference version only for priority.
Edge case: the journal version can have a different result, which is itself informative.
Check: is there a journal version.
Record: the version status.

**Step 15. Decide how to cite it.**
Input: the decision and the version.
Output: a citation with the meeting, the abstract number, and the date, or the journal citation.
Rationale: a conference citation must be identifiable, and "presented at a conference" is not.
Branch: if the abstract is not retrievable, cite the programme and the session.
Edge case: an abstract book can have a DOI, which should be used.
Check: is the citation resolvable.
Record: the citation.

**Step 16. Decide whether the community is worth the fee.**
Input: the role, the programme, and the cost.
Output: attend, attend remotely, send one person, or skip.
Rationale: the value of a meeting is in the access it provides, not in the certificate.
Branch: if the programme is mostly vendor sessions, the fee buys marketing.
Edge case: a small workshop can be worth more than a large meeting for a specialist.
Check: does the programme match the learning need.
Record: the decision and the reason.

### Phase E — Plan and record

**Step 17. Write the learning need.**
Input: the role and the current gaps.
Output: two or three specific capabilities to gain.
Rationale: a need statement turns attendance into a plan with an outcome.
Branch: if the need is "stay current", narrow it to a topic and a decision.
Edge case: a need can be met by reading rather than by attending.
Check: is the need specific.
Record: the need.

**Step 18. Choose the format that fits the need.**
Input: the need and the available formats.
Output: a conference, a course, a workshop, a reading plan, or a project.
Rationale: different needs are met by different formats, and a conference is often the most expensive way to learn a single method.
Branch: if the need is a skill, a workshop or project is better than a talk.
Edge case: a conference is good for orientation and network, not for depth.
Check: does the format match the need.
Record: the format.

**Step 19. Budget hours and cost.**
Input: the plan.
Output: hours, fees, travel, and the output artefact.
Rationale: a plan without a budget is a wish, and the budget makes the trade-off visible.
Branch: if the budget exceeds the allowance, choose the highest-output item.
Edge case: remote attendance can reduce cost without reducing content.
Check: are hours and cost stated.
Record: the budget.

**Step 20. Define the output.**
Input: the need and the format.
Output: a note, a summary, a prototype, or a change to a document.
Rationale: an output makes the learning checkable and shareable.
Branch: if there is no output, the learning is likely to be forgotten.
Edge case: an output can be a single page of notes circulated to the team.
Check: is the output defined.
Record: the output.

**Step 21. Record the plan and the evidence used.**
Input: the plan and the artefacts.
Output: a register entry with the community, the artefact, the use, and the date.
Rationale: the register is what makes the plan auditable and repeatable.
Branch: if an artefact was used in a document, link the two.
Edge case: a talk can be recorded in the register even when it produces no citable artefact.
Check: is the plan recorded.
Record: the register.

**Step 22. Review the plan at the end of the year.**
Input: the register and the outputs.
Output: a statement of what changed in practice and what did not.
Rationale: the review is the only evidence that the plan worked.
Branch: if nothing changed, change the plan rather than the hours.
Edge case: a plan can succeed by preventing a bad decision, which is hard to measure.
Check: is there a review.
Record: the review.

The procedure produces a defensible use of community material and a learning plan with an output. It does not produce expertise, and it does not turn an abstract into evidence.

### Value by role

| Role | Community value | Best format | Poor use of budget |
|---|---|---|---|
| Analyst | Methods and benchmarks | Workshop, small meeting | Large vendor expo |
| Clinical lead | Practice change | Society guideline session | Product showcase |
| Regulatory lead | Policy direction | Regulator-hosted sessions | Trade show |
| Product manager | Buyer priorities | Customer meetings | Academic conference |
| Researcher | Peer critique | Workshop, paper session | Keynote-only attendance |
| Learner | Vocabulary and network | Course, tutorial | Poster hall alone |

The table is a planning heuristic. The question it answers is not "which meeting is best" but "which meeting is best for this person this year".

### Preprint checks

- Which server, and what is its moderation policy.
- Which version, and what changed between versions.
- Is there a journal version, and does it agree.
- Is the analysis complete enough to appraise.
- Are data and code available.
- Are the authors identifiable and contactable.
- Is there public review or commentary.
- Has the preprint been withdrawn.

A preprint that has been through public review is stronger than one that has not, and it is still not a peer-reviewed paper.

### What a conference cannot give you

A conference cannot give you a skill, a settled result, or a defensible citation for a regulatory document. It can give you orientation, contacts, and an early view of what the field is arguing about.

A team that sends one person to a large meeting and expects the organisation to learn is usually disappointed. A team that sends one person with a specific question and an output to produce usually is not.

- An abstract is a claim that work exists.
- A poster is a claim with a conversation attached.
- A proceedings paper is a claim with a method attached.
- A journal paper is a claim with review attached.

The progression is the useful mental model for a programme.

### Community types and their artefacts

| Community type | Main artefact | Review standard | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large professional society | Abstracts, journal, guideline | Variable by track | Orientation, guideline |
| Specialist society | Abstracts, journal | Moderate to high | Depth in a specialty |
| Academic conference | Proceedings papers | Moderate | Methods and results |

A learner who wants depth should look at the specialist societies and workshops. A learner who wants orientation should look at the large meetings. The artefact types differ accordingly.

| Item | Typical cost tier | What it buys | What it does not |
|---|---|---|---|

| Specialist workshop | Moderate | Critique, depth | Breadth |

### Questions before paying a fee

- What artefact will I produce from this.
- Which sessions match a specific gap.
- Is the recording available afterwards.
- Is there a remote rate.
- Is the workshop content available separately.
- Would a book or a paper meet the same need.

A "no" to the first question is a reason to reconsider the spend.

The budget question is easier when the plan is written before the programme is opened. A learner who starts from the programme will find sessions to attend; a learner who starts from a need will find a smaller set that is worth the fee.

- A programme is a menu, not a curriculum.
- A certificate is a record of attendance, not of learning.
- A fee buys access, not expertise.

The three statements are the budget discipline in one line each.

| Session type | Typical selection | Typical content | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keynote | Invited | Overview or vision | Orientation |
| Oral session | Reviewed abstracts | New results | Signal |
| Poster | Reviewed abstracts | Preliminary results | Signal, with discussion |
| Workshop | Applied or invited | Methods and practice | Skill |
| Tutorial | Paid, often vendor-run | Tool training | Skill, with caveat |

A programme that lists session types lets a reader plan around maturity. A programme that does not is itself a signal about the organiser.

### Predatory conference checks

- Is there a named programme committee with affiliations.
- Is the review process described.
- Is the acceptance decision separated from the fee.
- Is the venue and date specific.
- Is the organiser contactable at a physical address.
- Is the conference associated with a known society or a recognised series.
- Are past proceedings indexed and retrievable.
- Are the topics coherent rather than a list of unrelated fields.
- Is the fee disclosed before submission.
- Does the invitation arrive unsolicited with a deadline measured in days.

Three or more negative answers indicate a venue whose proceedings should not be cited as evidence.

### A branch worked in miniature: the poster and the paper

A team finds a poster abstract reporting a 12 percent improvement in a benchmark. The abstract is from a large meeting and the number is attractive.

The first branch is to check the proceedings. If a full paper exists, appraise it: the split, the baseline, the metric, and the interval. The number may survive, shrink, or reverse.

The second branch is to check for a journal version. If one exists, it is the citable object, and the conference version is relevant only for priority.

The third branch is that neither exists. The abstract is then a signal of work in progress, and the correct use is to contact the authors or to wait. A model built on the abstract's number is built on a claim that has not been reviewed.

The wrong branch is the one that treats the poster as a finding because the meeting is well known.

## 6. Worked example

A slide reads: "Peer-reviewed evidence presented at the annual meeting shows a 12 percent improvement." The team wants to use the sentence in a payer discussion.

### Framing the claim

The sentence contains four claims:

1. **Venue claim.** The work was presented at an annual meeting.
2. **Review claim.** The work was peer-reviewed.
3. **Result claim.** The improvement was 12 percent.
4. **Use claim.** The result is strong enough for a payer discussion.

The venue claim is easy to check. The review claim is the one that is usually wrong. The result claim may be accurate and preliminary. The use claim depends on the first three.

| Sub-claim | Type | What could support it |
|---|---|---|
| Presented at the meeting | Event | The programme and abstract number |
| Peer-reviewed | Process | The venue's review policy |
| 12 percent improvement | Result | The abstract, and any full paper |
| Suitable for a payer discussion | Judgement | A full paper or a replicated result |

### Candidate sources

| # | Candidate | Type | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | The conference abstract | Abstract | The origin of the 12 percent |
| C2 | The conference review policy | Process document | The review claim |
| C3 | The proceedings paper, if any | Proceedings | The method and analysis |
| C4 | A journal version, if any | Journal article | The reviewed result |
| C5 | A news summary of the talk | Secondary | The likely source of "peer-reviewed" |
| C6 | The programme committee page | Process document | The committee and track |

### Scoring the candidates

**C1, the abstract.**
- Authority: moderate for the existence of the work.
- Currency: current for the meeting.
- Applicability: the study population, if stated.
- Accessibility: the abstract book or programme.
- Provenance: usually no methods beyond a summary.
- Verdict: signal only.

**C2, the review policy.**
- Authority: high for the process.
- Currency: current.
- Applicability: all abstracts at the meeting.
- Accessibility: usually on the venue site.
- Provenance: published by the organiser.
- Verdict: primary support for the review claim.

**C3, the proceedings paper.**
- Authority: moderate; conference review is lighter than journal review.
- Currency: current.
- Applicability: the study.
- Accessibility: proceedings site.
- Provenance: methods present, often short.
- Verdict: citation with caveat.

**C4, the journal version.**
- Authority: high, if peer reviewed.
- Currency: depends on the publication date.
- Applicability: the study.
- Accessibility: depends on the journal.
- Provenance: full methods and review.
- Verdict: primary support, if it exists.

**C5, the news summary.**
- Authority: low.
- Currency: current.
- Applicability: unclear.
- Accessibility: public.
- Provenance: secondary.
- Verdict: background, not support.

**C6, the committee page.**
- Authority: high for the committee composition.
- Currency: current.
- Applicability: the track.
- Accessibility: public.
- Provenance: organiser.
- Verdict: context for the review claim.

### What the review policy usually says

Conference review policies vary, and the variation is the point:

| Policy type | What it means | What it supports |
|---|---|---|
| Full paper review, multiple reviewers | Comparable to a light journal review | Citation with caveat |
| Abstract review for relevance and form | Screening, not appraisal | Signal only |
| Single-reviewer or chair decision | Minimal review | Signal only |
| No review, programme committee invitation | Curated, not reviewed | Context |
| Industry session | Sponsor control | Marketing |

The phrase "peer-reviewed" is accurate only for the first row, and even there the review is usually lighter than a journal's.

### The arithmetic of publication

If 120 abstracts in a topic are tracked and 66 reach a full publication, the proportion is 55.0 percent with a 95 percent Wilson interval of 46.1 to 63.6 percent. If 20 of the 120 are still in preparation and are excluded, the proportion among completed attempts is 66.0 percent, with an interval of 56.3 to 74.5 percent.

The numbers are illustrative and the method is in section 7. The interpretation is not illustrative: a substantial share of abstracts never becomes a paper, and the share varies by field and by meeting.

### Verdict

| Sub-claim | Verdict | Supportable sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Peer-reviewed evidence | Reject | "An abstract presented at the annual meeting reported a 12 percent improvement." |
| 12 percent improvement | Signal | "The abstract reports a 12 percent improvement; the analysis is not available." |
| Suitable for a payer discussion | Reject | Requires a full paper or a replicated result |

The corrected sentence is weaker and it is honest. It also gives the team a reason to find the full paper, which is the next step in any credible payer conversation.

### Two diagnostics that would change the verdict

**Diagnostic 1: a journal version with the same result.** If a peer-reviewed journal article reports the same 12 percent with a method and an interval, the review claim becomes supportable, and the sentence can cite the article rather than the meeting.

**Diagnostic 2: a proceedings paper with a full analysis.** If the proceedings contain a full paper with the split, baseline, and interval, the result is citable with a caveat about the review standard, and the "peer-reviewed" claim is still too strong but the result claim is supportable.

A third diagnostic is a public review or commentary on the abstract, which would raise the maturity without making it a journal paper.

### What the team should record

The register records the abstract, the meeting, the review policy, the proceedings status, and the journal status. The slide is rewritten to cite the abstract, and a task is opened to find the journal version before the payer meeting.

The case produces a rule: never write "peer-reviewed" unless you have read the review policy and the artefact it applies to. The phrase is a claim about a process, and the process is documented or it is not.

### What a payer reviewer asks about a conference citation

- Is the study reported in a peer-reviewed journal.
- What is the comparator and the population.
- Is the effect absolute or relative.
- Is there a published analysis with an interval.
- Is the result replicated.

A conference abstract rarely satisfies more than one of these. A journal article usually satisfies several, which is why the payer conversation starts there.

### How to find the journal version

- Search the title and the first author.
- Search the trial or cohort identifier, if one is reported.
- Check the authors' publication lists.
- Check the society's journal, which often publishes the full paper.
- Contact the corresponding author if the abstract is recent.

The search takes minutes and changes the citation from a signal to evidence when it succeeds.

### What the abstract can still tell you

Even when it is not evidence, an abstract is useful. It tells you who is working on the problem, which methods are being tried, and what the field considers an interesting result. That is orientation, and it is valuable.

The mistake is to use an orientation artefact as a decision artefact. The fix is not to ignore conferences; it is to use them for the question they answer.

- Orientation is a legitimate use.
- Citation is a different use.
- A payer decision is a third use.

Each use has its own artefact, and the abstract serves only the first.


## 7. Code, query, or calculation

The script estimates the proportion of abstracts that reach a full publication and attaches a Wilson interval. The interval matters because samples of a few dozen abstracts produce wide ranges, and a point estimate invites overreading.

```python
"""Abstract-to-publication tracking with a Wilson interval."""

import math

TRACKED = 120
PUBLISHED = 66


def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    denom = 1 + z * z / n
    centre = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / denom
    half = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / denom
    return p, centre - half, centre + half


p, lo, hi = wilson(PUBLISHED, TRACKED)
print(f"tracked {TRACKED}   published {PUBLISHED}")
print(f"proportion {p:.1%}   95% CI {lo:.1%} to {hi:.1%}")

for pending in (0, 10, 20):
    n = TRACKED - pending
    p2, lo2, hi2 = wilson(PUBLISHED, n) if n > 0 else (0, 0, 0)
    print(f"excluding {pending:2d} pending: {p2:.1%} ({lo2:.1%} to {hi2:.1%})")

print()
print("median time to publication: 14 months (sample assumption)")
print("conference abstracts are a signal, not a settled result")
```

Expected output:

```text
tracked 120   published 66
proportion 55.0%   95% CI 46.1% to 63.6%
excluding  0 pending: 55.0% (46.1% to 63.6%)
excluding 10 pending: 60.0% (50.7% to 68.7%)
excluding 20 pending: 66.0% (56.3% to 74.5%)

median time to publication: 14 months (sample assumption)
conference abstracts are a signal, not a settled result
```

Three readings matter. The interval is about 17 percentage points wide, which is the honest precision for a sample of this size. Excluding abstracts still in preparation raises the proportion, which is why the denominator has to be defined before the number is quoted. And the median time to publication is an assumption in the script, not a measured value; in a real analysis it would come from the tracking data.

The Wilson interval is used rather than the normal approximation because the proportion is not near 0 or 1 and the sample is moderate; the normal approximation behaves poorly in those conditions.

To adapt the script, replace the counts with your tracked set and record the date each abstract was first presented. A publication rate without a follow-up window is ambiguous, because a recent abstract has had less time to become a paper.

### A venue audit in eight checks

- Who organises it, and can they be contacted.
- Is the review policy published.
- Is the programme committee named.
- Are sessions typed and labelled.
- Are sponsors disclosed.
- Are proceedings indexed and retrievable.
- Is the fee transparent.
- Does the programme match a stated need.

Three or more negative answers are a reason to skip the fee and read the proceedings instead.

### A programme-reading routine

- Read the session types before the titles.
- Mark the sessions that report a design and a sample.
- Mark the industry sessions and the sponsored tracks.
- Choose one methods session over three results sessions.
- Note the two sessions most relevant to a current decision.
- Plan the output before the meeting.
- Skip the rest without guilt.

The routine takes twenty minutes and turns a programme into a plan.

### What to do after the meeting

- Write the output within a week.
- Record the artefacts and their status in the register.
- Send the notes to the team, with the sources.
- Follow up with two people, not twenty.
- Check whether any abstract has a journal version.

The week after the meeting is when most of the value is either captured or lost.

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why it is needed | What breaks it | How to test it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The review policy describes practice | The review claim depends on it | Policy not followed | Ask authors, compare quality |
| The abstract reflects the work | The signal depends on it | Abstract overstates | Find the full paper |
| The journal version exists for important work | The evidence depends on it | Work never published | Track the rate |
| The venue is what it claims | The evaluation depends on it | Predatory organiser | Structural checks |
| Sponsorship is disclosed | Bias assessment depends on it | Undisclosed funding | Check the programme |
| CME credit implies quality | Credit is a proxy | Accredited but promotional | Read the content |
| The learner's need is known | The plan depends on it | Need is "stay current" | Narrow to a decision |
| The output is achievable | The plan depends on it | No time after the event | Schedule the output |
| The cost fits the budget | The plan is feasible | Fees exceed allowance | Choose remote or reading |
| The community is accessible | Participation depends on it | Membership barriers | Check eligibility |
| The programme reflects the field | Orientation depends on it | Vendor capture | Check session types |
| The recording is available | Learning can be asynchronous | No recording | Check the platform |
| The output time is protected | Learning transfers | Calendar conflict | Schedule it |
| The fee is within budget | The plan is feasible | Travel costs | Compare remote options |

Two limits are structural. First, a community cannot certify the truth of a talk; it can only describe the process that selected it. Second, a learning plan cannot create time; it can only allocate it.

A third limit is access. Membership, travel, and fees exclude some learners, and the free alternatives — preprints, recorded sessions, society journals, and working groups — are often enough for orientation.

## 12. Exercises

**Exercise 1 — Artefact classification (recall, 20 minutes).**
Classify five citations as abstract, proceedings paper, journal paper, guideline, or preprint, and state the review standard each implies.
Deliverable: a five-row table with artefact, venue, and review standard.
Acceptance criteria: each classification is justified; at least one citation is an abstract presented as evidence.
Time: 20 minutes.

**Exercise 2 — Venue evaluation (application, 30 minutes).**
Evaluate one conference you attend using the eight checks in section 10.
Deliverable: a one-page assessment with the checks and a verdict.
Acceptance criteria: each check has evidence; the verdict states whether to attend, attend remotely, or skip.
Time: 30 minutes.

**Exercise 3 — Programme reading (critique, 30 minutes).**
Read a conference programme and identify three sessions likely to be settled and three likely to be preliminary, with reasons.
Deliverable: a two-column list with reasons.
Acceptance criteria: reasons refer to session type, abstract content, or committee; at least one industry session is identified.
Time: 30 minutes.

**Exercise 4 — Preprint assessment (application, 25 minutes).**
Take one preprint in your field and assess its moderation, version history, and journal status.
Deliverable: a short assessment with the version cited and the status.
Acceptance criteria: the server's policy is checked; the version is recorded; a journal version is searched for.
Time: 25 minutes.

**Exercise 5 — Publication rate (calculation, 35 minutes).**
Track ten abstracts from a meeting two years ago and estimate the publication proportion with an interval.
Deliverable: a table of abstracts and status, plus the proportion and interval.
Acceptance criteria: the search method is described; the interval is computed; non-publication is recorded as a finding, not a failure.
Time: 35 minutes.

**Exercise 6 — Predatory check (critique, 20 minutes).**
Evaluate an unsolicited conference invitation against the structural signals in section 5.
Deliverable: a short finding with the signals present and a recommendation.
Acceptance criteria: at least five signals are assessed; the recommendation is specific; no reputational claim is made without evidence.
Time: 20 minutes.

**Exercise 7 — Personal plan (design, 40 minutes).**
Build a twelve-month plan with needs, formats, hours, cost tier, and outputs.
Deliverable: a table with at least eight activities and a total.
Acceptance criteria: each activity maps to a need and an output; hours and cost are stated; the plan fits the available budget.
Time: 40 minutes.

**Exercise 8 — Fee decision (design, 25 minutes).**
Decide whether one specific meeting is worth the fee for your role, and write the decision with its rationale.
Deliverable: a one-page memo with the need, the programme evidence, the cost, and the alternative.
Acceptance criteria: the memo names what the fee buys; it names what it does not; the alternative is concrete.
Time: 25 minutes.

### Marking guide

| Exercise | Evidence of a pass | Common failure |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Classification | Review standard stated | Venue named only |
| 2 Venue | Eight checks with evidence | Impression only |
| 3 Programme | Reasons tied to evidence | Session titles only |

## 13. Self-check answers

**1. What is the difference between an abstract and a proceedings paper?**
An abstract is a short summary, often reviewed only for relevance; a proceedings paper is a fuller document with methods, reviewed to a variable standard. Neither is a journal article.

**2. Why is "peer-reviewed" a process claim rather than a quality claim?**
Because it describes a review that occurred, not the outcome of that review. A peer-reviewed paper can be wrong, and a lightly reviewed abstract can be right; the phrase only tells you what process was applied.

**3. What does a society's policy statement establish?**
It establishes the society's position at a date. It is interested advocacy, and it is evidence of what the community thinks rather than of what the evidence shows.

**4. Why do many abstracts never become papers?**
Because the work is preliminary, the results are negative, the authors move on, or the analysis does not survive review. The rate varies by field, and it is one reason an abstract is a signal.

**5. What should you check before citing a preprint?**
The server's moderation policy, the version, whether a journal version exists, and whether the preprint has been withdrawn. The citation should name the version and the date.

**6. What makes a conference predatory?**
A fee-driven acceptance process, no substantive review, an unverifiable organiser, a broad and incoherent scope, and proceedings that are not indexed. The signals are structural, not reputational.

**7. Why is CME credit not a measure of learning?**
Because it measures attendance at an accredited activity, not the change in capability or practice. A plan with outputs is a better measure.

**8. What is the value of a large meeting?**
Orientation, contacts, and a view of what the field is arguing about. It is poor value for depth and skills, which are better obtained in workshops and projects.

**Rubric for Exercises 5 and 7.** A strong publication-rate exercise describes the search, computes the interval, and interprets it. A weak exercise reports a proportion without a denominator or a window. A strong plan maps every activity to a need and an output. A weak plan lists meetings without hours or cost.

**9. Why is an abstract book a weak citation?**
Because it often lacks page numbers, DOIs, and a stable publisher, and the abstract may never have been reviewed beyond screening. A citation to it should include the meeting, the abstract number, and the date.

**10. What does a late-breaking abstract signal?**
It signals recency and light review. The work may be important and it is preliminary, so it should be treated as a signal until a paper exists.

**11. Why can several abstracts from one study look like replication?**
Because the same cohort can be presented at several meetings with different outcomes. The citations multiply while the evidence does not, and a reader who counts abstracts overstates the evidence.

**12. What is the difference between an overlay journal and a preprint server?**
An overlay journal reviews preprints and publishes the reviewed version; a preprint server only moderates and posts. The review status is the difference, not the platform.

**13. What should a learner do with a recorded session?**
Watch it with a question, take notes against that question, and produce a short output. A recording watched without an output is entertainment with a professional label.

**14. Why is a society journal a useful membership benefit?**
It provides a curated stream of methods and policy content at a lower cost than attending, and it is the artefact that can be cited.

**15. What makes a conference citation resolvable?**
The meeting name, the year, the abstract number, and the authors. A citation that says "presented at a conference" is not resolvable, and a citation that adds the session title is better but still weak.

**16. Why should the review policy be read rather than inferred from the venue's reputation?**
Because the same venue can review full papers rigorously and abstracts lightly, and reputation does not distinguish the two. The policy does.

**17. What is the value of a poster discussion?**
It gives direct access to the authors at a stage when the work can still be influenced, which is useful for collaboration and for understanding preliminary results.

**18. How would you handle a talk that reports unpublished data?**
Treat it as a signal, ask the speaker whether a manuscript exists, and record the contact and the date. Do not cite it as evidence.

## 14. Interview questions

**1. How do you decide whether a conference is worth attending?**
Strong answer: starts from a learning need, checks the programme and review policy, and compares the cost with the output. Weak answer: attends because the meeting is well known.

**2. What is the difference between an abstract and a paper?**
Strong answer: review standard, length, method detail, and maturity; an abstract is a signal and a paper is evidence. Weak answer: says they are the same work.

**3. How would you evaluate a preprint?**
Strong answer: checks moderation, version, journal status, and whether the analysis is complete enough to appraise. Weak answer: treats it as unreviewed and ignores it.

**4. Why might a society's guideline be interested?**
Strong answer: societies advocate for members and can have funding relationships; the methods section shows the evidence review and the conflicts. Weak answer: assumes societies are neutral.

**5. What makes a conference citation acceptable in a regulated document?**
Strong answer: usually nothing; a conference abstract is not adequate support, and a journal paper or the guideline is required. Weak answer: cites the abstract if it is from a famous meeting.

**6. How do you keep learning current without attending everything?**
Strong answer: a small set of alerts, one society, one working group, and a reading routine with outputs. Weak answer: tries to attend more.

**7. What would you check about a CME activity?**
Strong answer: accreditation, disclosures, content, and whether it produces an output relevant to practice. Weak answer: counts the credits.

**8. How do you tell a good workshop from a bad one?**
Strong answer: selectivity, the committee, the format, and whether participants produce something. Weak answer: judges by the venue's size.

**9. Why is a poster session useful?**
Strong answer: it provides discussion with authors about preliminary work, which is valuable for orientation and for finding collaborators. Weak answer: treats posters as lower-quality papers.

**10. How do you decide which communities to join?**
Strong answer: maps the role's decisions to the communities that publish the relevant methods and guidance, and joins the smallest set that covers them. Weak answer: joins the largest society.

**11. What does a programme committee tell you about a venue?**
Strong answer: the field, the standard, and whether the track is industry-led; a named committee is a signal of accountability. Weak answer: says it is a list of names.

**12. How would you use a conference to test an idea?**
Strong answer: presents preliminary work to get critique, then uses the feedback to improve the study before publication. Weak answer: attends without presenting.

**13. What is the risk of choosing a meeting by its location?**
Strong answer: the programme, not the city, determines the value; a convenient meeting with the wrong programme is an expensive holiday. Weak answer: treats location as neutral.

**14. Why should a learning plan include a reading component?**
Strong answer: reading is the cheapest way to cover breadth, and meetings are better for critique and network. Weak answer: replaces reading with attendance.

**15. What would make you leave a session early?**
Strong answer: the content is a product pitch, the method is not described, or the session does not match the stated need. Weak answer: never leaves, to justify the fee.

**16. How do you compare two conferences for the same need?**
Strong answer: compares programme types, review policies, costs, and outputs for the specific need. Weak answer: compares size or location.

**17. What should a team do with what one person learned at a meeting?**
Strong answer: require a written output circulated within a week and recorded in the source register. Weak answer: relies on a verbal debrief.

**18. Why is a workshop often better than a large meeting for a method?**
Strong answer: the format allows critique and practice, and the selection can be more focused than a large programme. Weak answer: assumes bigger is better.
