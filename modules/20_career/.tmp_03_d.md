
## 10. Failure modes and red flags

| Failure | Early warning sign | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Duty list instead of evidence | Most bullets start with "responsible for" | Describes presence, not contribution | Reader cannot infer capability | Rewrite with action, method, metric, consequence |
| No numbers | Zero digits in the experience section | Scale and effect are invisible | Reader assumes junior scope | Add a denominator and a period to three bullets |
| Unverifiable metric | You cannot explain the baseline | The number was estimated or inherited | Interview collapse or post-offer problem | Reconstruct the source or soften the claim |
| Wrong dialect | Strong work, no screens | Vocabulary does not match the target function | Filtered before a human reads | Keyword audit against three postings |
| Title inflation | Your title differs from the offer letter's records | Attempt to match a target level | Background check mismatch | Normalise with the official title in parentheses |
| Over-length | Three pages for eight years | Everything kept, nothing edited | Reader skims and remembers nothing | Cut to the page budget in Step 5 |
| Parsing failure | Copy-paste scrambles the text | Tables, columns, text boxes | Sections disappear in the ATS | One column, standard headings, text test |
| Hidden keywords | White text or metadata terms | Attempt to game ranking | Detected as dishonesty | Keywords only in visible, truthful context |
| Publication dump | Two pages of citations for a non-research role | Academic habit | Signals wrong target | Three selected publications with DOIs |
| Skills over-claim | A tool listed that you used once | Breadth signalling | Interview question exposes depth | List only tools you can discuss for five minutes |
| Gap concealed | Dates rounded to hide months | Fear of stigma | Verification finds the discrepancy | Use real dates; address the gap in one line |
| Generic profile | Headline is the current job title | Profile not maintained | Invisible in recruiter search | Rewrite headline with target role and skills |
| No referral path | 100 percent inbound applications | Only one channel used | Low conversion regardless of quality | Allocate half the search time to conversations |
| Confidential detail | Client name or patient detail in a bullet | Enthusiasm to show scale | Governance breach | Describe the class, not the client |

Two red flags deserve separate treatment because they end candidacies rather than delaying them.
A false claim about a licence or a degree is disqualifying in every health employer, and it is
checked. A resume that contains patient identifiers is a reportable incident at some employers,
even if the candidate is not hired.

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why it is needed | What breaks it | How to test it |
|---|---|---|---|
| A resume is screened before it is read | It justifies the top-third design | Some small employers read everything | Ask contacts how their process works |
| Three postings represent a family's vocabulary | It bounds the keyword audit | Niche roles with distinct dialects | Check whether a fourth posting adds new must-match terms |
| Metrics improve credibility | They supply scale and effect | Metrics that cannot be explained | Rehearse the denominator for every number |
| One page is right under eight years | It forces editing | Countries and employers where two pages are normal | Follow the convention of the target country |
| ATS parsing is the main technical risk | It drives the format rules | Human-only processes at small employers | Run the copy-to-text test anyway; it is cheap |
| Referrals dominate inbound | It justifies the channel split | Roles with structured pipelines and large intakes | Track your own channel conversion |
| Title normalisation is acceptable | Internal titles are unreadable | Employers that treat any change as misrepresentation | Keep the official title in parentheses |
| LinkedIn search indexes the headline and skills | It justifies the rewrite | Recruiters who search only by title | Check whether you appear in a search for the target title |
| The document can be maintained cheaply | It justifies the master file | Fast-changing tools and roles | Review quarterly; update the master after each role |

The main limit is that the document cannot create evidence. It can make existing evidence legible
and it can stop a reader from drawing a wrong inference, but a thin history produces a thin
resume no matter how it is written. When the document is strong and the response rate is low, the
problem is usually the target list or the channel, not the wording.

## 12. Exercises

**Exercise 1. Rung audit (critique, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: every bullet in your current resume labelled with its rung from 1 to 6 and a
one-line reason.
Acceptance criteria: at least half the bullets are labelled 1 to 3; the three bullets to rewrite
first are identified; no bullet is labelled 5 or 6 without a named decision.

**Exercise 2. Bullet rewrite (application, 60 minutes).**
Deliverable: six bullets rewritten in the five-part form, each with a metric, a period, and a
consequence.
Acceptance criteria: no bullet begins with a weak opener; each metric has a denominator; a reader
can state what changed after reading each bullet.

**Exercise 3. Summary (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a two- to three-line summary for one target family.
Acceptance criteria: it names the role noun, the domain, one proof point with a number, and the
target; it contains no "passionate," "results-driven," or "leverage."

**Exercise 4. Title and date normalisation (application, 20 minutes).**
Deliverable: a table of every role with official title, market title, dates, and any explanation
required.
Acceptance criteria: no title overstates the role; every gap longer than three months is either
explained or accepted; dates are month and year.

**Exercise 5. Parsing test (application, 20 minutes).**
Deliverable: the plain-text extraction of your PDF, with a note on any lost or reordered content.
Acceptance criteria: contact details, headings, employers, titles, and dates survive in order;
the file passes a manual ATS-style field extraction.

**Exercise 6. LinkedIn rebuild (application, 60 minutes).**
Deliverable: headline, about section, three experience entries, and top 10 skills.
Acceptance criteria: the headline contains the target role and two searchable skills; the first
two lines of the about section state the role and a proof point; skills match the resume.

**Exercise 7. Keyword audit (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a coverage table for three postings with a decision (add, learn, ignore) for every
missing term.
Acceptance criteria: every "add" is defensible in an interview; at least one term is marked
"ignore" with a reason; coverage is computed, not estimated.

**Exercise 8. Two-reader test (critique, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: notes from two readers, one domain expert and one not, with the 30-second decision
each made.
Acceptance criteria: both readers can name the target role; at least one misreading is recorded
and fixed; the fixes are replacements, not additions.

## 13. Self-check answers

**1. What distinguishes a responsibility statement from an achievement claim?**
A responsibility statement describes a duty that existed whether or not you performed it well. An
achievement claim names what changed, by how much, over what scope, and how it was measured. The
test is whether the sentence would still be true if a different person had held the role.

**2. Write a bullet for "reduced query rate" in the five-part form.**
"Designed 42 edit checks for a 14-site registry, which reduced the query rate at database lock
from 7.9 percent to 1.8 percent between the pilot and primary study." The action is designed, the
method is edit checks, the metric has a denominator (queries per data point at lock), the scope is
14 sites, and the consequence is a lower rework burden before lock.

**3. What must a summary contain?**
The target role noun, the domain, one proof point with a number, and the target. It should not
contain adjectives about personality. A recruiter uses it to confirm the match; a manager uses it
to decide whether to keep reading.

**4. When may you change a title?**
When the official title is uninterpretable outside the employer, and the change is a translation
rather than an upgrade. Keep the official title in parentheses. Never change a level word
(associate to senior, analyst to scientist) unless the employer's own records use that word.

**5. What is the parsing test?**
Copy the PDF text into a plain editor and inspect the order and completeness. If a section is
missing or the contact block is broken, an ATS may see the same failure. It takes two minutes and
catches the most common formatting defect.

**6. Why use the same claims on the resume and LinkedIn?**
Because a recruiter may read either first and an interviewer may compare them. Inconsistent
claims create a credibility problem that has nothing to do with the work. The packaging differs
because the readers differ; the facts do not.

**7. Coverage is 0.34. What do you add?**
Nothing yet. Decide for each missing term whether you have the experience. If you do, rewrite a
bullet to show it. If you do not, either build the evidence (a portfolio artifact) or mark it
"learn" and accept the gap. Adding the word without the evidence produces an interview you cannot
pass.

**8. How do you handle a 20-month gap?**
Use real dates. Add one line in the summary naming the reason if it aids the narrative and the
return if it is recent. Prepare a one-sentence answer and two sentences on current evidence. Do
not apologise, and do not invent consulting work.

**Rubric for Exercise 2.** Full marks: six bullets, all with a strong opener; each has a metric
with a denominator; scope and period are stated; no bullet exceeds about 30 words; the claims are
ones the candidate can defend.

**Rubric for Exercise 8.** Full marks: two readers, one domain and one not; the 30-second decision
recorded for each; at least one misreading identified; fixes are replacements rather than
additions; the revised version is retested.

## 14. Interview questions

**1. "Walk me through this bullet. What was the baseline?"**
Strong: names the baseline, the measurement period, and who else was involved. Weak: repeats the
bullet or says the number came from a report they did not build.

**2. "You list causal inference. What estimand did you target?"**
Strong: names the estimand, the confounding strategy, and one sensitivity analysis. Weak:
describes a regression and calls it causal.

**3. "Why did you move from a CRO to a payer?"**
Strong: connects the decision type (population health, coverage) to the evidence they want to
build. Weak: says they wanted a new challenge.

**4. "Your resume says you trained four programmers. What did you teach?"**
Strong: names the curriculum, the assessment, and the outcome. Weak: says they answered
questions.

**5. "What is not on your resume that should be?"**
Strong: names a relevant piece of evidence and explains why it was cut. Weak: says nothing was
cut.

**6. "How do you decide what to leave off?"**
Strong: describes the page budget, the target family, and the cut order. Weak: says they include
everything to be safe.

**7. "You have a gap. What happened?"**
Strong: one factual sentence, then evidence of current capability. Weak: a long justification or
an evasion.

**8. "Why is your title different from your offer letter?"**
Strong: explains the internal title and the market translation, with the official title
preserved. Weak: becomes defensive or admits inflating.

**9. "How do you tailor your resume without overfitting to one posting?"**
Strong: three postings, recurring terms, a stable summary, and bullets selected per application.
Weak: changes the whole document for every application or changes nothing.

**10. "What would you change about your resume now?"**
Strong: names a specific weakness and a specific fix. Weak: says it is complete.

## 15. Key terms

- **Achievement bullet** — a statement of action, method, metric, and consequence.
- **Applicant tracking system (ATS)** — software that receives and parses applications.
- **Background check** — verification of employment, education, and record.
- **Boolean search** — recruiter query using AND, OR, NOT, and phrases.
- **Coverage** — the weighted share of posting requirements evidenced on the resume.
- **Curriculum vitae (CV)** — an exhaustive academic record.
- **Denominator** — the base against which a percentage is computed.
- **Employment gap** — a period without employment.
- **Endorsement** — a connection's confirmation of a skill.
- **Functional resume** — a layout organised by skill rather than chronology.
- **Headline** — the line under a LinkedIn name.
- **Keyword** — a term a recruiter or ATS searches for.
- **Keyword stuffing** — repeating terms to game ranking.
- **Open to work** — a LinkedIn availability setting.
- **ORCID** — a persistent researcher identifier.
- **Parsing** — extraction of structured fields from a document.
- **Referral** — a candidate introduced by an employee.
- **Requisition** — an approved, funded opening.
- **Responsibility statement** — a description of duties.
- **Reverse chronological** — ordering roles from most to least recent.
- **Right to work** — legal authorisation to work in a jurisdiction.
- **Scope** — the size of what you influenced.
- **Shortlist** — candidates forwarded to the hiring manager.
- **Skills section** — a grouped list of competencies.
- **Sourcing** — proactive identification of candidates.
- **Summary** — two to three lines naming the target role and core evidence.
- **Title normalisation** — restating an internal title in market language.
- **Work authorisation** — the legal right to work, with or without sponsorship.
