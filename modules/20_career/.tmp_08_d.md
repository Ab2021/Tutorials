
## 10. Failure modes and red flags

| Failure | Early warning sign | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Team story | "We decided" throughout | Habit of collaborative language | Interviewer cannot score contribution | Rewrite with precise "I" and accurate team credit |
| No decision | The story describes activity | Tasks were mistaken for actions | Reads as junior | Name the choice and the alternative |
| No result | The story ends at the action | Result felt obvious to the candidate | No evidence of effect | Add a number or a concrete outcome |
| No reflection | The lesson is "communication is important" | Reflection rehearsed as a slogan | No evidence of learning | Name the change you now make |
| Blame story | The failure belongs to someone else | Fear of admitting error | Inferred low ownership | Choose a failure that is yours |
| Conflict without resolution | "Eventually she understood" | The disagreement was never closed | Inferred avoidance | Describe the decision and the relationship |
| Over-rehearsed delivery | The answer sounds recited | Script memorised word for word | Discounted as inauthentic | Rehearse structure, not sentences |
| Five-minute answer | The interviewer interrupts | No time discipline | Structure lost, scoring abandoned | Practise at 90 seconds |
| Fabrication | Details change between tellings | Embellishment to fit a rubric | Reference check or probe exposes it | Use only true stories |
| Ignoring the panel | You answer only the lead interviewer | Nerves | Other panellists score disengagement | Include the room |
| No questions | You ask about benefits only | Preparation focused on answers | Read as passive | Prepare three questions |
| Defensive under probe | You repeat the story louder | Probe experienced as attack | Low coachability score | Acknowledge, clarify, answer the underlying question |
| Confidential detail | Client name or patient detail appears | Enthusiasm to show scale | Governance breach | Describe the class, not the identifier |

Two red flags are about the employer. A behavioural stage with no defined competencies and no
consistent questions is likely to be scored on impression, which disadvantages candidates who do
not resemble the interviewer. A question about family plans, health, or age is improper in most
jurisdictions; you are not obliged to answer, and you may end the process.

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why it is needed | What breaks it | How to test it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The employer uses a competency rubric | It justifies the coverage matrix | Some interviews are unstructured | Ask what the stage assesses |
| Eight to ten stories cover most questions | It bounds preparation | Unusual frameworks with twelve competencies | Map the framework before writing |
| Past behaviour predicts future behaviour | It justifies the format | People change contexts and roles | Treat stories as evidence, not prophecy |
| 90 seconds is the right default length | It fits most stages | Some panels allow longer answers | Ask the interviewer how much detail they want |
| A specific change beats a large success | It reflects rubric weighting | Some cultures reward scale | Watch the interviewer's follow-ups |
| Probes are tests, not attacks | It prevents defensiveness | Some interviewers are hostile | Answer the underlying question, then stop |
| The same story can serve two competencies | It reduces preparation | The emphases conflict | Tell it once per competency in practice and compare |
| Confidential work can be described generically | It enables stories from regulated roles | The detail is the evidence | Prepare the generic version with your manager |
| Reflection can be learned | It justifies the rewriting | Some incidents genuinely taught nothing | Choose a different incident |

The main limit is that the behavioural stage measures presentation as well as behaviour. A
candidate who has done the work but cannot narrate it will score below one who narrates well. That
is unfair and it is the format. The response is not to invent stories but to practise the true ones
until the evidence is audible.

## 12. Exercises

**Exercise 1. Competency decode (recall, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: six competencies from one posting, each with the behaviour the rubric likely rewards.
Acceptance criteria: each competency is phrased as an observable behaviour; at least one is inferred
from a responsibility rather than a requirement.

**Exercise 2. Eight stories (application, 2 hours).**
Deliverable: eight stories in the six-line structure, each with a number and a specific change.
Acceptance criteria: every story has a decision, a result, and a change; no story is told entirely
in "we"; each fits on one page.

**Exercise 3. Coverage matrix (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a matrix of stories by competencies with strong, partial, or blank entries.
Acceptance criteria: every competency has at least one strong entry or a named gap; no competency
relies on a single story for a role with six or more questions.

**Exercise 4. Two lengths (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: recordings of three stories at 90 seconds and 3 minutes.
Acceptance criteria: structure audible at both lengths; action is the longest section; the
90-second version is within 15 seconds of target.

**Exercise 5. Failure story (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: one failure story that passes ownership, specificity, and change, plus a one-line
answer to "what would you do differently."
Acceptance criteria: the failure is the candidate's decision; the moment of noticing is named; the
change is observable.

**Exercise 6. Conflict story (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: one conflict story with both interests, the evidence, the escalation decision, the
resolution, and the relationship afterwards.
Acceptance criteria: the other party's position is described as reasonable; the resolution names
who decided; the relationship outcome is stated.

**Exercise 7. Probe drill (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a recording of three probes answered without repeating the original story.
Acceptance criteria: each answer adds information; none becomes defensive; the ownership answer
distinguishes your part from the team's.

**Exercise 8. New-employer adaptation (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: the bank remapped to a second employer's competencies, with two new emphasis notes.
Acceptance criteria: every competency covered; at least one story re-emphasised for a different
competency; the changes are recorded in one page.

## 13. Self-check answers

**1. What is a behavioural question testing?**
A disposition the employer has defined as a competency, evidenced by a specific past action in a
specific situation. The story is the vehicle; the behaviour is the payload.

**2. What are the six lines of a strong answer?**
Situation, task, action, result, insight, transfer. The action is the longest and must contain a
decision.

**3. Why map stories to competencies before writing?**
Because the interviewer scores against a rubric. Writing first produces stories about what you find
interesting rather than what the role assesses.

**4. How long should a behavioural answer be?**
90 seconds by default, with a 3-minute version available if the interviewer asks for detail. The
action should take about half the time.

**5. What makes a failure story strong?**
The failure is your decision, the moment you noticed is named, the consequence is stated, and the
change is specific enough to be observed in your behaviour afterwards.

**6. What makes a conflict story strong?**
Both interests are described as reasonable, the evidence is named, the escalation decision is
explained, the resolution names who decided, and the relationship afterwards is stated.

**7. How do you answer a probe without repeating the story?**
Add information: the specific contribution, the alternative you rejected, the cost, or what you
would still change. Repeating the original answer signals that there is nothing underneath it.

**8. What do you do if you have no story for a competency?**
Say so, offer the nearest equivalent, and describe how you would approach the situation. Do not
invent a story; the probe will expose it.

**Rubric for Exercise 5.** Full marks: the failure is the candidate's decision; the signal missed
is named; the consequence is stated; the change is observable; the story is under 2 minutes.

**Rubric for Exercise 6.** Full marks: both interests; evidence rather than authority; an explicit
escalation decision; a named resolution; the relationship outcome; no blame.

## 14. Interview questions

**1. "Tell me about a time you failed."**
Strong: your decision, the missed signal, the consequence, the change. Weak: a circumstance story.

**2. "Tell me about a conflict with a clinician."**
Strong: both interests, evidence, escalation, resolution. Weak: "we agreed to disagree."

**3. "Tell me about a time you influenced without authority."**
Strong: the coalition, the framing, the decision changed. Weak: "I convinced them."

**4. "Tell me about a time you had to say no."**
Strong: the trade-off, who was protected, what was cut. Weak: says they never say no.

**5. "Tell me about a mistake that affected a patient."**
Strong: the error, the disclosure, the system change. Weak: minimises or hides behind process.

**6. "Tell me about a time you worked in ambiguity."**
Strong: how you scoped the problem and what you tested first. Weak: "I figured it out."

**7. "Tell me about developing someone."**
Strong: what you taught, how you measured it, what they can do now. Weak: "I mentored juniors."

**8. "Why are you leaving your current role?"**
Strong: a forward-looking reason, no disparagement. Weak: complaints about a manager.

**9. "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager."**
Strong: the issue, how you raised it, what happened, the relationship afterwards. Weak: claims
never to disagree.

**10. "What would your colleagues say about you?"**
Strong: a specific behaviour with an example. Weak: adjectives.

## 15. Key terms

- **Accountability** — the obligation to answer for a decision.
- **Anchored rating scale** — a scale with behavioural anchors per level.
- **Behavioural consistency** — past behaviour predicts future behaviour.
- **Behavioural question** — a question about what you did in a past situation.
- **Blameless review** — incident review focused on system causes.
- **Competency** — a defined behaviour or skill assessed by the employer.
- **Conflict** — a disagreement about goals, methods, or priorities.
- **Credit sharing** — naming others' contributions accurately.
- **Escalation** — raising an issue to someone with authority.
- **Evidence** — specific action, number, or artifact supporting a claim.
- **Failure** — an outcome that did not meet the goal.
- **Follow-up probe** — a question asking for more detail on a claim.
- **Fabrication** — inventing or embellishing a story.
- **Influence without authority** — changing a decision without formal power.
- **Insight** — what you learned about the situation or yourself.
- **Near miss** — an event that could have caused harm but did not.
- **Ownership** — answering for your part in an outcome.
- **Psychological safety** — the belief that speaking up is safe.
- **Reflection** — what you learned and changed.
- **Resolution** — a decision or process that ends a disagreement.
- **Root cause** — the underlying reason an event occurred.
- **Situational question** — a hypothetical scenario question.
- **Speak-up** — raising a concern about safety or conduct.
- **STAR** — situation, task, action, result.
- **STARI** — STAR plus improvement.
- **Transfer** — applying the learning to a new context.
