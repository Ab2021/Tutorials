
## 10. Failure modes and red flags

| Failure | Early warning sign | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base-only comparison | You quote one number per offer | Equity and bonus are unfamiliar | Wrong decision by tens of thousands | Compute four-year total |
| Accepting immediately | You say yes on the call | Relief or politeness | Leaves approved money unclaimed | Ask for 48 to 72 hours and counter once |
| Bluffing a competing offer | You cannot name the employer | Pressure to anchor high | Credibility loss and a lower final offer | Use only real alternatives |
| Negotiating before the written offer | Numbers change between calls | Verbal terms are provisional | Confusion and mistrust | Ask for the package in writing first |
| Asking for five things | Your email is a list | Fear of missing a lever | Employer says no to the package | One primary, one fallback |
| Ignoring the band | You ask above the posted maximum | Research skipped | Request declined, relationship strained | Read the posted range and the level |
| Ignoring internal equity | You demand parity with a colleague | Pay is confidential and uneven | Employer cannot explain it internally | Ask for a level review or a signing bonus instead |
| Equity valued at grant price | You treat options as cash | Grant statements look like money | Overstated offer value | Scenario analysis with probabilities |
| Missing the clawback | You spend the signing bonus | Repayment terms unread | Owed money if you leave early | Read the repayment agreement |
| Not checking the grant agreement | Vesting differs from the offer summary | The letter is a summary | Unexpected cliff or exercise window | Request the grant agreement before signing |
| No pre-acceptance questions | You learn about on-call in week one | Focus on money | Regret and early exit | Ask the eight questions |
| Ignoring geography | You compare nominal offers | Cost of living ignored | A lower real standard of living | Apply a published index |
| Deadline pressure | The offer expires in 24 hours | Artificial urgency | Decision under duress | Ask for an extension in writing |

Two red flags are about the employer. A refusal to put agreed terms in writing is a governance
signal, and the terms you cannot document are the ones that will be forgotten. A band that moves
substantially after one ask suggests the first offer was not made in good faith, which is worth
weighing in the decision.

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why it is needed | What breaks it | How to test it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The posted band is the real band | It anchors the ask | Some employers post a wide range for multiple levels | Ask which level the offer is for |
| Equity has positive expected value | It justifies inclusion in total comp | Many startups return zero | Value under a downside scenario |
| Cost-of-living indices are comparable | They enable cross-city comparison | Indices weight housing and consumption differently | Use the same index for all offers and state it |
| The recruiter can influence the offer | It justifies the conversation | Some bands are fixed by a committee | Ask what flexibility exists |
| A counter is expected | It justifies asking | Some employers make a final first offer | Ask whether the offer is negotiable |
| 48 to 72 hours is available | It enables computation | Some offers are genuinely urgent | Ask for the deadline in writing |
| The role is as described | It justifies accepting | Postings and interviews can differ from the job | Ask the pre-acceptance questions |
| Future raises are percentage-based | It justifies negotiating base | Some employers use fixed increases | Ask how the review cycle works |
| The BATNA is stable | It determines leverage | Another offer can expire | Confirm dates before the call |

The main limit is that negotiation advice is conditional on a market you cannot fully observe. The
band, the internal equity, and the approver's mood are hidden. The method reduces the uncertainty; it
does not remove it. A polite ask that is declined costs almost nothing, and the expected value of
asking is positive in most mid-level hiring processes.

## 12. Exercises

**Exercise 1. Market range (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a range for one role and geography from three named sources, with the quantity each
reports and its limitation.
Acceptance criteria: sources are dated; the quantities are normalised to base or total; the range has
a low, midpoint, and high; at least one limitation is stated per source.

**Exercise 2. Total rewards (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a four-year total for two offers, with every component shown.
Acceptance criteria: base, bonus, equity, signing, and match are separate lines; the arithmetic is
reproducible; the comparison states which offer is higher and by how much.

**Exercise 3. BATNA (application, 20 minutes).**
Deliverable: a written BATNA, reservation, target, and walk-away, each with one sentence of
justification.
Acceptance criteria: the BATNA is real and dated; reservation is above walk-away; target is within
the researched range.

**Exercise 4. The ask (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a 60-second script with enthusiasm, the specific ask, the reason, and the fallback.
Acceptance criteria: one primary lever; a reason tied to market evidence or scope; no personal
expenses; delivered in under 60 seconds.

**Exercise 5. Lever selection (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a lever recommendation for four scenarios (base at band max, visa constraint, level too
low, remote constraint).
Acceptance criteria: each recommendation names a primary and a fallback; the reason references the
employer's constraint; at least one scenario correctly avoids asking for base.

**Exercise 6. Geography (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: two offers compared nominally and with a published cost-of-living index.
Acceptance criteria: the index and its year are cited; the adjustment is applied to the total, not
only base; the conclusion states whether the ranking changes.

**Exercise 7. Equity scenarios (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a three-scenario valuation of a private-company grant with probabilities and an expected
value.
Acceptance criteria: strike, share count, and vesting are stated; the downside is zero or the strike;
the expected value is labelled as a judgement; the comparison includes illiquidity.

**Exercise 8. Pre-acceptance memo (design, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a one-page memo with the eight questions, the answers you received, and a
recommendation.
Acceptance criteria: every question has an answer or a stated unknown; the recommendation is
conditional on the unknowns; at least one answer changes the decision.

## 13. Self-check answers

**1. Why build a range from three sources?**
Because each source has a different bias. Posted ranges are employer-specific but only available
where disclosure is required; government statistics are large but aggregate occupations; crowdsourced
data are level-specific but self-reported and skewed. Three sources with different biases produce a
range you can defend.

**2. Compute the four-year value for a $155,000 base, 10 percent bonus, $80,000 RSU over four years,
$10,000 signing, and 4 percent match.**
Annual cash is $155,000 × 1.10 + $155,000 × 0.04 = $170,500 + $6,200 = $176,700. Four years of cash
is $706,800; add $80,000 of equity and $10,000 signing for $796,800.

**3. What is a BATNA, and why must it be real?**
The best alternative to a negotiated agreement: what you do if this offer fails. It must be real
because a bluff is testable, and a discovered bluff damages credibility for the rest of the process.

**4. What are the four parts of a good ask?**
Enthusiasm and commitment, the specific adjustment, the reason, and the fallback lever.

**5. Base is at the band maximum. What do you ask for?**
A signing bonus, additional equity, or a written review date. Asking for base above the maximum
requires a level change, which is a different conversation.

**6. How does cost-of-living adjustment change a comparison?**
It converts nominal offers to a common geography. In the worked example, the Raleigh offer rises from
$679,880 to $755,422 in Boston-equivalent terms, closing part but not all of the gap.

**7. Why is startup equity valued in scenarios?**
Because the value depends on an exit price and liquidity that are unknown. A single number at the
preferred price overstates the expected value and hides the probability of zero.

**8. Name four pre-acceptance questions.**
Decision rights without escalation; success criteria and who judges them; on-call and pager
responsibility; why the role is open and what happened to the previous holder.

**Rubric for Exercise 5.** Full marks: four scenarios; a primary and fallback for each; the reason
references the employer's constraint; at least one scenario avoids base; visa and level scenarios are
handled correctly.

**Rubric for Exercise 8.** Full marks: eight questions with answers or stated unknowns; a conditional
recommendation; at least one answer changes the decision; the memo is under one page.

## 14. Interview questions

**1. "What are your salary expectations?"**
Strong: a researched range with a reason, or a deferral that asks for the band. Weak: a single number
with no basis, or "whatever is fair."

**2. "Why do you want this role?"**
Strong: the employer's problem, the role's decision, and the transferable evidence. Weak: mission
language.

**3. "Do you have other offers?"**
Strong: a factual statement of processes and timelines, with no invented numbers. Weak: a bluff.

**4. "How do you compare offers?"**
Strong: four-year total, equity terms, cost of living, scope, and review cadence. Weak: base salary.

**5. "What would make you decline?"**
Strong: a specific scope or safety condition. Weak: says nothing would.

**6. "How do you value equity?"**
Strong: scenarios, strike price, vesting, liquidity, and exercise window. Weak: quotes the grant
value as cash.

**7. "What are your notice period and start date?"**
Strong: a specific date with buffer and a confirmation of the notice terms. Weak: "as soon as
possible."

**8. "Are you negotiating with anyone else?"**
Strong: a factual answer that protects confidentiality and states the timeline. Weak: invents
competition.

**9. "What questions do you have for us?"**
Strong: decision rights, success criteria, on-call, funding, and review cycle. Weak: benefits only.

**10. "Why is the band structured this way?"**
Strong: level, geography, and internal equity. Weak: assumes the band is arbitrary.

## 15. Key terms

- **409A valuation** — independent valuation of private-company common stock.
- **Anchor** — the first number in a negotiation.
- **Base salary** — fixed annual pay before bonus and equity.
- **BATNA** — best alternative to a negotiated agreement.
- **Clawback** — repayment obligation if you leave early.
- **Cliff** — the period before any equity vests.
- **Comp band** — the approved range for a level and geography.
- **Cost-of-living index** — relative price index for a geography.
- **Equity** — ownership or appreciation rights granted over time.
- **Exercise window** — period after leaving to exercise options.
- **Exploding offer** — offer with an unreasonably short deadline.
- **Internal equity** — pay consistency across employees at the same level.
- **Level** — the rung that determines band and scope.
- **Non-compete** — clause restricting future employment.
- **Notice period** — required period before leaving.
- **Percentile** — position within a market distribution.
- **Refresh** — additional equity granted after the initial grant.
- **Reservation price** — the minimum acceptable package.
- **Restricted stock unit (RSU)** — a promise of shares that vest on a schedule.
- **Retirement match** — employer contribution to a retirement plan.
- **Signing bonus** — one-time payment at start.
- **Salary history ban** — prohibition on asking about prior pay.
- **Stock option** — right to buy shares at a strike price.
- **Strike price** — price at which an option can be exercised.
- **Target bonus** — bonus at 100 percent performance.
- **Total compensation** — base plus bonus plus equity value.
- **Total rewards** — compensation plus benefits and non-cash terms.
- **Vesting** — schedule on which equity becomes yours.
