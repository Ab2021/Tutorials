## 1. Why this topic exists

A resume is not a record of what you did. It is a screening instrument read by two people with
different questions, in sequence, under time pressure. A recruiter asks whether you match the
requisition well enough to forward. A hiring manager asks whether you can own the decision the
role carries. Both read the top third of the first page first, and both stop when the evidence
does not appear.

The translation problem is specific to regulated biomedical work. A clinical research
coordinator's actual output is a clean database and a passed monitoring visit, but the resume
line says "managed study documents." A postdoc's actual output is a validated pipeline that
three other labs now use, but the resume line says "performed bioinformatics analysis." A
pharmacovigilance analyst's actual output is a signal assessment that changed a label, but the
resume line says "conducted case reviews." In each case the work is strong and the claim is
invisible. The reader cannot infer what was not written.

### 1.1 The two readers

**The recruiter** is screening against a requisition, often across several roles, and usually
has 30 to 90 seconds per resume. They look for title match, years, location, work authorisation,
and a small number of required skills. They are not evaluating your statistics. Their risk is
forwarding a candidate who will be rejected by the hiring manager.

**The hiring manager** reads a shorter stack, more carefully, and is evaluating risk. Can this
person do the work in the first 90 days? Will they need supervision I cannot provide? Do they
understand the domain well enough to be trusted with clinical or regulatory stakeholders? They
read for ownership verbs, scale, and outcomes, and they discount adjectives.

A resume that satisfies the recruiter and fails the hiring manager is common: it is a keyword-
dense list of tools with no evidence of judgment. A resume that satisfies the manager and fails
the recruiter is also common: it is a dense technical narrative with no title match and no
location line. The document has to satisfy both, which is why the ordering of information
matters more than the wording of any single bullet.

### 1.2 Why the seven-second finding matters, and what it does not prove

A widely cited vendor study by Ladders (2018) used eye-tracking to estimate that recruiters spend
about 7.4 seconds on an initial resume screen, up from about 6 seconds in the company's 2012
study. The study is a vendor marketing asset with a small sample recruited for the exercise, and
it measures an initial screen rather than a hiring decision. It should not be treated as a
precise benchmark. It is useful for one design conclusion: the first screen is a pattern-matching
task, so the pattern has to be legible without reading.

The pattern includes a clear title line, a recent role with a recognisable employer and dates, a
short summary that names the target role, and bullets whose first four words carry the claim.
Everything else is read only if the pattern matches.

### 1.3 Who this blocks

**Candidates from academia** write for reviewers who will read the whole document. They lead
with publications, use passive constructions, and describe methods rather than decisions. Their
resume reads as a CV, which signals "still an academic" to a recruiter even when the skills fit.

**Candidates from clinical roles** undersell operational evidence. A nurse who built a
departmental audit that changed a handoff protocol has evidence of influencing clinical
practice, which is exactly what a clinical informatics role wants. The resume says "participated
in quality improvement."

**Career changers from outside health** over-claim domain fluency and under-claim transferable
evidence. They write "passionate about healthcare" and omit the operations analytics that would
actually transfer.

**International candidates** face format expectations that differ by country (a two-page CV is
normal in much of Europe; a one-page resume is expected in the United States), plus work
authorisation statements that must be accurate and are frequently mishandled.

### 1.4 What this topic produces

By the end of Section 5 you will have a one- or two-page resume with a target-role summary, six
to ten rewritten bullets, a projects section built from portfolio artifacts, a skills section
that is defensible in an interview, and a LinkedIn profile whose headline, about section, and
experience entries use the same evidence in a searchable form. You will also have a keyword
audit against three real postings and a two-person readability test.

It does not cover cover letters, which are increasingly optional and are covered briefly in
Section 4, or interview performance, which is Topics 04 to 08. It does not cover salary
negotiation (Topic 09). It does not claim that any wording guarantees a callback; the evidence
on resume interventions is thin and mostly vendor-produced.

### 1.5 The honesty constraint

Every claim on a resume is a claim you will be asked to defend, and some are verifiable. Dates,
titles, degrees, licences, and certifications are checked by background screeners. Publications
are checked by DOI. Metrics are checked by asking you to explain the denominator. A resume that
inflates is not a clever optimisation; it is a liability that surfaces after the offer, when the
cost of withdrawing is highest.

The rule used throughout this topic: every bullet must survive three questions. What was the
decision? What did you personally do? How would you know the number is right?

### 1.6 The asymmetry between effort and attention

Candidates spend days on wording and reviewers spend seconds. The correct response is not to
write faster; it is to put the effort where the reviewer looks. A single afternoon spent on the
title line, the summary, and the first four words of each bullet returns more than a week spent
polishing the last page.

The asymmetry also explains why small defects are expensive. A misplaced date, a title that does
not match the target, or a bullet that begins with "assisted" are each trivial in isolation and
each costs a rung in the reader's inference. The document is judged by its weakest visible
element because the reviewer has no time to average.

## 3. The core mental model

A resume is a stack of claims ordered by expected reader attention, and every claim must be
traceable to evidence you can produce on request. The document is not a summary of your history;
it is a curated set of proofs for the role in front of you.

```text
  READER PATH (top third of page 1)

  [ Name | target-role line | location | contact ]
        |
        v
  [ Summary: 2-3 lines, role + evidence + domain ]
        |
        v
  [ Most recent role: title, employer, dates, 3-5 bullets ]
        |
        v
  [ Earlier roles: fewer bullets, older = shorter ]
        |
        v
  [ Projects: portfolio artifacts, one line each ]
        |
        v
  [ Skills: only what you can defend ]
        |
        v
  [ Education, licences, publications ]
```

### 3.1 The evidence ladder

Each bullet sits somewhere on a ladder from activity to consequence. The higher the rung, the
more it survives screening.

| Rung | Form | Example | Reader inference |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Activity | "Responsible for data cleaning" | Can follow instructions |
| 2 | Method | "Cleaned data using Python and structured query language" | Has used tools |
| 3 | Output | "Built a cleaning pipeline for 14 sites" | Delivers artifacts |
| 4 | Metric | "Reduced query rate from 8% to 2%" | Measures work |
| 5 | Decision | "Changed site inclusion criteria based on the query pattern" | Owns judgment |
| 6 | Consequence | "Cut database lock time by three weeks across two studies" | Affects the organisation |

Most biomedical resumes sit at rungs 1 to 3. The candidates who get interviews sit at 4 to 6,
and they keep rungs 5 and 6 for the two or three bullets that matter most. A page of rung-6
claims reads as inflation; a page of rung-2 claims reads as junior.

### 3.2 Two dialects, one document

The same work is described differently by different functions.

| Work | Academic dialect | Industry dialect |
|---|---|---|
| Analysed trial data | "Conducted statistical analysis of longitudinal outcomes" | "Owned the primary analysis for a 640-patient Phase II trial; the result supported the endpoint selection for Phase III" |
| Built a model | "Developed a machine learning model achieving 0.87 area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC)" | "Built a readmission model that improved area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) from 0.21 to 0.29 over the incumbent screen; validated on the most recent 12 months" |
| Managed data | "Maintained the study database" | "Owned data quality for a 14-site registry; query rate at lock was 1.8%, with zero critical findings at inspection" |
| Wrote a protocol | "Contributed to study design" | "Drafted the estimand and sensitivity analysis section of the SAP for a 1,200-patient study" |

The industry dialect is not more impressive; it is more specific. Specificity is what survives a
seven-second read.

### 3.3 What the document is competing with

For any given posting, the shortlist is built from three pools: referrals, recruiter-sourced
candidates, and inbound applicants. Inbound is the largest and least differentiated pool. A
resume can move you within the inbound pool; it cannot beat a referral on its own. That is why
Topic 01's targeting and this topic's positioning are complementary: the resume is necessary and
insufficient.

### 3.4 LinkedIn is a search index, not a poster

LinkedIn works differently from a resume because the first reader is often a search query, not a
person. Recruiters filter by title, location, current employer, skills, and keywords, then read
the headline and the first two lines of the current role. The profile therefore needs the terms
a recruiter would search for in the places the search indexes: headline, job titles, skills, and
the first lines of the about section.

This is why the same evidence is written twice. The resume is optimised for a human reading
top-down in seven seconds. The profile is optimised for a Boolean search and then a human
reading the first two lines. The claims must be identical; the packaging differs.

> **Note:** Keyword matching is a discovery mechanism, not a persuasion mechanism. A profile
> stuffed with every tool in the field ranks in searches and converts poorly, because the human
> who opens it finds no evidence. Match the vocabulary, then prove two or three claims.

## 4. Definitions and vocabulary

| Parsing | Extraction of fields from a document into structured data | Assumed to be perfect for every format | Process |
| Keyword | A term a recruiter or ATS searches for | Stuffed into white text or a hidden section | Term |
| Boolean search | Recruiter query using AND, OR, NOT, and quoted phrases | Confused with keyword frequency | Query |

| Sourcing | Proactive identification of candidates by recruiters | Confused with inbound applications | Activity |

| Achievement bullet | A statement of action, method, metric, and consequence | Written as a job description | Bullet |
| Responsibility statement | A description of duties | Used as the default bullet form | Bullet |

## 5. The mechanics, step by step

The rewrite takes 15 to 25 hours for a first pass and 3 to 5 hours per subsequent target. Do it
in the order below; the summary depends on the bullets, and the keyword audit depends on the
target.

### Step 1. Harvest evidence before writing

**Input:** the accountability statements from Topic 01 and the portfolio index from Topic 02.
**Output:** 20 to 30 candidate claims, each with a number, a date, and an artifact.
**Rationale:** writing from the existing resume reproduces its weaknesses. Harvesting from the
evidence inventory produces claims the old document never contained.

For each claim, record four fields:

```text
claim:        what changed
metric:       the number and its denominator
scope:        patients, sites, studies, users, budget
artifact:     where a reader could verify it
```

If the artifact field is empty, the claim can still be used, but it must be one you can defend
orally in detail. Claims with no artifact and no detail are removed.

### Step 2. Choose the target and its dialect

**Input:** three postings from the primary family.
**Output:** a list of 10 to 15 recurring terms and the two or three decisions the role owns.
**Rationale:** a resume tailored to a family outperforms a generic resume, and tailoring to a
single posting overfits. Three postings give the stable vocabulary without the noise of one
team's phrasing.

Extract from each posting: the job title, the first three responsibilities, the required tools,
the domain terms (claims, electronic health records, CDISC, FHIR, health economics and outcomes
research), and the level signals. Terms appearing in two or more postings go on the must-match
list. Terms appearing once go on the optional list.

### Step 3. Normalise titles and dates

**Input:** your employment history.
**Output:** a title line that a market reader can interpret, with the official title preserved
where it differs.
**Rationale:** internal titles are often uninterpretable outside the employer. "Clinical Data
Associate II" could be a data manager, a programmer, or an analyst. The reader will not guess.

| Situation | Acceptable | Not acceptable |
|---|---|---|
| Internal title is obscure | "Data Analyst (internal title: Clinical Data Associate II)" | Renaming yourself "Senior Data Scientist" |
| Title is inflated by the employer | Use the official title; let the bullets show scope | Downgrading to appear modest |
| Role was a postdoc | "Postdoctoral Researcher, Biomedical Imaging" | "Data Scientist" with no explanation |
| Contract or agency role | "Data Analyst (contract via [agency]), [client]" | Hiding the agency |
| Part-time or unpaid | State the arrangement if the scope is relevant | Presenting it as full-time |

Dates: month and year, consistent format, no unexplained gaps longer than three months. If a
role was concurrent, say so; overlapping dates without explanation read as an error.

### Step 4. Write bullets with the five-part form

**Input:** the harvested claims.
**Output:** six to ten bullets, distributed across the two most recent roles.
**Rationale:** the form forces the method and the consequence into the same sentence, which is
what the reader needs to infer competence.

```text
[Action verb] [what you built or changed] using [method], which [consequence],
for [scope].
```

Transformations:

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| "Responsible for statistical programming for clinical trials" | "Programmed 23 Phase I–III oncology studies in SAS, delivering 100% of analysis datasets on or before the data cut, with zero quality findings across four sponsor audits." |
| "Worked with claims data" | "Built a claims-based cohort of 48,000 commercially insured adults, reducing the 30-day readmission cohort from 61,000 to 48,000 by applying continuous-enrollment and index-event rules." |
| "Helped with dashboards" | "Replaced a monthly Excel report with a governed dashboard used by 14 regional managers, cutting preparation time from 3 days to 2 hours per cycle." |
| "Performed data quality checks" | "Designed 42 edit checks for a 14-site registry; query rate at database lock fell from 7.9% to 1.8% between the pilot and the primary study." |

Rules for verbs: use "built," "designed," "owned," "led," "reduced," "cut," "delivered,"
"validated," "migrated." Avoid "responsible for," "assisted with," "participated in," "helped,"
"supported," and "worked on." Those verbs describe presence, not contribution.

Rules for numbers: a percentage needs a denominator; a count needs a period; a saving needs a
baseline. "Reduced by 37%" is weaker than "reduced alerts per 1,000 patient-days from 41 to 26."
If you cannot disclose the absolute number, give the relative change and say the baseline is
confidential.

### Step 5. Decide what to cut

**Input:** the full claim set.
**Output:** a page budget with a fixed number of bullets per role.
**Rationale:** length is a signal of editing, not of experience. A two-page resume with 20
bullets reads as an inventory; a one-page resume with 8 strong bullets reads as judgment.

| Experience | Length | Bullets | Publications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 8 years, industry target | 1 page | 3 to 5 most recent role, 2 to 3 prior, 2 projects | Omit or one line |
| 8 to 15 years | 2 pages | 5 most recent, 3 prior, 2 earliest | One line if relevant |
| PhD with 4+ papers, research target | 2 pages, or CV plus resume | 4 to 6 | Selected list, 3 to 5 with DOIs |
| Clinical background, informatics target | 1 to 2 pages | 4 clinical, 3 analytics | Omit unless relevant |
| Academic application | CV, any length | Not applicable | Full list |

Cut in this order: oldest roles to one line, coursework, hobbies, references, "Microsoft Office,"
soft-skill lists, and any bullet without a number that is not the only evidence of a required
skill.

### Step 6. Format for parsing and scanning

**Input:** the drafted content.
**Output:** a document that parses cleanly and reads in an F-pattern.
**Rationale:** parsing failures are silent. A two-column layout with a text box for skills can
lose the entire skills section before a human sees it.

Format rules:

- One column, standard headings: Summary, Experience, Projects, Skills, Education, Licenses.
- No tables, text boxes, headers, footers, or images for content that matters.
- Standard font, 10.5 to 12 point, margins of at least 0.7 inch.
- Dates right-aligned on the same line as the title, or immediately below.
- File name: `Lastname_Firstname_Resume.pdf`.
- Export to PDF from the source document, and check that the text is selectable.
- If the employer requests DOCX, send DOCX; some parsers handle DOCX better than PDF.

The parsing test: copy the entire PDF text into a plain text editor. If the order is scrambled,
sections are missing, or the contact line is broken, the ATS may see the same thing.

### Step 7. Build the projects section from the portfolio

**Input:** two portfolio artifacts.
**Output:** two to four project entries, each three lines.
**Rationale:** projects are the only place where a career changer can show domain work, and the
only place where a candidate can show a decision without an employer's confidentiality.

Each entry:

```text
Project name | one line: decision, method, result | link to case study and code
```

Example: "Readmission risk model (public claims extract) | temporal validation of a
gradient-boosted model against the incumbent rule; AUPRC 0.21 to 0.29, calibration slope 1.04 |
case study, code."

Do not list a project you cannot discuss for 10 minutes. Do not list a certificate as a project.

### Step 8. Write the skills section honestly

**Input:** the tools and methods you have actually used.
**Output:** a grouped list of 12 to 20 items.
**Rationale:** skills sections are read by both humans and parsers, and they are the easiest
place to over-claim. An interviewer who asks about a listed skill and gets a shallow answer
discounts every other claim.

Group by function, not by proficiency:

```text
Languages: Python, SQL, SAS, R
Data: OMOP CDM, FHIR, claims (ICD-10, CPT), Epic Clarity
Methods: temporal validation, calibration, propensity score matching, survival analysis
Tools: Git, Docker, Azure, Tableau
```

Omit proficiency bars, percentages, and star ratings. They are not comparable across candidates
and are widely read as filler.

### Step 9. Write the summary last, place it first

**Input:** the finished bullets.
**Output:** two to three lines.
**Rationale:** the summary is a conclusion, so it can only be written after the evidence exists.
It should let a recruiter confirm the match and a manager see the thesis.

Template:

```text
[Role noun] with [N years] in [domain], focused on [decision type].
[One proof point with a number]. [One line on what you are targeting].
```

Example: "Biomedical data scientist with six years in oncology trial analytics, focused on
validation and reproducible pipelines. Cut analysis dataset delivery time by 30 percent across 23
studies and built a readmission model validated on 12 months of held-out claims. Seeking a
clinical data science role where model evidence has to survive clinical review."

Avoid "passionate," "results-driven," "detail-oriented," and "seeking to leverage." They carry no
information and consume the line the reader actually reads.

### Step 10. Build the LinkedIn profile from the same claims

**Input:** the finished resume.
**Output:** headline, about, experience, skills, and settings.
**Rationale:** the profile is found by search and read in two lines. It must contain the target
title and the domain terms in the indexed fields.

Headline patterns, in order of preference:

1. "Clinical Data Scientist | Temporal validation and calibration for risk models | EHR + claims"
2. "Biostatistician moving into real-world evidence | Trial SAPs, causal inference, RWD"
3. "Data Analyst | SQL, Python, healthcare claims | Open to clinical analytics roles"

The headline should contain the target role noun, two or three searchable skills, and the domain.
It should not contain "Seeking opportunities" alone, which wastes the most indexed line.

About section: four short paragraphs, 150 to 250 words. First two lines must state the role and
the strongest proof point, because the rest is behind "see more." Then a paragraph on the
evidence, a paragraph on the domain, and a closing line on what you are looking for.

Experience entries: the same bullets as the resume, trimmed to three per role, with the employer's
public description replaced by what you did. Add skills to each role so the search index
associates them with the position.

Skills: up to 50, but order the top 10 as the ones you want to be found for. Endorsements are
weak evidence; do not spend time on them.

Settings: set location to the metro you will work in, not your current suburb; set "open to
work" visibility deliberately; turn on "profile in search" if you want inbound.

### Step 11. Run the keyword audit

**Input:** the resume text and three postings.
**Output:** a coverage table and a decision per missing term.
**Rationale:** the audit catches genuine omissions without turning the document into a keyword
list. It takes 20 minutes and is the highest-yield check in this procedure.

For each term on the must-match list, record present, partial, or absent. For each absent term,
decide: add (you have the experience), learn (you do not, and it appears in most postings), or
ignore (it is employer-specific or a nice-to-have). Never add a term you cannot defend.

Section 7 implements the audit with a small script.

### Step 12. Test with two humans and iterate

**Input:** the formatted document.
**Output:** a list of stopping points and fixes.
**Rationale:** you cannot see your own document. Two readers, one with domain knowledge and one
without, surface different failures.

Test protocol:

1. Give the reader the resume and a posting. Ask them to decide in 30 seconds whether to
   interview, and to say why.
2. Ask them to name the target role and the strongest evidence.
3. Ask them to find one number and explain what it means.
4. Record where they stopped and what they misread.
5. Fix the first three issues. Do not add more content; replace.

### Step 13. Maintain the document between searches

**Input:** the live resume and profile.
**Output:** a versioned master file and a target-specific variant per application.
**Rationale:** rebuilding from scratch before each search wastes the work and loses evidence you
have already verified. A master file plus a 20-minute tailoring pass is faster and produces more
consistent claims.

Keep two files: `master.docx`, which contains every verified bullet including ones not currently
used, and `resume_target.pdf`, which contains the selected bullets for one application. Date the
file names and keep the last three versions. When a role ends, add its bullets to the master
within a month, while the numbers are still recoverable. When a search begins, the master is the
inventory and the tailoring is a selection exercise.

### Branch points and edge cases

**Career change from academia.** Lead with a summary that names the target role and the
transferable evidence, then a projects section, then experience. Put the degree at the bottom
unless the employer requires it at the top. Translate papers into decisions: what changed because
the result was known?

**Clinical background.** Put the clinical role first if the target is clinical informatics,
because the licence and workflow credibility are the differentiator. Quantify the operational
work: audits, protocols, staffing, throughput, quality metrics. Do not bury it under a list of
tools.

**PhD, non-academic target.** A four-page academic CV is the wrong document. Use two pages:
summary, experience (including postdoc as a job), projects, skills, education. Publications:
three selected, with DOIs, under "Selected publications." Do not include the full list unless the
role is research-heavy.

**Visa constraints.** State work authorisation only if it helps: "Authorised to work in the US
without sponsorship" is a strong line; "Requires H-1B sponsorship" is a filter but an honest one,
and it belongs in the application form rather than the summary where possible. Never say
"authorised" if you are not.

**Return after a gap.** Use dates, not explanations, on the resume. If the gap is relevant,
address it in one line in the summary or in the cover note: "Returning to analytics after a
two-year caregiving period; completed a validated readmission model and a SQL refresher in 2026."
Then let the artifact speak.

**Non-traditional degree.** Put the degree where it belongs chronologically and add a line for
relevant coursework or a credential only if it is real. Compensate with projects and referrals;
do not invent a degree equivalence.

**Over-levelled target.** If your evidence supports mid-level and you apply to senior, the resume
will be read as thin rather than ambitious. Apply to the level the evidence supports, and let the
interview surface the stretch. Section 6 of Topic 01 covers the offer case.

**Remote roles.** Add the metro you are based in and your willingness to travel if the posting
requires it. Do not describe yourself as "remote" as if it were a location; recruiters filter by
geography.

### Decision table: how to present a gap

| Gap length | Cause | Resume treatment | Interview treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 3 months | Between roles | No treatment; dates speak | Mention only if asked |
| 3 to 12 months | Caregiving, health, study | Dates; one line in summary if it aids the narrative | Brief factual answer, then redirect to recent evidence |
| 12 to 24 months | Caregiving, relocation, burnout | Dates; one line; add a recent artifact or course | Explain the period in one sentence and the return in two |
| Over 24 months | Any | Dates; summary line naming the return; strong recent project | Address directly, no apology, evidence of current capability |
| Any, with a confidentiality constraint | Termination, legal | Dates only | Say the separation is subject to an agreement and move on |

## 6. Worked example

This example follows one candidate through a rewrite and a six-week application cycle. The
numbers are from his application log, which is a single-person record with obvious confounders
and is presented as an anecdote, not as evidence about the market.

### 6.1 The candidate

Tomás is 31, with a BSc in statistics and six years at a contract research organisation (CRO) as
a statistical programmer. He has programmed 23 Phase I–III oncology studies in SAS, built CDISC
SDTM and ADaM datasets, and supported four sponsor audits with no findings. He has used SQL for
extraction and Python for automation, but not for modelling. He wants a clinical data scientist
role at a payer or health-tech company in Philadelphia, where he lives.

He has applied to 42 roles over ten weeks using his existing resume. He has had two recruiter
screens and no hiring-manager interviews.

### 6.2 Diagnostic 1: the document is at rung 2

His ten bullets:

| # | Bullet | Rung | Problem |
|---:|---|---:|---|
| 1 | "Responsible for statistical programming for clinical trials" | 1 | No method, no scope, no outcome |
| 2 | "Created SDTM and ADaM datasets" | 2 | Output but no scale or quality result |
| 3 | "Worked with cross-functional teams" | 1 | Presence, not contribution |
| 4 | "Performed quality control on datasets" | 2 | No error rate or consequence |
| 5 | "Used SAS and SQL daily" | 1 | Tool list as a bullet |
| 6 | "Supported audits" | 2 | No result |
| 7 | "Automated reporting processes" | 2 | No time saved |

referral changed the conversation.

### 6.7 What the interview exposed

In the first hiring-manager interview, the interviewer asked about bullet 6: "You say AUPRC went
from 0.21 to 0.29. What was the prevalence, and how did you choose the operating threshold?"

Tomás had the answers because the portfolio case study contained them: prevalence 11.4 percent,
threshold chosen at the 90th percentile to match the care management team's capacity of 60
outreach slots per month. The resume claim was defensible because the artifact existed. Without
the artifact, the bullet would have been a liability.

The second interview probed the audit claim. He could describe the four audits, the two that
sampled his datasets, and the one finding that was resolved before the close-out. The specificity
was the point.

### 6.8 Vary one assumption

Change one input: Tomás requires H-1B sponsorship.

The evidence does not change. The feasible employer set does. Three adjustments:

1. **Target list.** Payers and large health-tech companies with a documented sponsorship history
   move to the top; startups and small vendors move out. The number of feasible employers in
   Philadelphia falls from roughly 30 to 12, by his own count of postings that mention
   sponsorship or have public petition records.
2. **Summary line.** He adds "Authorised to work in the US; will require sponsorship for a change
   of employer" to the application form, not the resume. On the resume, he omits it, because it
   is a screening filter that belongs in the system of record rather than the first screen.
3. **Timeline.** He plans for 24 weeks rather than 16, because the feasible set is smaller and
   each process is slower. The extra time goes to referrals at the 12 target employers, which is
   the channel most likely to survive a sponsorship screen.

The general lesson: a constraint changes the target set and the timeline, not the evidence. Write
the same claims and change where you send them.

### 6.9 What transferred and what did not

Three things transferred directly. The CDISC vocabulary was already a requirement in two of the
three postings, so the rewrite did not need to manufacture it. The audit evidence was real and
specific, so it survived probing. The programming discipline, which is what a CRO actually
teaches, read as reliability to a payer hiring manager who had been burned by analysts who could
not reproduce their own numbers.

Two things did not transfer. Clinical inference was absent: Tomás could describe a hazard ratio
but had never chosen an estimand or defended a confounding strategy. Dashboarding was absent: he
had automated reports but never built a governed product with users.

He did not try to close both before applying. He closed the claims gap with the portfolio project
and left the dashboard gap visible in the interview, where he described the reporting automation
he had built and what he would need to learn. That is a defensible answer because it is bounded.
Claiming dashboard experience he did not have would not have been.

## 7. Code, query, or calculation

The script below performs the keyword audit and the bullet-quality check from Section 5. It uses
only the standard library, embeds both the before and after bullet sets, and prints a report you
can compare against your own document.

Assumptions:

- Requirement status is assigned by the candidate, not inferred from text. The script does not
  attempt natural-language understanding; it scores the table you give it.
- Presence is checked by keyword matching in the bullet text, which is deliberately simple. A
  bullet can mention SQL without evidencing it, which is why the human review in Step 12 still
  matters.
- The 0.60 threshold is an editorial heuristic for a first pass. It is not derived from hiring
  data.

```python
"""Resume audit: keyword coverage and bullet quality, standard library only.

Run: python resume_audit.py
Inputs are inline so the output is reproducible. Replace RESUME_TEXT and
REQUIREMENTS with your own text to audit a real resume.
"""

import re

# (requirement, postings_mentioning, status)
# status is one of "strong", "partial", "weak", "absent".
REQUIREMENTS = [
    ("SQL", 3, "partial"),
    ("Python", 3, "partial"),
    ("Claims data", 2, "absent"),
    ("Causal inference", 2, "absent"),
    ("Dashboarding", 2, "absent"),
    ("Clinical data standards", 3, "strong"),
    ("Stakeholder communication", 3, "weak"),
    ("Study design", 2, "absent"),
]

STATUS_SCORE = {"strong": 1.0, "partial": 0.5, "weak": 0.25, "absent": 0.0}

WEAK_OPENERS = (
    "responsible for",
    "assisted",
    "helped",
    "worked on",
    "worked with",
    "participated",
    "supported",
    "involved in",
    "duties included",
    "used",
    "ensured",
)

BEFORE = [
    "Responsible for statistical programming for clinical trials",
    "Created SDTM and ADaM datasets",
    "Worked with cross-functional teams",
    "Performed quality control on datasets",
    "Used SAS and SQL daily",
    "Supported audits",
    "Automated reporting processes",
    "Trained new team members",
    "Ensured compliance with CDISC standards",
    "Reduced programming time by 20 percent",
]

AFTER = [
    "Programmed 23 Phase I-III oncology studies in SAS, delivering 100 percent of analysis "
    "datasets on or before the data cut across four sponsor audits with zero quality findings",
    "Built SDTM and ADaM datasets for a 640-patient Phase II study, reducing the median "
    "programming cycle from 12 to 8 days by templating the derivation code",
    "Automated a monthly safety reporting process in Python, cutting preparation from 3 days "
    "to 4 hours and eliminating two manual transcription steps",
    "Designed 42 edit checks for a 14-site registry; query rate at database lock fell from "
    "7.9 percent to 1.8 percent between pilot and primary study",
    "Trained four new programmers on CDISC conventions; all four passed their first internal "
    "quality review without rework",
    "Built a claims-based readmission cohort of 48,000 adults as a portfolio project, "
    "validating the model on the most recent 12 months with AUPRC improving from 0.21 to 0.29",
]


def coverage(reqs):
    weights = [p for _, p, _ in reqs]
    scores = [STATUS_SCORE[s] * p for _, p, s in reqs]
    return sum(scores) / sum(weights)


def weak_opener(bullet):
    low = bullet.lower().strip()
    for opener in WEAK_OPENERS:
        if low.startswith(opener):
            return opener
    return None


def has_number(bullet):
    return bool(re.search(r"\d", bullet))


def audit(bullets, label):
    weak = [(b, weak_opener(b)) for b in bullets if weak_opener(b)]
    numbered = [b for b in bullets if has_number(b)]
    mean_words = sum(len(b.split()) for b in bullets) / len(bullets)
    print(f"{label}: {len(bullets)} bullets")
    print(f"  weak openers: {len(weak)}")
    for bullet, opener in weak:
        print(f"    - starts with '{opener}': {bullet[:60]}")
    print(f"  bullets containing a number: {len(numbered)}/{len(bullets)}")
    print(f"  mean words per bullet: {mean_words:.1f}")


def main():
    print("KEYWORD COVERAGE")
    print(f"  {'requirement':26s} {'postings':>8s} {'status':>8s} {'score':>6s}")
    for label, count, status in REQUIREMENTS:
        print(f"  {label:26s} {count:8d} {status:>8s} {STATUS_SCORE[status]:6.2f}")
    cov = coverage(REQUIREMENTS)
    print(f"  weighted coverage: {cov:.3f}")
    print(f"  verdict at 0.60: {'pass' if cov >= 0.60 else 'rewrite the dialect'}")

    print()
    audit(BEFORE, "BEFORE")
    print()
    audit(AFTER, "AFTER")

    print()
    print("NOTE")
    print(f"  coverage above scores the BEFORE resume: {cov:.3f}")
    print("  the AFTER resume adds claims and validation terms, which this")
    print("  table does not re-score; re-run with new statuses to compare.")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

Reading the output:

1. **Coverage of 0.338 is the diagnostic, not the verdict.** The script says "rewrite the
   dialect" because the threshold is 0.60. The fix is not to add the word "claims" to a bullet
   you cannot defend; it is to build the evidence that makes the word true, which is what the
   portfolio project did.
2. **Five weak openers in ten bullets is the pattern a recruiter sees.** The words "responsible
   for," "worked with," and "used" tell the reader that a duty exists, not that a result does.
   Removing them is mechanical and immediately improves the first four words of each line.
3. **One bullet in ten contains a number before the rewrite, five of six after.** The target is
   not every bullet; it is the three to five bullets that carry the strongest claims.
4. **Mean length rose from 4.6 to 24.3 words.** Longer is acceptable when the words are
   evidence. A 30-word bullet with a metric beats a 6-word duty. Beyond about 30 words the
   reader loses the thread, so the rewrite sits near the upper bound deliberately.
5. **The script cannot tell whether a claim is true.** It flags form, not substance. The human
   test in Step 12 is what catches a number you cannot explain.

## 8. Data, standards, and regulatory context

There is no standard for resume content, and no regulator approves a CV. What is regulated is the
truth of the claims and the handling of the document as personal data. Both matter more in
biomedicine than in most fields because employers verify credentials and because a misstatement
about clinical or regulatory work can end a candidacy after the offer.

### 8.1 What is verified, and by whom

| Claim | Verification route | Consequence of a mismatch |
|---|---|---|
| Employment dates and titles | Background check, payroll records, references | Offer withdrawn or termination |
| Degree and institution | Degree verification service or registrar | Offer withdrawn |
| Licences (RN, MD, pharmacy) | State or national licence lookup | Candidacy ends; some roles require a licence by law |
| Certifications (RHIA, PMP, cloud) | Issuer verification | Credibility loss, possible termination |
| Publications and DOIs | Bibliographic databases | Credibility loss |
| Work authorisation | Right-to-work check (I-9 in the US, share code in the UK) | Offer withdrawn, legal exposure |
| Criminal record | Background check under applicable law | Role-dependent |
| Clearance | Government verification | Role unavailable |

Two implications follow. First, never round a date range to hide a gap; the check will find the
real dates, and the discrepancy is worse than the gap. Second, never list a certification you
have not completed, including one you are "about to" complete. List it as "in progress" with an
expected date, or leave it off.

### 8.2 Personal data rules

In the European Union and the United Kingdom, a CV is personal data under the General Data
Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the UK GDPR. Employers must have a lawful basis to process it,
must not keep it indefinitely without a retention basis, and must handle special-category data
(such as health information) with additional care. A candidate who volunteers a disability or a
health condition in a CV is disclosing special-category data; that is sometimes necessary for
accommodation, but it should be a deliberate choice made through the employer's accommodation
process rather than an incidental line in a document.

In the United States, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule does not generally apply to a CV, because a job
applicant is not a covered entity disclosing protected health information. It does apply to what you say about your work:
describing a patient case with identifiers is a disclosure problem for your employer, not for
you as an applicant, but it will be treated as a governance failure by any employer who reads it.

### 8.3 What the ATS actually does

An applicant tracking system parses a document into fields: name, contact, employers, titles,
dates, education, skills. Parsing failures are common with multi-column layouts, tables, text
boxes, graphics, and non-standard headings. There is no universal parsing standard, and different
systems behave differently, which is why the copy-to-text test in Step 6 is the practical check.

Two claims about ATS are worth correcting because they circulate widely. First, most systems do
not auto-reject on a keyword score; they rank and filter, and a human decides. Second, keyword
stuffing in white text or metadata is detectable and is treated as dishonesty. The realistic
model is a ranked list that a recruiter scans, with the top of the list determined by title
match, recency, location, and a small number of required terms.

### 8.4 What the standards do not cover

- They do not require a one-page format. Length is a convention that varies by country and
  seniority.
- They do not require a photo, date of birth, marital status, or nationality, and in many
  jurisdictions including those data invites discrimination claims. Omit them unless a specific
  country's convention requires them.
- They do not validate the quality of a metric. A number can be truthful and still misleading if
  the denominator is chosen to flatter.
- They do not protect you from a reference who describes you differently from your resume. Brief
  your referees; the mismatch is a common cause of late-stage rejection.

### 8.5 Country conventions

| Convention | United States | United Kingdom | Germany | Canada |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Document | Resume, 1 page early career, 2 later | CV, 2 pages typical | Tabular CV, often with photo | Resume or CV, 1 to 2 pages |
| Photo | Omit | Omit | Often included by convention | Omit |
| Date of birth | Omit | Omit | Sometimes included | Omit |
| Address | City and state | City | Full address sometimes expected | City and province |
| Publications | Selected list | Selected list | Full list common in research | Selected list |
| References | On request | On request | Sometimes attached | On request |

The table is convention, not law, and conventions vary by sector. In Germany, a photo and date
of birth are still common in some industries despite discrimination law; in the United States,
including them invites legal risk. When applying across borders, follow the target country's
convention for format and the target employer's convention for content.

## 9. Industry practice and case evidence

### 9.1 Documented: screening is fast and pattern-based

The Ladders eye-tracking study (2018) reported an average initial screen of 7.4 seconds, with
recruiters fixating most on job titles and company names, and recommended simple layouts, clear
section headings, bolded titles, and bulleted accomplishments. The study is a vendor publication
with a small sample and should be treated as directional. Its design conclusion is nonetheless
consistent with how screening works: the first pass is pattern recognition, not reading.

The same study reported that cluttered, multi-column layouts and keyword stuffing performed
poorly, which is consistent with the parsing problem described in Section 8.3.

### 9.2 Documented: degree requirements are uneven and often unnecessary

The Harvard Business School report *Dismissed by Degrees* (Fuller and Raman, 2017) analysed 26
million online job postings from 2015 and surveyed 600 business and HR leaders. It documented
degree inflation: employers requiring a four-year degree for roles whose incumbents often did not
hold one. Among its findings, 61 percent of employers surveyed acknowledged that applicant
tracking systems screen out qualified candidates without degrees.

That figure is a survey self-report from 2016–17, not a measurement of current systems, and it
covers middle-skill roles rather than data science. It is still the best public evidence that
degree screens are imperfect and that non-traditional candidates are filtered for reasons
unrelated to capability. The practical response is referrals and direct evidence, not a longer
education section.

### 9.3 Documented: skills-first search is growing

LinkedIn's Economic Graph report *Skills-Based Hiring: Increasing Access to Opportunity* (March
2025) estimates that hiring on skills rather than prior job title expands the median talent pool
by 6.1 times globally, and by 15.9 times in the United States. In artificial intelligence roles
the global figure is 8.2 times. LinkedIn also reports, citing OECD work, that 14 percent of
recruiter searches in OECD countries filter by skills versus under 2 percent by degree.

These are platform-derived estimates about the pool of candidates who could be considered, not
about who is hired. They support one inference for a candidate: the skills and headline fields
are searched, so they should contain the terms the role uses. They do not support the claim that
a degree no longer matters, because the same report shows that hiring outcomes lag the search
behaviour.

### 9.4 Inference, labelled

- **Inference:** for biomedical data roles, the recruiter screen is dominated by title match,
  domain terms, location, and work authorisation, in that order. This is inferred from the
  structure of postings and from recruiter practice, not from a controlled study.
- **Inference:** a two-page resume is acceptable in the United States for candidates with more
  than about eight years of experience, and a one-page resume is expected below that. This is a
  convention, and it varies by employer and by country.
- **Inference:** the projects section is the highest-leverage addition for career changers,
  because it is the only section that can show a decision without an employer's confidentiality.
- **Inference:** LinkedIn's search index rewards a headline that names the target role. A
  headline that names only the current employer's internal title is invisible to the searches
  that matter.

> **Note:** None of the figures in this section is a placement rate or a callback rate. The
> Ladders study measures eye movements in a small sample; the HBS report measures posted
> requirements and employer self-reports; the LinkedIn report measures platform search behaviour.
> Each is useful for a design decision and none is a forecast of your outcome.

## 10. Failure modes and red flags

| Failure | Early warning sign | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Duty list instead of evidence | Most bullets start with "responsible for" | Describes presence, not contribution | Reader cannot infer capability | Rewrite with action, method, metric, consequence |
| No numbers | Zero digits in the experience section | Scale and effect are invisible | Reader assumes junior scope | Add a denominator and a period to three bullets |
| Unverifiable metric | You cannot explain the baseline | The number was estimated or inherited | Interview collapse or post-offer problem | Reconstruct the source or soften the claim |
| Wrong dialect | Strong work, no screens | Vocabulary does not match the target function | Filtered before a human reads | Keyword audit against three postings |
| Title inflation | Your title differs from the offer letter's records | Attempt to match a target level | Background check mismatch | Normalise with the official title in parentheses |
| Over-length | Three pages for eight years | Everything kept, nothing edited | Reader skims and remembers nothing | Cut to the page budget in Step 5 |
| Parsing failure | Copy-paste scrambles the text | Tables, columns, text boxes | Sections disappear in the ATS | One column, standard headings, text test |
| Hidden keywords | White text or metadata terms | Attempt to game ranking | Detected as dishonesty | Keywords only in visible, truthful context |
| Publication dump | Two pages of citations for a non-research role | Academic habit | Signals wrong target | Three selected publications with DOIs |
| Skills over-claim | A tool listed that you used once | Breadth signalling | Interview question exposes depth | List only tools you can discuss for five minutes |
| Gap concealed | Dates rounded to hide months | Fear of stigma | Verification finds the discrepancy | Use real dates; address the gap in one line |
| Generic profile | Headline is the current job title | Profile not maintained | Invisible in recruiter search | Rewrite headline with target role and skills |
| No referral path | 100 percent inbound applications | Only one channel used | Low conversion regardless of quality | Allocate half the search time to conversations |
| Confidential detail | Client name or patient detail in a bullet | Enthusiasm to show scale | Governance breach | Describe the class, not the client |

Two red flags deserve separate treatment because they end candidacies rather than delaying them.
A false claim about a licence or a degree is disqualifying in every health employer, and it is
checked. A resume that contains patient identifiers is a reportable incident at some employers,
even if the candidate is not hired.

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why it is needed | What breaks it | How to test it |
|---|---|---|---|
| A resume is screened before it is read | It justifies the top-third design | Some small employers read everything | Ask contacts how their process works |
| Three postings represent a family's vocabulary | It bounds the keyword audit | Niche roles with distinct dialects | Check whether a fourth posting adds new must-match terms |
| Metrics improve credibility | They supply scale and effect | Metrics that cannot be explained | Rehearse the denominator for every number |
| One page is right under eight years | It forces editing | Countries and employers where two pages are normal | Follow the convention of the target country |
| ATS parsing is the main technical risk | It drives the format rules | Human-only processes at small employers | Run the copy-to-text test anyway; it is cheap |
| Referrals dominate inbound | It justifies the channel split | Roles with structured pipelines and large intakes | Track your own channel conversion |
| Title normalisation is acceptable | Internal titles are unreadable | Employers that treat any change as misrepresentation | Keep the official title in parentheses |
| LinkedIn search indexes the headline and skills | It justifies the rewrite | Recruiters who search only by title | Check whether you appear in a search for the target title |
| The document can be maintained cheaply | It justifies the master file | Fast-changing tools and roles | Review quarterly; update the master after each role |

The main limit is that the document cannot create evidence. It can make existing evidence legible
and it can stop a reader from drawing a wrong inference, but a thin history produces a thin
resume no matter how it is written. When the document is strong and the response rate is low, the
problem is usually the target list or the channel, not the wording.

## 12. Exercises

**Exercise 1. Rung audit (critique, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: every bullet in your current resume labelled with its rung from 1 to 6 and a
one-line reason.
Acceptance criteria: at least half the bullets are labelled 1 to 3; the three bullets to rewrite
first are identified; no bullet is labelled 5 or 6 without a named decision.

**Exercise 2. Bullet rewrite (application, 60 minutes).**
Deliverable: six bullets rewritten in the five-part form, each with a metric, a period, and a
consequence.
Acceptance criteria: no bullet begins with a weak opener; each metric has a denominator; a reader
can state what changed after reading each bullet.

**Exercise 3. Summary (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a two- to three-line summary for one target family.
Acceptance criteria: it names the role noun, the domain, one proof point with a number, and the
target; it contains no "passionate," "results-driven," or "leverage."

**Exercise 4. Title and date normalisation (application, 20 minutes).**
Deliverable: a table of every role with official title, market title, dates, and any explanation
required.
Acceptance criteria: no title overstates the role; every gap longer than three months is either
explained or accepted; dates are month and year.

**Exercise 5. Parsing test (application, 20 minutes).**
Deliverable: the plain-text extraction of your PDF, with a note on any lost or reordered content.
Acceptance criteria: contact details, headings, employers, titles, and dates survive in order;
the file passes a manual ATS-style field extraction.

**Exercise 6. LinkedIn rebuild (application, 60 minutes).**
Deliverable: headline, about section, three experience entries, and top 10 skills.
Acceptance criteria: the headline contains the target role and two searchable skills; the first
two lines of the about section state the role and a proof point; skills match the resume.

**Exercise 7. Keyword audit (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a coverage table for three postings with a decision (add, learn, ignore) for every
missing term.
Acceptance criteria: every "add" is defensible in an interview; at least one term is marked
"ignore" with a reason; coverage is computed, not estimated.

**Exercise 8. Two-reader test (critique, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: notes from two readers, one domain expert and one not, with the 30-second decision
each made.
Acceptance criteria: both readers can name the target role; at least one misreading is recorded
and fixed; the fixes are replacements, not additions.

## 13. Self-check answers

**1. What distinguishes a responsibility statement from an achievement claim?**
A responsibility statement describes a duty that existed whether or not you performed it well. An
achievement claim names what changed, by how much, over what scope, and how it was measured. The
test is whether the sentence would still be true if a different person had held the role.

**2. Write a bullet for "reduced query rate" in the five-part form.**
"Designed 42 edit checks for a 14-site registry, which reduced the query rate at database lock
from 7.9 percent to 1.8 percent between the pilot and primary study." The action is designed, the
method is edit checks, the metric has a denominator (queries per data point at lock), the scope is
14 sites, and the consequence is a lower rework burden before lock.

**3. What must a summary contain?**
The target role noun, the domain, one proof point with a number, and the target. It should not
contain adjectives about personality. A recruiter uses it to confirm the match; a manager uses it
to decide whether to keep reading.

**4. When may you change a title?**
When the official title is uninterpretable outside the employer, and the change is a translation
rather than an upgrade. Keep the official title in parentheses. Never change a level word
(associate to senior, analyst to scientist) unless the employer's own records use that word.

**5. What is the parsing test?**
Copy the PDF text into a plain editor and inspect the order and completeness. If a section is
missing or the contact block is broken, an ATS may see the same failure. It takes two minutes and
catches the most common formatting defect.

**6. Why use the same claims on the resume and LinkedIn?**
Because a recruiter may read either first and an interviewer may compare them. Inconsistent
claims create a credibility problem that has nothing to do with the work. The packaging differs
because the readers differ; the facts do not.

**7. Coverage is 0.34. What do you add?**
Nothing yet. Decide for each missing term whether you have the experience. If you do, rewrite a
bullet to show it. If you do not, either build the evidence (a portfolio artifact) or mark it
"learn" and accept the gap. Adding the word without the evidence produces an interview you cannot
pass.

**8. How do you handle a 20-month gap?**
Use real dates. Add one line in the summary naming the reason if it aids the narrative and the
return if it is recent. Prepare a one-sentence answer and two sentences on current evidence. Do
not apologise, and do not invent consulting work.

**Rubric for Exercise 2.** Full marks: six bullets, all with a strong opener; each has a metric
with a denominator; scope and period are stated; no bullet exceeds about 30 words; the claims are
ones the candidate can defend.

**Rubric for Exercise 8.** Full marks: two readers, one domain and one not; the 30-second decision
recorded for each; at least one misreading identified; fixes are replacements rather than
additions; the revised version is retested.

## 14. Interview questions

**1. "Walk me through this bullet. What was the baseline?"**
Strong: names the baseline, the measurement period, and who else was involved. Weak: repeats the
bullet or says the number came from a report they did not build.

**2. "You list causal inference. What estimand did you target?"**
Strong: names the estimand, the confounding strategy, and one sensitivity analysis. Weak:
describes a regression and calls it causal.

**3. "Why did you move from a CRO to a payer?"**
Strong: connects the decision type (population health, coverage) to the evidence they want to
build. Weak: says they wanted a new challenge.

**4. "Your resume says you trained four programmers. What did you teach?"**
Strong: names the curriculum, the assessment, and the outcome. Weak: says they answered
questions.

**5. "What is not on your resume that should be?"**
Strong: names a relevant piece of evidence and explains why it was cut. Weak: says nothing was
cut.

**6. "How do you decide what to leave off?"**
Strong: describes the page budget, the target family, and the cut order. Weak: says they include
everything to be safe.

**7. "You have a gap. What happened?"**
Strong: one factual sentence, then evidence of current capability. Weak: a long justification or
an evasion.

**8. "Why is your title different from your offer letter?"**
Strong: explains the internal title and the market translation, with the official title
preserved. Weak: becomes defensive or admits inflating.

**9. "How do you tailor your resume without overfitting to one posting?"**
Strong: three postings, recurring terms, a stable summary, and bullets selected per application.
Weak: changes the whole document for every application or changes nothing.

**10. "What would you change about your resume now?"**
Strong: names a specific weakness and a specific fix. Weak: says it is complete.

## 15. Key terms

- **Achievement bullet** — a statement of action, method, metric, and consequence.
- **Applicant tracking system (ATS)** — software that receives and parses applications.
- **Background check** — verification of employment, education, and record.
- **Boolean search** — recruiter query using AND, OR, NOT, and phrases.
- **Coverage** — the weighted share of posting requirements evidenced on the resume.
- **Curriculum vitae (CV)** — an exhaustive academic record.
- **Denominator** — the base against which a percentage is computed.
- **Employment gap** — a period without employment.
- **Endorsement** — a connection's confirmation of a skill.
- **Functional resume** — a layout organised by skill rather than chronology.
- **Headline** — the line under a LinkedIn name.
- **Keyword** — a term a recruiter or ATS searches for.
- **Keyword stuffing** — repeating terms to game ranking.
- **Open to work** — a LinkedIn availability setting.
- **ORCID** — a persistent researcher identifier.
- **Parsing** — extraction of structured fields from a document.
- **Referral** — a candidate introduced by an employee.
- **Requisition** — an approved, funded opening.
- **Responsibility statement** — a description of duties.
- **Reverse chronological** — ordering roles from most to least recent.
- **Right to work** — legal authorisation to work in a jurisdiction.
- **Scope** — the size of what you influenced.
- **Shortlist** — candidates forwarded to the hiring manager.
- **Skills section** — a grouped list of competencies.
- **Sourcing** — proactive identification of candidates.
- **Summary** — two to three lines naming the target role and core evidence.
- **Title normalisation** — restating an internal title in market language.
- **Work authorisation** — the legal right to work, with or without sponsorship.
