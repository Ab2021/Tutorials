## 1. Why this topic exists

An offer is a package, and the package is the largest financial decision most people make in a
decade. Base salary compounds through every future raise, bonus, and promotion. Equity can be worth
nothing or several years of salary depending on terms most candidates never read. A start date, a
title, and a remote arrangement change daily life for years.

The negotiation is not a confrontation. It is a structured conversation under incomplete
information, and both sides know it. The employer has a band, a level, and an internal equity
constraint. You have a market estimate, a current package, and a decision about what you need. The
conversation succeeds when both sides can explain the outcome to their own stakeholders.

### 1.1 What candidates get wrong

Three failure patterns account for most of the money left on the table.

**Anchoring on base.** A candidate compares two offers by salary and ignores a 15 percent bonus
difference, a four-year vesting schedule, a retirement match, and a signing bonus. The comparison is
wrong by tens of thousands of dollars.

**Accepting the first number.** Most employers expect a counter and build room into the first offer.
Accepting immediately is not polite; it is a missed conversation that the employer planned for.

**Bluffing.** A candidate claims a competing offer that does not exist, or inflates one. Recruiters
ask for details, compare timelines, and sometimes request documentation. A discovered bluff ends
the negotiation and sometimes the offer.

### 1.2 Who is blocked

**International candidates** face a constrained negotiation. The prevailing wage determination for a
visa sets a floor and can limit how the package is structured; a large signing bonus may be easier
than a base increase. Knowing which levers are available requires asking the employer's immigration
counsel, not guessing.

**Early-career candidates** often believe negotiation is inappropriate. The evidence does not
support that belief: employers set bands expecting a conversation, and a polite, specific ask is
normal. What is inappropriate is an unsubstantiated demand.

**Candidates with a gap or a return** sometimes accept below band out of relief. The cost is a
compensation baseline that follows them for years, because future raises are usually percentage
increases on the current base.

**Candidates moving from academia** are unfamiliar with equity and bonus structures and often
undervalue them, or overvalue a title. Both errors are correctable with a one-hour conversation and
a spreadsheet.

### 1.3 What this topic produces

A method for building a market range from named sources, a total-rewards comparison across offers,
a negotiation script with fallback levers, and a worked example with the arithmetic. It also covers
the questions to ask before accepting that are not about money: decision rights, success criteria,
on-call, remote policy, and learning.

### 1.4 The negotiation is not the only conversation

The number matters, and the scope matters more over a career. A role that pays 5 percent more and
teaches you nothing new is usually a worse decision than one that pays 5 percent less and puts you in
a position to own the work you want in three years.

The practical test: write down what you will be able to do in two years that you cannot do now. If
the answer is "the same work with a different logo," the compensation premium has to be large to
justify the move.

### 1.5 What this topic does not cover

It does not provide legal or tax advice. Equity taxation, visa implications, and non-compete
enforceability vary by jurisdiction and by person; consult a professional for a decision that
matters. It does not cover salary benchmarking methodology in depth; it shows how to read the
sources and where they fail. It does not teach persuasion tactics that depend on deception, because
they are both unethical and ineffective in a market where recruiters talk to each other.

## 3. The core mental model

A negotiation has three quantities and five levers. Get the quantities right and the levers become
obvious.

```text
  QUANTITIES

  Walk-away        the worst package you would accept rather than stay or leave
  Reservation      the minimum you will accept, usually above walk-away
  Target           the package you ask for, justified by evidence

       walk-away <= reservation <= target <= band maximum
```

```text
  LEVERS

  Base salary        compounds; constrained by band and internal equity
  Target bonus       percentage; often fixed by level
  Equity             grant size, vesting, refresh, strike price
  Signing bonus      one-time; easiest lever; may have a clawback
  Level and scope    changes the band, the title, and the work
  Non-cash           remote days, start date, learning budget, title, review date
```

The negotiation zone is the overlap between your reservation and the employer's band maximum. If
the zone is empty, no script saves the conversation; you either change the level or decline.

### 3.1 Total rewards, not salary

```text
  annual cash   = base x (1 + target bonus rate) + base x retirement match rate
  annual equity = grant value / vesting years
  year 1        = annual cash + annual equity + signing bonus
  four years    = 4 x annual cash + 4 x annual equity + signing bonus
```

The formula is simple and most candidates do not run it. A $10,000 base difference is $40,000 over
four years before compounding; a $60,000 equity grant over four years is $15,000 a year; a 6 percent
retirement match on $150,000 is $9,000 a year.

Two adjustments matter. Equity must be valued by scenario, not at grant price. And geography must be
adjusted, because a Boston band and a Raleigh band for the same level differ, sometimes by 10 to 20
percent.

### 3.2 BATNA determines your power

Your best alternative to a negotiated agreement (BATNA) is what you do if this offer fails. It is
usually your current job, another offer, or continued searching. A strong BATNA lets you ask without
fear; a weak one tempts you to bluff.

The rule: never claim an alternative you do not have. You may say that you are in other processes
and expect a decision by a date, if that is true. You may not invent a number or an employer.

### 3.3 The ask is a package, not a demand

A good ask has four parts:

1. **Enthusiasm.** You want the role, and you say so first.
2. **The specific adjustment.** One number or one term, not a list.
3. **The reason.** Market evidence, scope, or a competing constraint, stated briefly.
4. **The flexibility.** A second lever you would accept if the first is not available.

Example: "I am excited about the role and the clinical evaluation ownership. Based on the posted
band and the scope we discussed, I was hoping for a base of $168,000. If the band is fixed at this
level, a signing bonus of $15,000 would bridge the difference."

The ask is 30 seconds. A long justification reads as anxiety and invites a negotiation about the
justification rather than the number.

### 3.4 The employer's constraints are real

The recruiter is not the decision-maker, and the band is not arbitrary. Three constraints shape what
is possible:

- **Internal equity.** Paying you above others at the same level creates a problem the employer must
  fix for everyone.
- **Level.** Moving a candidate up a level changes the band but requires evidence and approvals.
- **Budget timing.** Signing bonuses come from a different budget line than base and are often
  easier to approve late in a quarter.

Asking for a lever that the employer cannot move wastes the conversation. Asking which levers are
available is a legitimate question: "Is there flexibility on base, signing, or equity at this
level?"

### 3.5 The three conversations

Negotiation is usually three separate conversations, and mixing them causes confusion.

| Conversation | Timing | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Fit and expectations | Recruiter screen | Range, timeline, constraints, level |
| Offer and package | After the written offer | Base, bonus, equity, signing, start date |
| Scope and conditions | After the package is agreed | Decision rights, success criteria, on-call, review date |

The common error is raising scope in the package conversation, which makes the employer think you are
reopening the level. Keep the conversations separate and in order.

### 3.6 The pre-acceptance questions

Money is necessary and insufficient. Before accepting, ask:

| Question | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| What decisions does this role own without escalation? | Whether the scope matches the title |
| What does success look like at six and twelve months, and who judges it? | Whether the expectations are defined |
| How is on-call handled, and who carries the pager? | The real weekly burden |
| What is the model release and change-control process? | Whether you will be blocked or exposed |
| How is the team funded, and what happens if the budget is cut? | Stability |
| What is the remote or hybrid policy in practice, not the policy document? | Daily life |
| Why is the role open, and what happened to the last person? | Risk |
| What is the review and promotion cycle? | Whether the level is a ceiling |

A role that fails three of these is a role to decline regardless of the compensation.

> **Note:** The offer letter is the contract. Verbal assurances about scope, remote work, or a
> future review are not enforceable unless they are written into it or into an attached term sheet.

## 5. The mechanics, step by step

The procedure takes 4 to 6 hours across the offer period. Most of it is research and arithmetic; the
conversation itself is 30 minutes.

### Step 1. Build a market range from at least three sources

**Input:** the role, the level, the geography.
**Output:** a range with a low, midpoint, and high, each with a source and a date.
**Rationale:** a single source produces a single number, and a single number is either an anchor or
a wish. Three sources with different biases produce a range you can defend.

| Source | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Posted pay ranges | Employer-specific, current, legally required in some jurisdictions | Only available where disclosure is required; ranges are wide |
| Government statistics (for example BLS OEWS) | Large sample, consistent methodology | Occupation codes aggregate across industries and geographies; not role-specific |
| Crowdsourced aggregators (for example levels.fyi) | Level-specific, total compensation | Self-reported, skewed toward large technology employers and high-cost hubs |
| Professional society surveys | Sector-specific | Often member-only, self-selected, and infrequent |
| Recruiter conversations | Current and role-specific | The recruiter has an interest in your anchor |
| Your network | Real offers, recent | Small sample, unverifiable |

Read each source for the quantity it reports. A posted range is base salary; a BLS figure is annual
wage; an aggregator figure is usually total compensation; a society survey may report cash
compensation only. Mixing them produces a range that is too wide to be useful.

Use the posted ranges as the primary source where they exist. Use the government data to sanity-check
the distribution. Use the aggregators only for large employers with dense data, and treat a median
built from a handful of entries as a hint.

### Step 1b. Understand the recruiter's incentives

The recruiter is measured on time-to-fill, acceptance rate, and sometimes cost. They want you to
accept, and they usually have more information about the band than you do. They are not your
adversary and they are not your advocate; they are a channel to the approver.

Two practical consequences. First, ask the recruiter what the band is and where the offer sits within
it; recruiters often answer because it helps them close. Second, give the recruiter a reason they can
take to the approver. "The candidate asked for more" is weak; "the candidate's scope includes
regulatory ownership and the posted band supports the midpoint to 75th percentile" is a sentence an
approver can approve.

### Step 2. Normalise everything to total compensation and one geography

**Input:** the ranges.
**Output:** a table with base, bonus, equity, and total, adjusted to one city.
**Rationale:** sources report different quantities. Some report base, some report total cash, some
report total compensation including equity at grant value. Comparing them without normalising
produces a range that is not a range.

Use the formulas in Section 3.1. State the geography index you use and where it came from. If the
role is remote, confirm the band that applies to your location; a national band and a hub band can
differ by 20 percent.

### Step 3. Set the target, the reservation, and the walk-away

**Input:** the normalised range and your current package.
**Output:** three numbers with a one-line justification each.
**Rationale:** deciding the numbers before the conversation prevents anchoring on the employer's
first offer.

- **Target** is the number you ask for. It should be defensible from the range, usually between the
  midpoint and the 75th percentile for a candidate who meets the core requirements.
- **Reservation** is the minimum you will accept. It should account for the current package, the
  commute or relocation, the risk, and the learning.
- **Walk-away** is the point at which staying or continuing the search is better. It can be below
  the reservation if the non-cash terms are unusually good, but it must be decided in advance.

Write them down. A number decided in the conversation is a number chosen under pressure. If you
cannot state a walk-away, you have not decided what you need; you have only decided what you want.

### Step 4. Know your BATNA, and improve it if you can

**Input:** your alternatives.
**Output:** a named alternative with a date and a value.
**Rationale:** the BATNA determines how much risk you can take. A candidate with a current role at
$148,000 and a tolerable manager has a real alternative; a candidate who has already resigned does
not.

If the BATNA is weak, strengthen it before the conversation: schedule another interview, ask for a
decision from another process, or delay the start date. A BATNA with a date is stronger than one
without: "the other process decides on the 14th" is leverage; "I am interviewing elsewhere" is not. Do not invent a BATNA. The recruiter may ask
for the employer's name, the level, and the timeline, and a fabricated answer is difficult to
sustain.

### Step 5. Time the conversation

**Input:** the offer.
**Output:** a scheduled call, 48 to 72 hours after the written offer.
**Rationale:** the employer expects a response within a few days to a week. Asking for 48 to 72
hours to review the written offer is normal and gives you time to compute and to consult.

Script: "Thank you, I am glad to have the offer. I would like to review the written terms and come
back to you by Thursday. Could you send the full package, including the equity details and the
benefits summary?"

Do not negotiate before you have the written offer. Verbal numbers change.

### Step 6. Handle the expectations question early

**Input:** a recruiter asking "what are your salary expectations?"
**Output:** a range with a reason, or a deferral.
**Rationale:** the first number anchors the conversation. If you must give one, give a range whose
bottom is your target, not your reservation.

Options, in order of preference:

1. **Defer with a question.** "I would rather understand the scope and the band for the level before
   I anchor on a number. What range is budgeted for this role?"
2. **Give a researched range.** "Based on the posted band and comparable roles, I am targeting
   $165,000 to $175,000 base, with the full package considered."
3. **Give a total-compensation range.** Useful when equity is a large component, but only if you can
   explain the components.

Where salary history questions are prohibited, you may say so politely: "I understand prior salary
is not something we need to discuss; I am targeting the range for this level."

### Step 7. Compute the package and find the gap

**Input:** the written offer.
**Output:** a table with every component, annualised, and the four-year total.
**Rationale:** the gap between your target and the offer is usually in one or two components, and
the component determines the lever.

Compute: base, target bonus, equity grant and vesting, signing, retirement match, other benefits.
Then compute year one and four years. Compare with your target on the same basis.

### Step 8. Choose the lever

**Input:** the gap and the employer's constraints.
**Output:** one primary ask and one fallback.
**Rationale:** asking for everything at once gives the employer a reason to say no to the package.
One primary ask is easier to approve and easier to explain internally.

| Situation | Primary lever | Fallback | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base below band midpoint, candidate strong | Base | Signing bonus | Base compounds; signing is one-time |
| Base at band maximum | Signing bonus | Equity or early review | Band is a hard constraint |
| Equity small relative to level | Equity grant | Signing bonus | Equity is the long-term lever |
| Level arguably too low | Level and scope | Title plus review date | Level changes the band |
| Cannot relocate | Remote days or start date | Learning budget | Non-cash terms are often flexible |
| Visa prevailing wage constraint | Signing bonus | Equity | Base increase may require a new filing |
| Competing deadline | Decision extension | Signing bonus | Time is the constraint, not money |

### Step 8b. What not to ask for

Some asks reduce the probability of a yes or damage the relationship.

- **A list of five adjustments.** It reads as a package negotiation and gives the approver five
  reasons to say no.
- **A number above the posted band without a level argument.** It is not credible unless the scope
  justifies a different level.
- **Reimbursement for personal costs.** Commuting, childcare, and relocation are legitimate topics,
  but they are benefits questions, not base arguments.
- **A comparison with a colleague's pay.** It invites an internal equity investigation that may
  reduce the offer rather than raise it.
- **A threat to decline.** Unless you mean it, it is a bluff with the same consequences as any other.

### Step 9. Make the ask

**Input:** the lever and the evidence.
**Output:** a 30- to 60-second ask, delivered by phone or in writing.
**Rationale:** the ask is a request with a reason, not an argument. The employer needs a sentence
they can take to the approver.

Structure:

```text
1. Enthusiasm and commitment: "I want this role, and I can start on [date]."
2. The specific ask: "I am asking for a base of $168,000."
3. The reason: "The posted band is $150,000 to $175,000, and the scope we discussed includes
   ownership of the clinical evaluation."
4. The fallback: "If the base is fixed at this level, a signing bonus of $15,000 would work."
```

Do not apologise, do not over-explain, and do not mention personal expenses. The employer is not
negotiating your rent.

### Step 9b. Document the conversation

After each call, send a short email summarising what was discussed: the ask, the response, and the
next step. This is not distrust; it creates a record that the recruiter can forward and that you can
rely on if the offer letter differs from the conversation.

Example: "Thank you for the call. To confirm: I asked for a base of $168,000 with a signing bonus of
$15,000 as a fallback; you said you would check the band and come back by Thursday. I remain
enthusiastic about the role."

### Step 10. Handle the response

**Input:** the counter.
**Output:** an acceptance, a second ask on the fallback, or a decision to decline.
**Rationale:** most responses are one of three: yes, a partial move, or a statement that the number
is fixed. Each has a different next move.

| Response | Meaning | Next move |
|---|---|---|
| "Yes, we can do that" | Approved | Ask for the revised letter |
| "We can do $162,000" | Partial move; the band has room | Accept if above reservation, or ask for the fallback |
| "The base is fixed at this level" | Band or equity constraint | Ask for signing, equity, or a review date |
| "That is above the band" | The level may be wrong | Ask whether the level can be reviewed |
| Silence for several days | Internal approval | Follow up once, with a deadline |

If the employer moves to your reservation exactly, you can accept. Do not make a second ask for the
same lever; it reads as bad faith and the employer may withdraw the goodwill.

### Step 11. Get it in writing and check the terms

**Input:** the revised offer.
**Output:** a signed letter with every agreed term.
**Rationale:** the letter is the contract. Verbal promises about scope, remote work, or a review
disappear when the recruiter moves to another role.

Check: base, bonus target and eligibility date, equity grant, vesting schedule, cliff, strike price
for options, signing amount and clawback, start date, remote or hybrid terms, title, manager, and
any agreed review date. If a term is missing, ask for it in an email that the recruiter confirms,
and keep the email.

### Step 11b. When to walk away

Walking away is a decision, not a tactic. Walk if the final package is below your reservation and
the non-cash terms do not compensate; if the level is below your evidence and the employer will not
review it; if the pre-acceptance questions revealed a role that is not what was described; or if the
process itself was dishonest.

Do not walk to extract a better offer. A withdrawal that is reversed damages credibility, and the
employer will remember it at the review.

### Step 12. Accept, decline, and resign well

**Input:** the decision.
**Output:** a written acceptance or decline, and a resignation that preserves the relationship.
**Rationale:** the biomedical data market is small, and the recruiter you decline today may be the
one who calls in two years.

Accept in writing with the start date. Decline with one sentence of thanks and one of reason, no
negotiation. Resign to your manager first, in person or by call, with a written follow-up; do not
let them hear it from the recruiter. Confirm the notice period in your contract before you name a
start date, and add a week of buffer between the last day and the new role if you can.

### Branch points and edge cases

**Visa constraints.** The prevailing wage for the role and location sets a floor and may limit the
base. A signing bonus or equity grant does not change the prevailing wage in the same way. Ask the
employer's immigration counsel which levers are available before you ask for a base increase.

**Startup equity.** Options are not shares and the strike price is not the value. Ask for the number
of shares, the strike price, the current 409A and preferred price, the fully diluted share count,
the vesting and exercise window, and the liquidation preferences. Then value the grant under three
scenarios. Section 7 implements the arithmetic.

**Public-company RSUs.** The grant value is stated at grant date; the realised value depends on the
share price at vest. Treat the grant as volatile and compare it with cash at a discount if you are
risk-averse.

**Remote geography.** Confirm the band that applies to your residence and whether it changes if you
move. A remote offer at a hub band can be reduced later if the policy changes.

**Returning after a gap.** Do not accept below band out of relief. Use the same range and the same
evidence. If the employer levels you below your evidence, ask for a six-month review with a defined
criterion.

**Over-levelled offer.** If the title is above your evidence, negotiate the support rather than the
money: a mentor, a defined ramp, written success criteria, and a review at six months. Topic 01,
Section 5 covers the decision.

**A competing offer from the same employer.** If you have two roles at one company, the recruiters
may coordinate. Tell them both, ask which role the business prefers, and negotiate with that one.
Do not play them against each other; the internal equity constraint applies to both.

**Exploding offer.** A deadline of 24 to 48 hours is usually artificial. Ask for a one-week
extension in writing; most employers grant it. If they refuse, that is information about how they
will treat deadlines later.

**Internal promotion.** The same levers apply, but the band and the internal equity constraint are
visible to your manager. Ask for the level rationale and the band, and get the review criteria in
writing before accepting.

### Decision table: which lever to ask for

| Constraint | Ask for base | Ask for signing | Ask for equity | Ask for level |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base below band midpoint | Yes | Fallback | No | No |
| Base at band maximum | No | Yes | Yes | No |
| Equity below level norm | No | No | Yes | No |
| Scope above the title | No | No | No | Yes |
| Visa prevailing wage | No | Yes | Yes | No |
| Budget approved, start date flexible | No | Yes | No | No |
| Competing offer with a deadline | No | Yes | No | No |
| Scope includes regulatory ownership | No | No | No | Yes |

### Decision table: equity scenarios

| Scenario | Private options | Public RSUs |
|---|---|---|
| Base case | Current 409A or last round price | Current share price |
| Downside | Strike price or zero | 30 percent below current |
| Upside | Last preferred price or a multiple | 30 percent above current |
| Liquidity | Assume illiquid until an exit | Liquid at vest |

## 6. Worked example

Nadia is 31, a clinical data scientist with five years of experience at a health plan. She earns
$148,000 base with an 8 percent bonus and a 5 percent retirement match. She receives an offer from a
health-tech company in Boston and has a second process at a Raleigh-based payer at final stage.

### 6.1 The offer

| Component | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Base | $155,000 |
| Target bonus | 10% |
| RSU grant | $80,000 over 4 years, 1-year cliff |
| Signing bonus | $10,000 |
| Retirement match | 4% |
| Location | Boston, hybrid 3 days |

### 6.2 The market range

She builds the range from three sources:

| Source | Base range | Notes |
|---|---:|---|
| Posted band for the role | $150,000–$175,000 | Boston, senior level |
| BLS data scientist 75th percentile | About $158,880 | National, all industries (OEWS May 2025) |
| Aggregator, comparable employers | $165,000 median total cash | Self-reported, thin for this employer |

Her target is $168,000 base, between the posted midpoint ($162,500) and the 75th percentile of the
posted band. Her reservation is $158,000, which is a 7 percent increase on her current base and
above the BLS 75th percentile. Her walk-away is $152,000 with a signing bonus, because the role
offers clinical evaluation ownership she does not have today.

### 6.3 Diagnostic 1: the first comparison used base only

Her first comparison was Offer A at $155,000 against the Raleigh offer at $148,000. On base alone,
A was ahead by $7,000, or 4.7 percent.

The full comparison:

| Component | Offer A (Boston) | Offer B (Raleigh) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Base | $155,000 | $148,000 |
| Target bonus | 10% ($15,500) | 8% ($11,840) |
| Equity per year | $20,000 | $0 |
| Signing | $10,000 | $5,000 |
| Retirement match | 4% ($6,200) | 6% ($8,880) |
| Annual cash | $176,700 | $168,720 |
| Year 1 total | $206,700 | $173,720 |
| Four-year total | $796,800 | $679,880 |
| Cost-of-living index | 100.0 | 90.0 |
| Year 1, Boston-equivalent | $206,700 | $193,022 |

The four-year gap is $116,920 nominal: $796,800 against $679,880. The gap is $32,980 in year one
nominal and $13,678 after adjusting for cost of living. The equity
and the bonus, not the base, create most of the difference. Her base-only comparison understated the
gap by a factor of nearly five.

> **Note:** The cost-of-living index values are illustrative inputs for the arithmetic, not
> published index values. Use a current published index and record its source and year.

### 6.4 Diagnostic 2: she bluffed, and it cost her

In the first call, Nadia said she had "another offer at $175,000" to anchor the negotiation. The
recruiter asked which company and what the timeline was. Nadia deflected. The recruiter became
guarded, said the band was tight, and the written offer came in at $155,000, below the midpoint of
the posted band.

The bluff failed for three reasons. The number was above the posted band, which made it implausible.
The recruiter had probably heard the claim before and had a script for testing it. And the
credibility cost persisted: when Nadia later asked for $168,000 with a legitimate market argument,
the recruiter treated it as another negotiation tactic rather than a request.

The corrected approach uses the real BATNA. Her current role at $148,000 plus 8 percent is a real
alternative, and the Raleigh process at final stage is a real competing timeline. The ask becomes:

> "I want the role, and the clinical evaluation ownership is exactly the direction I want. The posted
> band is $150,000 to $175,000, and the scope we discussed sits above the midpoint. I am asking for a
> base of $168,000. If the base is fixed at this level, a signing bonus of $15,000 would bridge the
> difference."

| Stage | Base | Signing | RSU | Notes |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Initial offer | $155,000 | $10,000 | $80,000 | Below posted midpoint |
| Her ask | $168,000 | $15,000 fallback | — | One primary, one fallback |

The final package is a compromise: below her target, above her reservation, with the review date

```text

annual equity = 80,000 / 4 = 20,000
year 1        = 184,680 + 20,000 + 15,000 = 219,680
four years    = 4 x 184,680 + 80,000 + 15,000 = 833,720
```

Compared with the initial offer, the gain is $12,980 in year one and $36,920 over four years, before
any compounding of the higher base through future raises.

### 6.6 The pre-acceptance questions

Before signing, she asked the eight questions from Section 3.5. Three answers changed her view:

1. **Decision rights.** She would own the imaging performance chapter of a regulatory submission,
   with a regulatory lead reviewing. That is real scope, and it justified the senior title.
2. **On-call.** The team carried a pager for the model service, one week in six. She had not known,
   and it changed her assessment of the total package.
3. **Why the role was open.** The previous holder left after eight months, citing scope ambiguity.
   The written success criteria and the six-month review addressed the risk, but she asked for the

### 6.7 Vary one assumption

Change one input: the Raleigh offer is replaced by a startup offer in Boston with options instead of
RSUs.

| Component | Startup offer |
|---|---:|
| Base | $150,000 |
| Bonus | 0% |
| Options | 60,000 shares at a $3.00 strike |
| Current preferred price | $9.00 |
| Vesting | 4 years, 1-year cliff |
| Signing | $0 |
| Match | 0% |

The paper value of the options is 60,000 × ($9.00 − $3.00) = $360,000, or $90,000 a year if the
company reaches the preferred price and the shares are liquid. That is more than four times the RSU
grant on paper.

Three scenarios change the decision:

| Scenario | Share price at exit | Value per year | Four-year value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Downside | $3.00 | $0 | $0 |
| Base | $9.00 | $90,000 | $360,000 |
| Upside | $18.00 | $225,000 | $900,000 |

At a 30 percent probability of the base case and 10 percent of the upside, the expected equity value
is 0.3 × $360,000 + 0.1 × $900,000 = $198,000, or $49,500 a year. Even then, the startup's
four-year total is 4 × $150,000 + $198,000 = $798,000, which is below the health-tech offer's
$833,720 and carries illiquidity, a 90-day exercise window, and no match.

The decision is not automatic. A candidate with savings, a higher risk tolerance, and a belief in
the company may prefer the startup. A candidate supporting a family may not. The point is that the
comparison is made with scenarios and probabilities, not with the paper value.

The startup offer also changes the negotiation levers. With no bonus and no match, the only levers
are base, the number of options, the strike price (rarely negotiable), and the exercise window. The
most valuable ask is usually the grant size, because it is the only component that scales with the
company's success, and the second most valuable is the exercise window, because a 90-day window can
force an expensive decision at the worst possible time.

The general lesson: equity is a distribution, not a number. Negotiate the grant size, the strike
price, and the exercise window, and value the result under at least three scenarios before comparing
it with cash.

## 7. Code, query, or calculation

The script compares the three offers from Section 6, applies a cost-of-living adjustment, and values
the startup options under three scenarios. It uses only the standard library and states every input
in one place.

Assumptions:

- Offer terms are the negotiated terms from Section 6.5, not the initial offers.
- The cost-of-living indices (Boston 100.0, Raleigh 90.0) are illustrative inputs, not published
  values.
- Option scenarios and probabilities are the candidate's judgements, not market forecasts. The
  expected value is a decision aid, not a price.

```python
"""Offer comparison with equity scenarios and cost-of-living adjustment.

Run: python offer_compare.py
Standard library only. Compares a health-tech offer, a payer offer in a
lower-cost city, and a startup offer with options, under three share-price
scenarios.
"""

OFFERS = [
    {
        "name": "A health-tech, Boston",
        "base": 162_000,
        "bonus_rate": 0.10,
        "equity_per_year": 20_000,   # RSU grant 80k over 4 years
        "signing": 15_000,
        "match_rate": 0.04,
        "col_index": 100.0,
    },
    {
        "name": "B payer, Raleigh",
        "base": 148_000,
        "bonus_rate": 0.08,
        "equity_per_year": 0,
        "signing": 5_000,
        "match_rate": 0.06,
        "col_index": 90.0,
    },
    {
        "name": "C startup, Boston",
        "base": 150_000,
        "bonus_rate": 0.0,
        "equity_per_year": 0,        # options handled separately
        "signing": 0,
        "match_rate": 0.0,
        "col_index": 100.0,
    },
]

# Startup option terms.
OPTION_SHARES = 60_000
STRIKE = 3.00
SCENARIOS = [
    ("downside", 3.00, 0.60),
    ("base", 9.00, 0.30),
    ("upside", 18.00, 0.10),
]


def annual_cash(offer):
    return offer["base"] * (1 + offer["bonus_rate"]) + offer["base"] * offer["match_rate"]


def year_one(offer):
    return annual_cash(offer) + offer["equity_per_year"] + offer["signing"]


def four_year(offer):
    return 4 * annual_cash(offer) + 4 * offer["equity_per_year"] + offer["signing"]


def real(value, offer):
    return value / (offer["col_index"] / 100.0)


def option_value(price):
    return OPTION_SHARES * max(0.0, price - STRIKE)


def main():
    print("OFFER COMPARISON (NOMINAL USD)")
    print(f"  {'offer':24s} {'annual cash':>12s} {'year 1':>12s} {'4 years':>12s}")
    for o in OFFERS:
        print(f"  {o['name']:24s} {annual_cash(o):12,.0f} {year_one(o):12,.0f} {four_year(o):12,.0f}")

    print()
    print("COST-OF-LIVING ADJUSTED (BOSTON = 100)")
    for o in OFFERS:
        print(f"  {o['name']:24s} index {o['col_index']:5.1f}  "
              f"year 1 real {real(year_one(o), o):12,.0f}  "
              f"4y real {real(four_year(o), o):12,.0f}")

    print()
    print("STARTUP OPTIONS: VALUE BY SCENARIO")
    print(f"  {'scenario':10s} {'price':>7s} {'prob':>6s} {'value':>12s} {'per year':>12s} {'weighted':>12s}")
    weighted = 0.0
    for name, price, prob in SCENARIOS:
        value = option_value(price)
        weighted += value * prob
        print(f"  {name:10s} {price:7.2f} {prob:6.2f} {value:12,.0f} {value / 4:12,.0f} {value * prob:12,.0f}")
    print(f"  expected option value over 4 years: {weighted:,.0f}")

    startup = OFFERS[2]
    startup_4y = four_year(startup) + weighted
    print()
    print("STARTUP TOTAL WITH EXPECTED EQUITY")
    print(f"  cash over 4 years:            {four_year(startup):,.0f}")
    print(f"  expected equity:              {weighted:,.0f}")
    print(f"  total:                        {startup_4y:,.0f}")
    print(f"  health-tech offer for compare: {four_year(OFFERS[0]):,.0f}")
    print(f"  difference:                   {startup_4y - four_year(OFFERS[0]):,.0f}")

    print()
    print("GAP: INITIAL OFFER VS NEGOTIATED (HEALTH-TECH)")
    initial = 155_000
    initial_cash = initial * 1.10 + initial * 0.04
    initial_4y = 4 * initial_cash + 80_000 + 10_000
    print(f"  initial four-year value:    {initial_4y:,.0f}")
    print(f"  negotiated four-year value: {four_year(OFFERS[0]):,.0f}")
    print(f"  gain:                       {four_year(OFFERS[0]) - initial_4y:,.0f}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

## 8. Data, standards, and regulatory context

Pay negotiation is increasingly regulated, and the rules differ by jurisdiction. The practical
consequence is that some questions are prohibited, some ranges must be disclosed, and some
comparisons are protected. Knowing the rules lets you ask for information you are entitled to and
decline questions you are not.

### 8.1 Binding rules

| Rule | Jurisdiction | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| Directive (EU) 2023/970, Article 5 | European Union | Applicants may receive the initial pay or range on request; employers may not ask about pay history; job titles and vacancy notices must be gender-neutral |
| Colorado Equal Pay for Equal Work Act | Colorado, United States | Pay range disclosure in postings and promotion opportunities |
| California SB 1162 | California, United States | Pay scale disclosure in postings; pay data reporting |
| New York State pay transparency law | New York, United States | Pay range disclosure in advertisements |
| Washington Equal Pay and Opportunities Act | Washington, United States | Pay range disclosure on request or in postings, depending on size |
| National Labor Relations Act, Section 7 | United States | Employees may discuss wages and working conditions |
| Equal Pay Act of 1963 | United States | Equal pay for substantially equal work |

The EU directive was adopted on 10 May 2023 and published on 17 May 2023, with a transposition
deadline of 7 June 2026. Article 5(1) gives applicants the right to information about the initial pay
or its range on objective, gender-neutral criteria; Article 5(2) prohibits employers from asking
about pay history; Article 5(3) requires gender-neutral vacancy notices and job titles.

The United States has no federal pay-range disclosure requirement. Protections come from state and
local law, and roughly half of states have banned salary history inquiries according to trackers
maintained by legal publishers. The trackers are secondary sources; verify the current statute for
the state where the role is located.

### 8.2 What is convention

- **A counter is expected.** Most employers build room into the first offer, and a polite counter is
  a normal part of the process. This is a market convention, not a legal right.
- **A response window of 48 to 72 hours** is conventional. Some employers impose shorter deadlines,
  and asking for an extension is normal.
- **Equity terms** are governed by the grant agreement, not by the offer letter summary. The grant
  agreement is the document that defines vesting, exercise, and termination treatment.
- **Signing bonus clawbacks** are common and are stated in the offer or a separate repayment
  agreement. Read the clawback before accepting.

### 8.3 What the rules do not do

They do not guarantee a particular offer, and they do not create a right to a range in every
jurisdiction. They do not make equity liquid, and they do not protect you from a bad grant agreement.
They do not resolve the internal equity constraint, which is the reason many bands are inflexible.

The most useful protection is documentation: the written offer, the grant agreement, the benefits
summary, and the emails that confirm verbal terms. If a term matters to your decision, get it in
writing before you sign.

A second protection is a calculator. The rules determine what information you can obtain; they do not
tell you what an offer is worth. That is arithmetic, and Section 7 implements it.

### 8.4 What you may disclose and discuss

In the United States, Section 7 of the National Labor Relations Act protects employees' rights to
discuss wages and working conditions with each other, with limited exceptions for supervisors. That
protection does not create a right to a range from a prospective employer, and it does not override a
confidentiality obligation about customer or patient data.

In practice, the safe position is: discuss your own compensation freely with colleagues if you choose,
do not disclose another person's compensation, and do not bring confidential employer data into a
negotiation. A candidate who quotes an internal band document from a current employer has created a
governance problem, not leverage.

### 8.5 Visa and immigration constraints

In the United States, the H-1B prevailing wage for the role and location sets a floor, and the
employer must pay at least that amount. A base increase may require a new labour condition
application; a signing bonus or equity grant may not. In the United Kingdom, the skilled worker
salary threshold applies. These rules are specific and change; ask the employer's immigration counsel
which levers are available before you negotiate.

## 9. Industry practice and case evidence

### 9.1 Documented: the distribution is wide

The BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook reports a median annual wage for data scientists of $120,230
in May 2025, with the lowest 10 percent below $67,240 and the highest 10 percent above $199,130. The
OEWS profile for the same occupation reports a 25th percentile of $85,660 and a 75th percentile of
$158,880, with a mean of $126,800.

The distribution is wide because the occupation aggregates across industries and geographies. A
clinical data scientist at a health system, a real-world evidence scientist at a pharmaceutical
company, and a machine learning engineer at a vendor are all counted, and their compensation differs
substantially.

### 9.2 Documented: posted ranges are now common in some jurisdictions

The postings cited in Topic 01 include ranges such as $90,000 to $140,000 for a clinical data
scientist in Texas, $130,000 to $260,000 for a lead clinical informatics data scientist, $134,000 to
$201,000 for a senior clinical data scientist in California, and $131,000 to $187,000 for a senior
clinical data manager in Massachusetts. These are base salary ranges as posted, not total
compensation.

The ranges are wide, and the width is information: a range that spans 60 percent of the midpoint
usually means the employer is hiring at multiple levels within one posting. Ask which level the offer
corresponds to.

### 9.3 Documented: crowdsourced data has known biases

Levels.fyi describes a tiered verification system: self-reported entries, entries with proof of
identity, and entries with direct proof such as offer letters or pay stubs. The company states that
verified data are used to anchor self-reported entries and to remove outliers, and that level
normalisation is "an inexact science."

The founder has acknowledged in public discussion that the data are likely biased, and the site's
coverage is concentrated in large technology employers in major hubs. A median built from a handful
of entries for a niche role is a hint, not a fact. Use the site for large employers with dense data,
and use posted ranges and government statistics for everything else.

### 9.4 Documented: sector surveys exist but are narrow

The HIMSS 2022 Nursing Informatics Workforce Survey, with about 1,100 respondents, reported that 60
percent earned more than $100,000, up from 49 percent in 2020. The survey is specific to nursing
informatics, self-selected, and not a representative sample of biomedical data roles. It is useful
for a nursing informatics negotiation and not for a data science one.

### 9.5 What the sources agree on

Across government statistics, posted ranges, and sector surveys, three patterns are consistent. The
distribution within a title is wide, often spanning 50 percent or more from the 25th to the 75th
percentile. Geography explains a substantial share of the variation. And equity, where it exists,
is a large share of total compensation at senior levels and a small share at junior levels.

The patterns are consistent; the numbers are not directly comparable. A BLS wage includes base pay
and may include some additional pay depending on the occupation; a posted range is base; an
aggregator figure is usually total compensation. Comparing them without normalising produces a false
precision.

### 9.6 Inference, labelled

- **Inference:** the largest negotiation gains for mid-level candidates come from moving between
  employers rather than from an internal raise, because bands are wider at hire than at review. This
  is inferred from posted ranges and from the structure of compensation cycles, not from a
  controlled study.
- **Inference:** signing bonuses are the most available lever when base is constrained, because they
  come from a different budget line and do not affect internal equity.
- **Inference:** equity is undervalued by candidates from academia and overvalued by candidates from
  startups, and both errors are corrected by scenario analysis.
- **Inference:** candidates who ask about decision rights and success criteria are more likely to
  negotiate scope successfully, because the conversation is about the work rather than the number.

> **Note:** No average negotiation gain, acceptance rate, or offer uplift is quoted. Public figures
> for these are usually vendor surveys with self-selected samples, and the variation by level,
> geography, and employer is larger than any single average.

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why it is needed | What breaks it | How to test it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The posted band is the real band | It anchors the ask | Some employers post a wide range for multiple levels | Ask which level the offer is for |
| Equity has positive expected value | It justifies inclusion in total comp | Many startups return zero | Value under a downside scenario |
| Cost-of-living indices are comparable | They enable cross-city comparison | Indices weight housing and consumption differently | Use the same index for all offers and state it |
| The recruiter can influence the offer | It justifies the conversation | Some bands are fixed by a committee | Ask what flexibility exists |
| A counter is expected | It justifies asking | Some employers make a final first offer | Ask whether the offer is negotiable |
| 48 to 72 hours is available | It enables computation | Some offers are genuinely urgent | Ask for the deadline in writing |
| The role is as described | It justifies accepting | Postings and interviews can differ from the job | Ask the pre-acceptance questions |
| Future raises are percentage-based | It justifies negotiating base | Some employers use fixed increases | Ask how the review cycle works |
| The BATNA is stable | It determines leverage | Another offer can expire | Confirm dates before the call |
| Benefits are comparable | It simplifies the comparison | Health premiums and pensions differ materially | The ranking changes | Request the benefits summary and compare |

The main limit is that negotiation advice is conditional on a market you cannot fully observe. The
band, the internal equity, and the approver's mood are hidden. The method reduces the uncertainty; it
does not remove it. A polite ask that is declined costs almost nothing, and the expected value of
asking is positive in most mid-level hiring processes.

## 12. Exercises

**Exercise 1. Market range (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a range for one role and geography from three named sources, with the quantity each
reports and its limitation.
Acceptance criteria: sources are dated; the quantities are normalised to base or total; the range has
a low, midpoint, and high; at least one limitation is stated per source.

**Exercise 2. Total rewards (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a four-year total for two offers, with every component shown.
Acceptance criteria: base, bonus, equity, signing, and match are separate lines; the arithmetic is
reproducible; the comparison states which offer is higher and by how much.

**Exercise 3. BATNA (application, 20 minutes).**
Deliverable: a written BATNA, reservation, target, and walk-away, each with one sentence of
justification.
Acceptance criteria: the BATNA is real and dated; reservation is above walk-away; target is within
the researched range; each number has a one-line reason that would survive being said aloud.

**Exercise 4. The ask (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a 60-second script with enthusiasm, the specific ask, the reason, and the fallback.
Acceptance criteria: one primary lever; a reason tied to market evidence or scope; no personal
expenses; delivered in under 60 seconds.

**Exercise 5. Lever selection (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a lever recommendation for four scenarios (base at band max, visa constraint, level too
low, remote constraint).
Acceptance criteria: each recommendation names a primary and a fallback; the reason references the
employer's constraint; at least one scenario correctly avoids asking for base.

**Exercise 6. Geography (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: two offers compared nominally and with a published cost-of-living index.
Acceptance criteria: the index and its year are cited; the adjustment is applied to the total, not
only base; the conclusion states whether the ranking changes.

**Exercise 7. Equity scenarios (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a three-scenario valuation of a private-company grant with probabilities and an expected
value.
Acceptance criteria: strike, share count, and vesting are stated; the downside is zero or the strike;
the expected value is labelled as a judgement; the comparison includes illiquidity.

**Exercise 8. Pre-acceptance memo (design, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a one-page memo with the eight questions, the answers you received, and a
recommendation.
Acceptance criteria: every question has an answer or a stated unknown; the recommendation is
conditional on the unknowns; at least one answer changes the decision.

## 13. Self-check answers

**1. Why build a range from three sources?**
Because each source has a different bias. Posted ranges are employer-specific but only available
where disclosure is required; government statistics are large but aggregate occupations; crowdsourced
data are level-specific but self-reported and skewed. Three sources with different biases produce a
range you can defend.

**2. Compute the four-year value for a $155,000 base, 10 percent bonus, $80,000 RSU over four years,
$10,000 signing, and 4 percent match.**
Annual cash is $155,000 × 1.10 + $155,000 × 0.04 = $170,500 + $6,200 = $176,700. Four years of cash
is $706,800; add $80,000 of equity and $10,000 signing for $796,800.

**3. What is a BATNA, and why must it be real?**
The best alternative to a negotiated agreement: what you do if this offer fails. It must be real
because a bluff is testable, and a discovered bluff damages credibility for the rest of the process.

**4. What are the four parts of a good ask?**
Enthusiasm and commitment, the specific adjustment, the reason, and the fallback lever.

**5. Base is at the band maximum. What do you ask for?**
A signing bonus, additional equity, or a written review date. Asking for base above the maximum
requires a level change, which is a different conversation.

**6. How does cost-of-living adjustment change a comparison?**
It converts nominal offers to a common geography. In the worked example, the Raleigh offer rises from
$679,880 to $755,422 in Boston-equivalent terms, closing part but not all of the gap.

**7. Why is startup equity valued in scenarios?**
Because the value depends on an exit price and liquidity that are unknown. A single number at the
preferred price overstates the expected value and hides the probability of zero.

**8. Name four pre-acceptance questions.**
Decision rights without escalation; success criteria and who judges them; on-call and pager
responsibility; why the role is open and what happened to the previous holder. Any four of the eight
in Section 3.5 are acceptable if the reason for asking is stated.

**Rubric for Exercise 7.** Full marks: strike, share count, and vesting stated; three scenarios with
probabilities; downside is zero or the strike; expected value labelled as a judgement; illiquidity
and exercise window included in the comparison.

**Rubric for Exercise 5.** Full marks: four scenarios; a primary and fallback for each; the reason
references the employer's constraint; at least one scenario avoids base; visa and level scenarios are
handled correctly.

**Rubric for Exercise 8.** Full marks: eight questions with answers or stated unknowns; a conditional
recommendation; at least one answer changes the decision; the memo is under one page.

## 14. Interview questions

**1. "What are your salary expectations?"**
Strong: a researched range with a reason, or a deferral that asks for the band. Weak: a single number
with no basis, or "whatever is fair."

**2. "Why do you want this role?"**
Strong: the employer's problem, the role's decision, and the transferable evidence. Weak: mission
language.

**3. "Do you have other offers?"**
Strong: a factual statement of processes and timelines, with no invented numbers. Weak: a bluff.

**4. "How do you compare offers?"**
Strong: four-year total, equity terms, cost of living, scope, and review cadence. Weak: base salary.

**5. "What would make you decline?"**
Strong: a specific scope or safety condition. Weak: says nothing would.

**6. "How do you value equity?"**
Strong: scenarios, strike price, vesting, liquidity, and exercise window. Weak: quotes the grant
value as cash.

**7. "What are your notice period and start date?"**
Strong: a specific date with buffer and a confirmation of the notice terms. Weak: "as soon as
possible."

**8. "Are you negotiating with anyone else?"**
Strong: a factual answer that protects confidentiality and states the timeline. Weak: invents
competition.

**9. "What questions do you have for us?"**
Strong: decision rights, success criteria, on-call, funding, and review cycle. Weak: benefits only.

**10. "What if we cannot move on base?"**
Strong: names the fallback lever and the reason it works for both sides. Weak: repeats the base ask.

**11. "Why is the band structured this way?"**
Strong: level, geography, and internal equity. Weak: assumes the band is arbitrary.

## 15. Key terms

- **409A valuation** — independent valuation of private-company common stock.
- **Anchor** — the first number in a negotiation.
- **Base salary** — fixed annual pay before bonus and equity.
- **BATNA** — best alternative to a negotiated agreement.
- **Clawback** — repayment obligation if you leave early.
- **Cliff** — the period before any equity vests.
- **Comp band** — the approved range for a level and geography.
- **Cost-of-living index** — relative price index for a geography.
- **Equity** — ownership or appreciation rights granted over time.
- **Exercise window** — period after leaving to exercise options.
- **Exploding offer** — offer with an unreasonably short deadline.
- **Internal equity** — pay consistency across employees at the same level.
- **Level** — the rung that determines band and scope.
- **Non-compete** — clause restricting future employment.
- **Notice period** — required period before leaving.
- **Percentile** — position within a market distribution.
- **Refresh** — additional equity granted after the initial grant.
- **Reservation price** — the minimum acceptable package.
- **Restricted stock unit (RSU)** — a promise of shares that vest on a schedule.
- **Retirement match** — employer contribution to a retirement plan.
- **Signing bonus** — one-time payment at start.
- **Salary history ban** — prohibition on asking about prior pay.
- **Stock option** — right to buy shares at a strike price.
- **Strike price** — price at which an option can be exercised.
- **Target bonus** — bonus at 100 percent performance.
- **Total compensation** — base plus bonus plus equity value.
- **Total rewards** — compensation plus benefits and non-cash terms.
- **Vesting** — schedule on which equity becomes yours.
