## 1. Why this topic exists

The behavioural interview is where the employer decides whether you are safe to put in a room with
a clinician, a regulator, or a client. The technical stages test whether you can do the work. The
behavioural stage tests whether they want you to do it here, with these people, when something goes
wrong.

The questions are predictable: a failure, a conflict, a time you disagreed with someone senior, a
time you had to say no, a time you handled sensitive data. The answers are not. Most candidates
tell a story about what happened. Strong candidates tell a story about what they decided, what they
did specifically, what resulted, and what they changed afterwards.

### 1.1 What the interviewer is listening for

Four signals, in roughly this order:

1. **Ownership.** Do you describe your part precisely, including the parts that were yours alone?
2. **Judgment under ambiguity.** Do you show a decision process, or do you report events?
3. **Awareness of impact on others.** Do you understand why the other person behaved as they did?
4. **Learning that changed behaviour.** Do you name a specific change, or a generic lesson?

A candidate who covers all four in 90 seconds per story is strong. A candidate who covers none and
speaks for five minutes is not, regardless of how impressive the project was.

### 1.2 The failure that looks like humility

The common weak answer to "tell me about a failure" is a story about a project that failed for
reasons outside the candidate's control: the data were late, the sponsor changed scope, the
clinician would not engage. It sounds humble and it is not. It communicates that the candidate does
not see their own agency, or will not admit it.

The strong version names a decision the candidate made that turned out wrong, the signal they
missed, what they did when they noticed, and the rule they now apply. The failure can be small. A
small failure owned precisely is more persuasive than a large failure explained by circumstance.

### 1.3 Why the format is used, and what the evidence says

Behavioural questions rest on behavioural consistency: past behaviour in similar situations
predicts future behaviour. The meta-analytic evidence supports the format when it is structured and
scored. Taylor and Small (2002) reported a mean validity of .63 for past-behaviour questions with
descriptively anchored scales, against .47 for situational questions with the same scoring. Later
work has revised the absolute estimates downward, and Wingate, Bourdage and Steel (2024) reported
an overall interview validity of .28 corrected for criterion unreliability, with structured
question-based scoring improving validity.

The practical reading is not that the numbers are precise. It is that the interviewer is likely
working from a competency list, asking the same questions of every candidate, and scoring the
presence of specific behaviours. A vivid story that does not contain the behaviour scores zero
however entertaining it is.

### 1.4 What this topic produces

A story bank of eight to ten incidents, each mapped to the competencies your target roles assess,
each rehearsed at 90 seconds and 3 minutes, plus a method for handling the questions you did not
prepare. Section 6 shows a candidate rebuilding two weak stories and the effect on the loop.

### 1.5 The interview is a sample of your judgement, not your history

A behavioural loop asks six to ten questions. It cannot cover your career, so it samples. The
sample is biased toward situations with conflict, failure, and pressure, because those are the
situations where dispositions show.

That is why a single strong story can travel: the interviewer is not counting your experiences, they
are looking for a pattern of ownership under difficulty. If the sample contains one incident where
you owned a mistake and changed your behaviour, the interviewer can infer the disposition even
without a second example.

### 1.6 Why the same story can answer different questions

An incident contains many behaviours. A disagreement about a cohort definition contains conflict,
influence, judgment, and integrity. The interviewer's question selects which behaviour is in the
foreground, and the candidate's emphasis determines what is scored.

This is not spin. The facts stay the same; the opening sentence and the action you dwell on change.
A candidate who tells the conflict version when asked about influence has misread the question, not
lied.

### 1.7 What this topic does not cover

It does not cover the clinical case (Topic 06), system design (Topic 07), or negotiation (Topic 09).
It does not teach you to fabricate a narrative; the stories must be true and yours. It does not
cover the legal boundaries of what an employer may ask in detail; Topic 04, Section 8 covers that.

## 3. The core mental model

A behavioural answer is an evidence claim about a disposition. The story is the vehicle; the
disposition is the payload. The interviewer is matching your story against a competency rubric, and
the rubric rewards specificity, ownership, and reflection.

```text
  QUESTION            "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a clinician."
      |
      v
  COMPETENCY          Conflict resolution, influence, clinical safety, escalation
      |
      v
  STORY               Situation -> Task -> Action -> Result -> Insight
      |                                    |
      |                                    +--> what you did, specifically
      v
  EVIDENCE            "I" statements, numbers, named decision, named consequence
      |
      v
  INFERENCE           Would this person escalate appropriately in our hospital?
```

The interviewer moves from the question to the inference; the candidate moves from the story to the
evidence. The two paths meet at the action and result.

### 3.1 The six-line structure

STAR is the common frame, and it is incomplete for senior roles. Add insight and transfer.

| Line | Content | Time at 90 seconds |
|---|---|---:|
| Situation | When, where, and what was at stake | 10 seconds |
| Task | What you were responsible for | 10 seconds |
| Action | What you did, in first person, with the decision named | 40 seconds |
| Result | What happened, with a number if possible | 15 seconds |
| Insight | What you learned about the situation or yourself | 10 seconds |
| Transfer | What you now do differently, or would do here | 5 seconds |

The proportions matter. Northwestern Career Advancement's guidance suggests roughly 15 percent
situation, 10 percent task, 50 percent action, and 25 percent result. The action is the longest
part because it is the only part that is about you.

### 3.2 The specificity ladder

Stories improve as they move from generality to specificity.

| Rung | Form | Example |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Trait claim | "I am a strong collaborator" |
| 2 | General practice | "I always keep stakeholders informed" |
| 3 | Event | "We had a disagreement about the cohort definition" |
| 4 | Specific action | "I brought a one-page comparison of the two definitions and the counts each produced" |
| 5 | Named decision | "We chose the stricter definition because it changed the eligible population by 12 percent" |
| 6 | Consequence and change | "The stricter definition became the standard, and I now bring a count comparison to every definition meeting" |

Rungs 1 and 2 are not answers. Rungs 4 to 6 are. Most candidates stop at rung 3 and wonder why the
interviewer keeps probing.

### 3.3 The story bank and the competency map

Employers do not ask about your stories; they ask about their competencies. The bank is a mapping
problem: a small number of stories should cover a large number of competencies.

```text
  COMPETENCIES (from the job description)
    ownership | conflict | failure | ambiguity | influence
    prioritisation | safety | data ethics | mentoring | saying no
        \_________________________________________________/
                          |
                    STORY BANK (8-10)
        S1 failed model validation      -> failure, ownership, safety
        S2 disagreed with clinician     -> conflict, influence, safety
        S3 cut scope before a deadline  -> prioritisation, saying no
        S4 found leakage                -> data ethics, judgment
        S5 mentored a junior analyst    -> mentoring, leadership
        ...
```

A story can cover three competencies if it is told with the right emphasis. The same incident can
answer "tell me about a conflict" and "tell me about influencing without authority," with a
different action in the foreground. That is not fabrication; it is selection.

### 3.4 The story bank is indexed by competency, not by chronology

A common mistake is to organise stories by job: the CRO story, the hospital story, the PhD story.
The interviewer does not care which job the story came from; they care which competency it
evidences.

Index by competency and keep the chronology in a single line inside the story. That way, a question
about influence pulls the best influence story regardless of when it happened, and the answer does
not begin with five years of context.

### 3.5 The ten-minute version

Some stages ask for a project walkthrough rather than a single story. The same discipline applies:
situation and task in one minute, action in five, result in two, insight and transfer in two. The
failure mode is identical, a long situation and a short action.

Practise the ten-minute version once per project you intend to discuss. It is the version a hiring
manager asks for when they say "walk me through it," and it is the version most likely to expose
whether you actually owned the work.

### 3.6 Conflict is a process question

Interviewers ask about conflict because clinical work is full of disagreement about definitions,
thresholds, and priorities. The rubric usually rewards:

- Understanding the other person's interest, not just their position.
- Using evidence rather than authority.
- Escalating through the right channel when the disagreement could affect safety.
- Preserving the relationship afterwards.

The weak answer wins the argument and loses the relationship, or avoids the disagreement entirely.
The strong answer shows a process that resolved the issue and a relationship that survived it.

> **Note:** In a clinical safety disagreement, the correct answer may be to escalate even when it
> damages the relationship. Say so. An interviewer who hears "I always defer to the clinician" will
> worry about the next unsafe shortcut.

## 5. The mechanics, step by step

The procedure builds a story bank in 10 to 14 hours and maintains it in about an hour per new
employer. It assumes you have at least two years of work or research experience; the edge cases
below cover candidates who do not.

### Step 1. Decode the competencies before writing stories

**Input:** the job description, the employer's public competency framework if it has one, and the
interviewer roles.
**Output:** a list of six to ten competencies with the behaviour each rewards.
**Rationale:** the interviewer scores against a rubric. Writing stories before knowing the rubric
is guessing.

Competencies are usually written as behaviours, not skills. Translate:

| Posting phrase | Competency | Behaviour the rubric rewards |
|---|---|---|
| "Partner with clinical stakeholders" | Influence and communication | Adjusts language to the audience; resolves disagreement with evidence |
| "Work independently in ambiguity" | Judgment | Defines the question; states assumptions; knows when to ask |
| "Ensure data integrity" | Integrity and safety | Raises problems; documents; refuses an unsafe shortcut |
| "Mentor junior analysts" | Developing others | Specific teaching; feedback; measurable improvement |
| "Deliver under regulatory timelines" | Prioritisation | Trades scope; escalates early; protects the critical path |

If the posting lists no competencies, use the six that biomedical employers assess most: ownership,
judgment under ambiguity, collaboration and conflict, communication to non-specialists, integrity
and safety, and developing others.

### Step 2. Inventory incidents before selecting stories

**Input:** your history.
**Output:** 15 to 20 incidents, one line each, with a date and a rough outcome.
**Rationale:** the best story for a competency is often not the one you remember first. The
inventory surfaces candidates you would not have chosen under pressure.

An incident qualifies if it has four elements: a decision, a consequence, a person affected, and
something you would do differently. Incidents without a decision are tasks; incidents without a
consequence are activities.

Include incidents from outside work if you are early career: a thesis crisis, a volunteer
coordination problem, a teaching conflict. The rubric cares about behaviour, not job titles.

### Step 3. Select eight to ten stories and map coverage

**Input:** the incident inventory and the competency list.
**Output:** a coverage matrix with each competency covered by at least one story, and each story
tagged with its strongest two competencies.
**Rationale:** coverage prevents the common failure of having five stories about technical
brilliance and none about conflict. It also reduces preparation, because eight well-chosen stories
cover twenty questions.

A coverage matrix looks like this:

| Story | Ownership | Conflict | Failure | Ambiguity | Influence | Safety |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1 failed validation | Strong | — | Strong | — | — | Strong |
| S2 cohort disagreement | — | Strong | — | — | Strong | Strong |
| S3 scope cut | Strong | — | — | Strong | — | — |
| S4 leakage found | Strong | — | — | — | — | Strong |
| S5 mentoring | — | — | — | — | Strong | — |

A column with no strong entry is a gap. Fill it with a new story or re-tell an existing one with a
different emphasis, if the incident genuinely contains that behaviour.

### Step 4. Write each story in six lines

**Input:** the selected incidents.
**Output:** one page per story with the six-line structure and the numbers.
**Rationale:** writing forces the specificity that speech hides. A story that cannot be written in
six lines is usually three stories or none.

Template:

```text
Situation:  [When, where, what was at stake] (1-2 sentences)
Task:       [What I was responsible for] (1 sentence)
Action:     [What I did, with the decision and the alternatives] (3-4 sentences)
Result:     [What happened, with a number] (1-2 sentences)
Insight:    [What I learned about the situation] (1 sentence)
Transfer:   [What I do differently now] (1 sentence)
```

The action must contain a decision, not only activity. "I analysed the data" is activity. "I chose
the stricter cohort definition because the looser one changed the population by 12 percent, and I
brought the count comparison to the meeting" is a decision.

A complete example, at 90 seconds:

> "In my second year at the CRO, we had a Phase II study where the site query rate was three times
> the planning assumption, and the database lock was six weeks away. I was responsible for the query
> plan and the lock. I modelled the query volume by site and found that two new sites accounted for
> most of it. I proposed a targeted training session for those sites rather than a protocol
> amendment, because the amendment would have taken longer than the lock allowed. I ran the session,
> added a weekly query review, and we locked on time with a 2.1 percent open-query rate at lock. The
> lesson was that I had planned from the average site, not the distribution. I now model site-level
> variation before I commit to a query plan."

The story takes about 75 seconds and contains every element: a decision, an alternative considered
and rejected, a number, and a change.

### Step 5. Sharpen ownership language

**Input:** the drafted stories.
**Output:** a version with every "we" replaced by a precise "I" or by an accurate description of
the team.
**Rationale:** interviewers count first-person ownership. A story told entirely in "we" cannot be
scored, because the interviewer cannot tell what you did.

The replacement rules:

- "We decided" becomes "I proposed X; the team chose Y" if that is what happened.
- "We built" becomes "I built the feature pipeline; a colleague built the serving layer."
- "We found" becomes "I noticed the discrepancy when the row count did not match."
- Keep "we" where the team genuinely acted together, and say what your part was.

An answer with no "I" and an answer with only "I" are both weak. The strong version is precise
about both.

### Step 6. Build the failure story with the three-part test

**Input:** one or two failure incidents.
**Output:** a story that passes ownership, specificity, and change.
**Rationale:** the failure question is the most common and the most often failed. The rubric
rewards a candidate who can name their own error and has changed their behaviour.

The three parts:

1. **Ownership.** The failure is a decision you made or a signal you missed, not a circumstance.
2. **Specificity.** The moment you noticed, what you did, and what it cost.
3. **Change.** A rule you now apply, stated concretely enough to be observed.

Weak: "The project failed because the data were delayed and the sponsor kept changing scope."
Strong: "I committed to a delivery date before confirming the data transfer, and I did not build a
buffer for the query cycle. When the transfer slipped three weeks, I had no plan. I told the sponsor
on the day I knew, proposed a phased delivery, and we shipped the primary analysis on time but
dropped two secondary analyses. I now confirm data availability in writing before I give a date,
and I add a two-week buffer for query resolution."

### Step 7. Build the conflict story around interests and escalation

**Input:** one or two conflict incidents.
**Output:** a story with the other party's interest, the evidence you used, the resolution, and the
relationship afterwards.
**Rationale:** the rubric rewards understanding the other position and using a process, not winning
an argument.

Structure:

1. **The disagreement.** What each of you wanted, and why the other person's position was
   reasonable.
2. **The evidence.** What you brought, and what you did not know.
3. **The escalation decision.** Whether you escalated, to whom, and why. If the issue was a safety
   risk, escalation is the right answer even if it damaged the relationship.
4. **The resolution.** What was decided, and who decided it.
5. **The relationship.** What happened next, and what you changed in how you worked together.

The weak version ends at the disagreement. The strong version ends at the relationship.

### Step 7b. Build the integrity and safety story

**Input:** an incident where you raised a concern.
**Output:** a story with the concern, the pressure against raising it, the escalation, and the
outcome.
**Rationale:** biomedical employers weight integrity heavily because the cost of a silenced concern
is measured in patient harm. The story must show that you raised the issue before it was
comfortable to do so.

Structure: what you noticed, why it mattered, what made raising it difficult, who you told, what
they did, and what changed in the process. If the outcome was that nothing changed, say so and say
what you did next; an unanswered escalation is still evidence of the behaviour the rubric wants.

### Step 8. Prepare the motivation questions

**Input:** the role and the employer.
**Output:** a 60-second answer to "why this role" and a 45-second answer to "why are you leaving."
**Rationale:** these are behavioural questions in disguise. They test whether your story is
coherent and whether you will stay.

For "why this role": the employer's problem, the role's decision, your transferable evidence, and
the direction you want to grow. For "why leaving": a forward-looking reason with no disparagement.
"The team is consolidating two analytics functions and the role is being absorbed into a reporting
group; I want to keep building validation work" is specific and neutral.

### Step 9. Rehearse at two lengths and record

**Input:** the written stories.
**Output:** recordings at 90 seconds and 3 minutes, with the structure intact at both lengths.
**Rationale:** the interview will use both. A recruiter screen wants 90 seconds; a hiring manager
may ask for more. Candidates who have only the long version overrun; candidates with only the short
version cannot go deeper when probed.

Score each recording on four dimensions: structure audible, action specific, result quantified,
reflection present. A story that scores below 3 on any dimension is rewritten, not re-rehearsed.

### Step 9b. Score the recording against the rubric

| Dimension | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structure | No audible structure | STAR present but uneven | Structure clear at 90 seconds and 3 minutes |
| Ownership | "We" throughout | "I" appears but is vague | Specific first-person action, accurate team credit |
| Specificity | Traits and generalities | Event described | Named decision, alternative, and number |
| Reflection | No learning | Generic lesson | Specific change, observable in behaviour |

A story scoring below 3 on any dimension is rewritten, not rehearsed again. Rehearsing a weak story
makes it fluent, which makes it harder to notice that it is weak.

### Step 10. Prepare follow-up depth

**Input:** the stories.
**Output:** for each story, three follow-up answers ready.
**Rationale:** the interviewer will probe. The probe is not an attack; it is how the rubric
distinguishes a rehearsed story from a lived one.

The three follow-ups:

1. **"What was your specific contribution?"** Answer with the action in first person and the part
   you did not do.
2. **"What would you do differently?"** Answer with the change you already named, plus one thing
   you would do even now.
3. **"What was the hardest part?"** Answer with the decision, not the workload.

### Step 11. Handle the question you did not prepare

**Input:** an unexpected question.
**Output:** a relevant story told with the six-line structure, plus an honest statement of fit.
**Rationale:** the bank covers competencies, not exact questions. The skill is recognising which
competency the question is testing and selecting the nearest story.

The bridge: "I have not handled exactly that. The closest was X, where the same tension was
present. Let me tell you what I did." Then tell the story. Do not apologise for the gap, and do not
invent an experience.

### Step 12. Debrief within 24 hours

**Input:** the interview.
**Output:** a note with the questions, your stories, the probes, and one change.
**Rationale:** the same competencies recur across employers. The debrief converts one interview
into preparation for the next.

Record: question, competency, story used, the probe you handled least well, and the sentence you
would change. After three loops, the bank is calibrated to the market.

### Decision table: handling the probe

| Probe | What it is testing | Response pattern |
|---|---|---|
| "What was your specific contribution?" | Ownership | Name your action, then name what you did not do |
| "What would you do differently?" | Reflection | Name the change, plus one thing you would still change |
| "Why did that work?" | Causal reasoning | Name the mechanism, not the outcome |
| "Who disagreed with you?" | Conflict awareness | Name the interest, not the person |
| "What did it cost?" | Honesty about trade-offs | Name the cost, including the part that was not worth it |
| "That sounds like a team effort" | Ownership under challenge | Clarify your part without diminishing the team |

### Branch points and edge cases

**No industry experience.** Use incidents from research, teaching, or coordination. A thesis
disagreement with a supervisor is a legitimate conflict story if you describe the interests and the
resolution. Do not apologise for the setting; describe the behaviour.

**A clinical background.** You have conflict and safety stories that most candidates cannot match.
Use them, but translate: a rapid-response escalation is a safety story, and a disagreement about a
protocol is a conflict story. Avoid clinical jargon that the interviewer may not share.

**A gap in employment.** Prepare a 20-second factual answer and a 40-second evidence answer. Do not
volunteer the gap before it is asked, and do not apologise when it is.

**You were dismissed.** Say what happened in one sentence, what you learned, and what you did next.
Do not blame the employer, even if the employer was at fault. The interviewer is assessing how you
handle a difficult account, and blame is the signal they are listening for.

**A safety event.** Describe the event without patient identifiers, name your role and the
escalation, and focus on the system change. If the event is under legal review, say that you can
describe your actions but not the details, and offer the process you followed.

**Confidential work.** Describe the decision and your role, not the client or the data. "A Phase III
oncology programme" is sufficient; the client name is not.

**Neurodivergence or anxiety.** You can ask for the questions in advance or for a written format.
Many employers accommodate; asking is not a disadvantage. Rehearsal reduces but does not remove the
difficulty, and a scripted opening is a legitimate support.

**English as a second language.** Slow down and use shorter sentences. The rubric scores clarity,
not fluency, and a measured pace reads as composure.

### Decision table: which story to use

| Question | Primary competency | Story to select | Emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Tell me about a failure" | Ownership, learning | A decision you made that was wrong | The signal you missed and the rule you now apply |
| "Tell me about a conflict" | Conflict, influence | A disagreement with a stakeholder | Their interest, your evidence, the resolution |
| "Tell me about a time you influenced without authority" | Influence | A decision changed by evidence | The coalition and the framing |
| "Tell me about a time you said no" | Prioritisation, integrity | A scope cut or a refused shortcut | The trade-off and who was protected |
| "Tell me about ambiguity" | Judgment | An undefined problem you scoped | The assumptions and the first test |
| "Tell me about a mistake that affected a patient" | Safety, integrity | A near miss or a process failure | Escalation and system change |

### Decision table: how much to disclose in a failure story

| Situation | Disclose | Summarise | Withhold |
|---|---|---|---|
| Your decision caused a delay | The decision and the consequence | — | — |
| A colleague's error you did not catch | Your miss and the check you added | The colleague's name | Any detail that identifies them |
| A patient safety event | Your role, the escalation, the system change | Clinical detail not needed for the lesson | Identifiers and legal detail |
| A dismissal | The fact and the lesson | The circumstances | Allegations about others |
| A confidential programme | The decision and your role | The domain | Client, compound, or data |

## 6. Worked example

Sam is 34, with eight years as a clinical data manager at a contract research organisation. He is
interviewing for a clinical data science manager role at a pharmaceutical company. The loop includes
a 45-minute behavioural interview with the hiring manager and a 30-minute panel with two
cross-functional peers.

He has prepared ten stories. Six competencies appear in the posting: ownership, cross-functional
influence, developing others, judgment under ambiguity, integrity and safety, and prioritisation.

### 6.1 The coverage matrix before revision

| Story | Ownership | Influence | Developing | Ambiguity | Integrity | Prioritisation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1 database lock delay | Strong | — | — | — | Partial | Strong |
| S2 sponsor scope change | Partial | Strong | — | — | — | Strong |
| S3 mentoring two coordinators | — | — | Strong | — | — | — |
| S4 audit finding | Strong | Partial | — | — | Strong | — |
| S5 new EDC migration | Partial | — | — | Strong | — | Strong |
| S6 conflict with a site | — | Strong | — | — | Partial | — |
| S7 query backlog | Strong | — | — | — | — | Strong |
| S8 unblinding request | — | — | — | — | Strong | — |

Two columns are thin. "Developing others" has one story and it is small. "Judgment under ambiguity"
has one story that is really a project management story. The matrix makes the gap visible before
the interview does.

### 6.2 Diagnostic 1: the failure story blamed the sponsor

His first failure story: "We missed the database lock because the sponsor kept changing the
protocol and the sites were slow to respond." The mock interviewer's note: "I heard what happened to
him, not what he decided."

The diagnostic is specific. The story contains no decision by Sam, no moment of noticing, and no
change. It is a circumstance narrative.

The rewrite:

> "I committed to a database lock date before we had confirmed the query volume from the new sites.
> I assumed the query rate would match the previous study, and it was three times higher. I noticed
> in week two of the query period, when the open-query count was not falling. I told the programme
> lead the same day, proposed a phased lock with the primary endpoints first, and we locked the
> primary analysis on time and the secondary two weeks later. I now model the query volume from the
> site mix before I give a date, and I add a two-week buffer for the first study with a new site
> network."

The rewritten story has a decision ("I committed"), a missed signal ("I assumed"), a moment of
noticing ("week two"), an action, a result, and a change. It is 45 seconds and it is scoreable.

### 6.3 Diagnostic 2: the conflict story had no resolution

His conflict story described a disagreement with a site coordinator about a query. It ended with
"eventually she understood." The mock interviewer asked what was decided, and Sam could not say. The
story had a disagreement and no process.

The rewrite:

> "The coordinator believed the query was a data entry error and wanted to correct it at the site.
> I believed the value was correct and the mapping was wrong. Her interest was avoiding a site
> monitoring visit finding; mine was preserving the source data. I pulled the three previous
> instances of the same lab and showed that the mapping, not the value, had changed. We agreed to
> keep the source value and correct the mapping, and I documented the decision so the next
> coordinator would not reopen it. She raised two more mapping issues with me that quarter, which
> she had not done before."

The rewritten story names both interests, the evidence, the decision, the documentation, and the
relationship afterwards. It also shows a system fix, which is what a manager rubric rewards.

### 6.4 The revised matrix

After rewriting S1 and S6 and adding two stories, S9 on scoping an undefined endpoint definition
and S10 on a training programme, the matrix covers all six competencies with at least one strong
entry:

| Influence | 4 | Full |

The bank is ten stories for six competencies. The redundancy matters: if the interviewer probes a
story and it does not fit, there is a second. He also wrote the two weakest stories at 3 minutes as
well as 90 seconds, because the hiring manager was known to ask for detail.

### 6.5 The interview

Six questions in 45 minutes:

| Question | Story used | What worked | What he would change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tell me about a failure | S1 rewritten | The specific change at the end | Nothing |
| Tell me about a conflict | S6 rewritten | Both interests and the documentation | Shorter setup |
| Tell me about developing someone | S3 | The measurable improvement in the coordinator's query rate | Add what he would do differently |
| Tell me about ambiguity | S9 (new) | The assumptions and the first test | Less detail on the endpoint |

The panel asked two follow-ups. The first was "what was your specific contribution" on S3, and he
answered with the curriculum he designed and the part his manager did. The second was "what would
you do differently" on S9, and he named the stakeholder he did not consult early enough.

### 6.6 Vary one assumption

Change one input: the panel includes a clinician who asks, "Tell me about a time you had to tell a
clinician they were wrong."

The bank has no clinical story. Sam's nearest is S4, the audit finding, where he had to tell a
principal investigator that a source document was incomplete.

The plan changes in three ways:

1. **Reframe S4 for the clinical audience.** Lead with the patient-safety implication, then the
   evidence, then the escalation. The same facts, a different emphasis.
2. **Add one sentence of boundary.** "I have not had to correct a treating clinician; my closest
   equivalent is a principal investigator, where the same evidence and escalation process
   applied." That is honest and it gives the interviewer something to score.
3. **Prepare the escalation answer.** If the clinician had been unwilling, the answer is the
   escalation path: the medical monitor, the safety committee, or the clinical lead, depending on
   the setting.

### 6.7 The probe he fumbled

The panel asked, "You said the training session worked. How do you know it was the training and not
the weekly review you added at the same time?"

Sam's first answer was "the query rate dropped after the session." That is a before-after claim
with two simultaneous interventions. The interviewer pressed, and Sam said he had not separated
them.

The interviewer's note was "claims causation from a two-arm before-after." The honest answer, which
he gave on the second attempt, was better: "I cannot separate them. The
two sites that received the training dropped from 41 percent of open queries to 9 percent; the
other sites, which received only the review, dropped from 22 to 18. That is consistent with the
training mattering more, but it is not a clean comparison, because the sites differed at baseline."

The lesson is not that every behavioural story needs a causal design. It is that claiming more
certainty than the evidence supports is the failure the probe is designed to catch. A candidate who
says "I cannot separate them, and here is what the pattern suggests" scores higher than one who
asserts the training caused the drop.

| Activity | Hours |
|---|---:|
| Rewrite the two weak stories | 2.0 |
| Rehearse all ten at 90 seconds with a stranger | 1.5 |
| Prepare the motivation answers | 0.5 |
| Prepare follow-up depth for the three most likely stories | 1.0 |
| **Total** | **5.0** |

With five hours he would not add stories. The gap was quality in two stories and fluency across
the bank, not coverage. Adding a story would have produced an eleventh mediocre answer.

### 6.9 The question he did not prepare

The panel asked, "Tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news to a client." Sam had no story
for it, because his bank covered internal conflict and failure but not client communication.

He used the bridge: "I have not had to deliver a trial failure to a sponsor. The closest was telling
a programme lead that the database lock would slip by two weeks because of a query volume we had
not modelled. I told them the day I knew, with a revised plan and the two analyses I proposed to
defer. They accepted the delay because the plan was specific."

The answer worked because the behaviour transferred: early disclosure, a specific plan, and a
trade-off. The lesson is that the bridge is a skill in its own right, and it should be rehearsed
with the same discipline as the stories.

### 6.10 The general lesson

The bank is indexed by competency, not by profession. A story about an investigator can answer a
question about a clinician if the behaviour, the evidence, and the escalation path are the same,
and the honest boundary sentence is stronger than a fabricated clinical example.

## 7. Code, query, or calculation

The script implements the coverage matrix and the minimal covering set from Section 5, Step 3. It
takes ten stories tagged with the competencies they strongly evidence, reports coverage and gaps,
and selects the smallest set of stories that covers every competency.

Assumptions:

- Tags are assigned by the candidate and represent strong evidence, not a mention. A story that
  contains a conflict but does not show a resolution is not tagged "conflict."
- The competency list is the six used in Section 6. A real employer's framework may have eight or
  twelve; replace the list.
- Greedy set cover is not guaranteed to find the smallest possible set, but it is close and it is
  easy to explain. For ten stories, the difference does not matter.

```python
"""Story bank coverage and selection, standard library only.

Run: python story_bank.py
Given stories tagged with the competencies they evidence strongly, reports the
coverage of each competency, identifies gaps, and selects a minimal subset of
stories that covers every competency (greedy set cover).
"""

COMPETENCIES = [
    "ownership",
    "influence",
    "developing_others",
    "ambiguity",
    "integrity",
    "prioritisation",
]

STORIES = {
    "S1 database lock delay": ["ownership", "prioritisation"],
    "S2 sponsor scope change": ["influence", "prioritisation"],
    "S3 mentoring two coordinators": ["developing_others"],
    "S4 audit finding": ["ownership", "integrity"],
    "S5 EDC migration": ["ambiguity", "prioritisation"],
    "S6 site conflict": ["influence"],
    "S7 query backlog": ["ownership", "prioritisation"],
    "S8 unblinding request": ["integrity"],
    "S9 endpoint definition": ["ambiguity", "influence"],
    "S10 training programme": ["developing_others", "influence"],
}


def coverage(stories, competencies):
    counts = {c: 0 for c in competencies}
    for tags in stories.values():
        for tag in tags:
            if tag in counts:
                counts[tag] += 1
    return counts


def greedy_cover(stories, competencies):
    uncovered = set(competencies)
    chosen = []
    while uncovered:
        best_name, best_gain = None, set()
        for name, tags in stories.items():
            gain = set(tags) & uncovered
            if len(gain) > len(best_gain):
                best_name, best_gain = name, gain
        if not best_gain:
            break
        chosen.append((best_name, sorted(best_gain)))
        uncovered -= best_gain
    return chosen, uncovered


def main():
    print("COVERAGE")
    counts = coverage(STORIES, COMPETENCIES)
    for comp in COMPETENCIES:
        bar = "#" * counts[comp]
        print(f"  {comp:18s} {counts[comp]:2d} {bar}")
    gaps = [c for c, n in counts.items() if n == 0]
    print(f"  gaps: {', '.join(gaps) if gaps else 'none'}")
    print(f"  stories: {len(STORIES)}")
    print(f"  mean tags per story: "
          f"{sum(len(v) for v in STORIES.values()) / len(STORIES):.1f}")

    chosen, uncovered = greedy_cover(STORIES, COMPETENCIES)
    print()
    print("MINIMAL COVERING SET (greedy)")
    for name, gain in chosen:
        print(f"  {name:32s} covers {', '.join(gain)}")
    print(f"  stories selected: {len(chosen)} of {len(STORIES)}")
    if uncovered:
        print(f"  UNCOVERED: {', '.join(sorted(uncovered))}")
    else:
        print("  all competencies covered")

    print()
    print("REDUNDANCY")
    for comp in COMPETENCIES:
        print(f"  {comp:18s} {counts[comp]} stories "
              f"({'thin' if counts[comp] < 2 else 'redundant'})")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

Expected output, confirmed by running the script:

```text
COVERAGE
  ownership           3 ###
  influence           4 ####
  developing_others   2 ##
  ambiguity           2 ##
  integrity           2 ##
  prioritisation      4 ####
  gaps: none
  stories: 10
  mean tags per story: 1.7

MINIMAL COVERING SET (greedy)
  S1 database lock delay           covers ownership, prioritisation
  S9 endpoint definition           covers ambiguity, influence
  S3 mentoring two coordinators    covers developing_others
  S4 audit finding                 covers integrity
  stories selected: 4 of 10
  all competencies covered

REDUNDANCY
  ownership          3 stories (redundant)
  influence          4 stories (redundant)
  developing_others  2 stories (redundant)
  ambiguity          2 stories (redundant)
  integrity          2 stories (redundant)
  prioritisation     4 stories (redundant)
```

Reading the output:

1. **Four stories cover all six competencies.** The minimum is smaller than most candidates expect.
   The reason to prepare ten is not coverage; it is the follow-up probe. If the interviewer asks a
   second question about the same competency and your only story does not fit, you need another.
2. **Mean tags per story is 1.7.** A well-chosen incident usually evidences two competencies. If
   your mean is below 1.2, your stories are too narrow; if it is above 2.5, your tags are probably
   generous.
3. **Redundancy is the insurance.** Two stories per competency is the minimum for a real loop, and
   the script flags anything below two as thin. In Section 6, the two thin columns were the reason
   to add S9 and S10.
4. **The greedy set is not the interview plan.** The plan is the story that best fits the question
   and the interviewer's role. The set tells you which stories you cannot afford to remove.
5. **The redundancy count is a preparation target, not a quality score.** A competency with four
   stories is not four times better covered; it means you have options if a probe goes badly. Two
   is the working minimum.
6. **The output exposes false coverage.** A story tagged "integrity" that never shows a moment of
   pressure is covering the label, not the competency. Re-tag it and the gap becomes visible.

To use the script, replace the competency list with the employer's framework and retag your
stories. Re-run before each loop; the gaps change with the role.

## 8. Data, standards, and regulatory context

Behavioural interviews are governed by employment law rather than by technical standards. The rules
determine what an employer may ask, how answers may be used, and what verification is permitted
after the interview. A candidate who knows them can recognise an improper question and can prepare
for the checks that follow an offer.

### 8.1 What is binding

| Rule | Jurisdiction | What it constrains |
|---|---|---|
| Title VII of the Civil Rights Act | United States | Questions or selection criteria that disproportionately exclude protected groups |
| Americans with Disabilities Act | United States | Pre-offer inquiries about disability; accommodation process |
| Age Discrimination in Employment Act | United States | Questions that proxy for age |
| Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures | United States | Validation and adverse-impact analysis for selection procedures |
| Equality Act 2010 | United Kingdom | Questions about protected characteristics, including health and family plans |
| GDPR and UK GDPR | European Union and United Kingdom | Processing of interview notes and recordings; retention |
| Fair Credit Reporting Act | United States | Background checks conducted by third parties |

The Uniform Guidelines treat interviews, including informal and unscored ones, as selection
procedures, and the four-fifths rule is used as evidence of adverse impact. Behavioural questions
are lawful when they are job-related and applied consistently; a question about family plans or
health is not made lawful by asking it of every candidate.

### 8.2 What is convention

- **STAR** is a convention, not a requirement. It is widely taught because it produces scorable
  answers, and interviewers recognise it.
- **A fixed set of competencies** is a convention. Some employers use a published framework, some
  use the job description, and some use an unstructured conversation.
- **Reference checks** are a convention with legal constraints. Many employers now give only dates
  and titles; a candidate should ask referees what they will say.
- **Recording** an interview requires consent in many jurisdictions. A candidate may ask whether a
  session is recorded.

### 8.3 What the rules do not do

They do not make the interview fair, and they do not give a candidate a remedy for a subjective
judgment. They constrain what can be asked and how a selection process must be justified if its
outcomes show adverse impact. The practical value is narrower: you do not have to answer questions
about protected characteristics, you may request an accommodation, and a process that ignores these
rules is a risk signal about the employer.

### 8.4 What an employer may verify after the interview

| Check | What it verifies | Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Reference check | Employment dates, title, sometimes performance | Many employers give only dates and titles |
| Background check | Criminal record, employment, education | Requires disclosure and authorisation under the FCRA in the US |
| Degree verification | Institution and award | Requires consent in some jurisdictions |
| Licence verification | Professional registration | Public register in most jurisdictions |
| Right-to-work check | Legal authorisation | Required before employment in the UK; I-9 in the US |

A behavioural story that cannot survive a reference check is a liability. The most common failure is
not fabrication but inflation: a contribution described as ownership, or a team result described as
individual. The check may not catch it, but the interviewer's probe often does.

### 8.5 Professional and ethical codes

Clinical and research employers may also assess conduct against professional codes: honesty in
reporting, safeguarding of participants, conflicts of interest, and data integrity. A behavioural
story about a safety concern or a data integrity issue is assessed against those expectations, not
only against a generic competency rubric. If your story involves a regulated activity, describe the
process you followed, not only the outcome.

## 9. Industry practice and case evidence

### 9.1 Documented: past-behaviour questions are among the better-validated formats

Taylor and Small (2002) meta-analysed situational and past-behaviour interview questions. With
descriptively anchored rating scales, past-behaviour questions had a mean validity of .63, against
.47 for situational questions; inter-rater reliabilities were similar at about .79 and .77.

Wingate, Bourdage and Steel (2024) meta-analysed 37 studies with 30,646 participants and reported
an overall interview validity of .28 corrected for criterion unreliability, with structured,
question-based scoring improving validity, particularly for contextual performance. They describe
past-behaviour questions as resting on behavioural consistency theory.

Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens (2022) ranked the structured interview as the top selection
procedure in their revised estimates, with an operational validity of .42. The absolute numbers
vary across meta-analyses and corrections; the consistent finding is that structure and
behaviour-based questions outperform unstructured conversation.

### 9.2 Documented: large employers publish the method

Amazon publishes guidance on its Leadership Principles interview, recommending the STAR format,
multiple examples per principle, first-person ownership ("I" not "we"), quantified results, and
inclusion of failures. Its careers material states that candidates should prepare at least two
examples per principle and that interviews typically assess about two principles each.

The guidance is employer-specific and should not be generalised to every company. It is useful as
evidence that the method is standardised at large employers and that the behaviours assessed are
defined in advance.

### 9.3 Documented: the questions are predictable

Public career services at universities and governments publish question banks for behavioural
interviews, typically organised around communication, teamwork, problem-solving, conflict, and
failure. The State of Oregon's guidance, for example, lists about 25 common questions and advises
specific, first-person answers with results volunteered.

The predictability is the point: a candidate who prepares eight stories covers most of the bank.

### 9.4 What the published guidance has in common

Across the university career services, the UK National Careers Service, the State of Oregon, and
Amazon's own material, four recommendations recur: use a structured format, focus on your own
actions, quantify the result, and include what you learned. The consistency across sources with
different audiences is itself evidence that the format is a convention rather than one company's
preference.

The differences are also instructive. Amazon's guidance emphasises multiple examples per principle
and the "I" not "we" rule, because its interviewers assess defined principles and discount
team-level accounts. University guidance emphasises brevity and conversational delivery, because
student candidates tend to over-script. Both are addressing the same failure from different sides.

### 9.5 Inference, labelled

- **Inference:** behavioural stages carry more weight in biomedical hiring than their duration
  suggests, because they are the only stage that assesses safety and integrity directly.
- **Inference:** the failure and conflict questions are weighted most heavily for manager roles,
  because they predict how the candidate will handle a team in difficulty.
- **Inference:** interviewers discount stories that are entirely team-based, because they cannot
  score the candidate's contribution.
- **Inference:** a candidate who volunteers a specific change after a failure is read as more
  senior than one who describes a larger success.
- **Inference:** stories older than about five years are discounted, not because the behaviour
  expired but because the interviewer cannot tell whether it is still current. Refresh the bank
  after each role.

> **Note:** No pass rate or proportion-of-decision figure is quoted. The validity coefficients above
> describe selection procedures in general, not the probability of passing a particular interview.

## 10. Failure modes and red flags

| Failure | Early warning sign | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Team story | "We decided" throughout | Habit of collaborative language | Interviewer cannot score contribution | Rewrite with precise "I" and accurate team credit |
| No decision | The story describes activity | Tasks were mistaken for actions | Reads as junior | Name the choice and the alternative |
| No result | The story ends at the action | Result felt obvious to the candidate | No evidence of effect | Add a number or a concrete outcome |
| No reflection | The lesson is "communication is important" | Reflection rehearsed as a slogan | No evidence of learning | Name the change you now make |
| Blame story | The failure belongs to someone else | Fear of admitting error | Inferred low ownership | Choose a failure that is yours |
| Conflict without resolution | "Eventually she understood" | The disagreement was never closed | Inferred avoidance | Describe the decision and the relationship |
| Over-rehearsed delivery | The answer sounds recited | Script memorised word for word | Discounted as inauthentic | Rehearse structure, not sentences |
| Five-minute answer | The interviewer interrupts | No time discipline | Structure lost, scoring abandoned | Practise at 90 seconds |
| Fabrication | Details change between tellings | Embellishment to fit a rubric | Reference check or probe exposes it | Use only true stories |
| Ignoring the panel | You answer only the lead interviewer | Nerves | Other panellists score disengagement | Include the room |
| No questions | You ask about benefits only | Preparation focused on answers | Read as passive | Prepare three questions |
| Defensive under probe | You repeat the story louder | Probe experienced as attack | Low coachability score | Acknowledge, clarify, answer the underlying question |
| Confidential detail | Client name or patient detail appears | Enthusiasm to show scale | Governance breach | Describe the class, not the identifier |
| One story for every question | The same incident answers conflict, failure, and ambiguity | Convenience | Interviewer sees a narrow history | Map at least two stories per competency |
| Motivation answer unprepared | "Why this role" is vague | Focus on stories | Inferred flight risk | Prepare a 60-second employer-specific answer |
| Story too old | The example is from eight years ago | No recent incidents collected | Inferred that nothing recent qualifies | Refresh the bank after each role |

Two red flags are about the employer. A behavioural stage with no defined competencies and no
consistent questions is likely to be scored on impression, which disadvantages candidates who do
not resemble the interviewer. A question about family plans, health, or age is improper in most
jurisdictions; you are not obliged to answer, and you may end the process.

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why it is needed | What breaks it | How to test it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The employer uses a competency rubric | It justifies the coverage matrix | Some interviews are unstructured | Ask what the stage assesses |
| Eight to ten stories cover most questions | It bounds preparation | Unusual frameworks with twelve competencies | Map the framework before writing |
| Past behaviour predicts future behaviour | It justifies the format | People change contexts and roles | Treat stories as evidence, not prophecy |
| 90 seconds is the right default length | It fits most stages | Some panels allow longer answers | Ask the interviewer how much detail they want |
| A specific change beats a large success | It reflects rubric weighting | Some cultures reward scale | Watch the interviewer's follow-ups |
| Probes are tests, not attacks | It prevents defensiveness | Some interviewers are hostile | Answer the underlying question, then stop |
| The same story can serve two competencies | It reduces preparation | The emphases conflict | Tell it once per competency in practice and compare |
| Confidential work can be described generically | It enables stories from regulated roles | The detail is the evidence | Prepare the generic version with your manager |
| Reflection can be learned | It justifies the rewriting | Some incidents genuinely taught nothing | Choose a different incident |
| The interviewer wants a long answer | It justifies the 3-minute version | Some panels prefer brevity | Ask how much detail they want, or watch for interruption |
| The story needs a villain | Your account makes someone else look bad | Conflict framed as a contest | Inferred lack of empathy | Describe the interest, not the person |

The main limit is that the behavioural stage measures presentation as well as behaviour. A
candidate who has done the work but cannot narrate it will score below one who narrates well. That
is unfair and it is the format. The response is not to invent stories but to practise the true ones
until the evidence is audible.

## 12. Exercises

**Exercise 1. Competency decode (recall, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: six competencies from one posting, each with the behaviour the rubric likely rewards.
Acceptance criteria: each competency is phrased as an observable behaviour; at least one is inferred
from a responsibility rather than a requirement.

**Exercise 2. Eight stories (application, 2 hours).**
Deliverable: eight stories in the six-line structure, each with a number and a specific change.
Acceptance criteria: every story has a decision, a result, and a change; no story is told entirely
in "we"; each fits on one page.

**Exercise 3. Coverage matrix (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a matrix of stories by competencies with strong, partial, or blank entries.
Acceptance criteria: every competency has at least one strong entry or a named gap; no competency
relies on a single story for a role with six or more questions.

**Exercise 4. Two lengths (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: recordings of three stories at 90 seconds and 3 minutes.
Acceptance criteria: structure audible at both lengths; action is the longest section; the
90-second version is within 15 seconds of target.

**Exercise 5. Failure story (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: one failure story that passes ownership, specificity, and change, plus a one-line
answer to "what would you do differently."
Acceptance criteria: the failure is the candidate's decision; the moment of noticing is named; the
change is observable.

**Exercise 6. Conflict story (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: one conflict story with both interests, the evidence, the escalation decision, the
resolution, and the relationship afterwards.
Acceptance criteria: the other party's position is described as reasonable; the resolution names
who decided; the relationship outcome is stated.

**Exercise 7. Probe drill (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a recording of three probes answered without repeating the original story.
Acceptance criteria: each answer adds information; none becomes defensive; the ownership answer
distinguishes your part from the team's.

**Exercise 8. New-employer adaptation (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: the bank remapped to a second employer's competencies, with two new emphasis notes.
Acceptance criteria: every competency covered; at least one story re-emphasised for a different
competency; the changes are recorded in one page.

## 13. Self-check answers

**1. What is a behavioural question testing?**
A disposition the employer has defined as a competency, evidenced by a specific past action in a
specific situation. The story is the vehicle; the behaviour is the payload.

**2. What are the six lines of a strong answer?**
Situation, task, action, result, insight, transfer. The action is the longest and must contain a
decision.

**3. Why map stories to competencies before writing?**
Because the interviewer scores against a rubric. Writing first produces stories about what you find
interesting rather than what the role assesses.

**4. How long should a behavioural answer be?**
90 seconds by default, with a 3-minute version available if the interviewer asks for detail. The
action should take about half the time.

**5. What makes a failure story strong?**
The failure is your decision, the moment you noticed is named, the consequence is stated, and the
change is specific enough to be observed in your behaviour afterwards.

**6. What makes a conflict story strong?**
Both interests are described as reasonable, the evidence is named, the escalation decision is
explained, the resolution names who decided, and the relationship afterwards is stated.

**7. How do you answer a probe without repeating the story?**
Add information: the specific contribution, the alternative you rejected, the cost, or what you
would still change. Repeating the original answer signals that there is nothing underneath it.

**8. What do you do if you have no story for a competency?**
Say so, offer the nearest equivalent, and describe how you would approach the situation. Do not
invent a story; the probe will expose it.

**Rubric for Exercise 3.** Full marks: every competency has a strong entry or a named gap; no
competency depends on one story for a six-question loop; at least one story carries two
competencies; the gaps have a plan.

**Rubric for Exercise 4.** Full marks: three stories at both lengths; structure audible at both;
action is the longest section; the 90-second version is within 15 seconds; the 3-minute version adds
detail rather than repeating.

**Rubric for Exercise 5.** Full marks: the failure is the candidate's decision; the signal missed
is named; the consequence is stated; the change is observable; the story is under 2 minutes.

**Rubric for Exercise 8.** Full marks: every competency mapped; at least one story re-emphasised;
the new emphasis is written as an opening sentence; the remap takes under an hour; no story is
invented to fill a gap.

**Rubric for Exercise 6.** Full marks: both interests; evidence rather than authority; an explicit
escalation decision; a named resolution; the relationship outcome; no blame.

## 14. Interview questions

**1. "Tell me about a time you failed."**
Strong: your decision, the missed signal, the consequence, the change. Weak: a circumstance story.

**2. "Tell me about a conflict with a clinician."**
Strong: both interests, evidence, escalation, resolution. Weak: "we agreed to disagree."

**3. "Tell me about a time you influenced without authority."**
Strong: the coalition, the framing, the decision changed. Weak: "I convinced them."

**4. "Tell me about a time you had to say no."**
Strong: the trade-off, who was protected, what was cut. Weak: says they never say no.

**5. "Tell me about a mistake that affected a patient."**
Strong: the error, the disclosure, the system change. Weak: minimises or hides behind process.

**6. "Tell me about a time you worked in ambiguity."**
Strong: how you scoped the problem and what you tested first. Weak: "I figured it out."

**7. "Tell me about developing someone."**
Strong: what you taught, how you measured it, what they can do now. Weak: "I mentored juniors."

**8. "Why are you leaving your current role?"**
Strong: a forward-looking reason, no disparagement. Weak: complaints about a manager.

**9. "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager."**
Strong: the issue, how you raised it, what happened, the relationship afterwards. Weak: claims
never to disagree.

**10. "Tell me about a time you delivered bad news."**
Strong: early disclosure, a specific plan, a trade-off, the relationship afterwards. Weak: waited
until the news was unavoidable.

**11. "How do you handle a question you have no story for?"**
Strong: names the nearest equivalent, states the boundary, and describes how they would approach
it. Weak: improvises a story that does not fit.

**12. "What would your colleagues say about you?"**
Strong: a specific behaviour with an example, including one they found difficult. Weak: adjectives
only.

## 15. Key terms

- **Accountability** — the obligation to answer for a decision.
- **Anchored rating scale** — a scale with behavioural anchors per level.
- **Behavioural consistency** — past behaviour predicts future behaviour.
- **Behavioural question** — a question about what you did in a past situation.
- **Blameless review** — incident review focused on system causes.
- **Competency** — a defined behaviour or skill assessed by the employer.
- **Conflict** — a disagreement about goals, methods, or priorities.
- **Credit sharing** — naming others' contributions accurately.
- **Escalation** — raising an issue to someone with authority.
- **Evidence** — specific action, number, or artifact supporting a claim.
- **Failure** — an outcome that did not meet the goal.
- **Follow-up probe** — a question asking for more detail on a claim.
- **Fabrication** — inventing or embellishing a story.
- **Influence without authority** — changing a decision without formal power.
- **Insight** — what you learned about the situation or yourself.
- **Near miss** — an event that could have caused harm but did not.
- **Ownership** — answering for your part in an outcome.
- **Psychological safety** — the belief that speaking up is safe.
- **Reflection** — what you learned and changed.
- **Resolution** — a decision or process that ends a disagreement.
- **Root cause** — the underlying reason an event occurred.
- **Situational question** — a hypothetical scenario question.
- **Speak-up** — raising a concern about safety or conduct.
- **STAR** — situation, task, action, result.
- **STARI** — STAR plus improvement.
- **Transfer** — applying the learning to a new context.
