## 1. Why this topic exists

A resume is not a record of what you did. It is a screening instrument read by two people with
different questions, in sequence, under time pressure. A recruiter asks whether you match the
requisition well enough to forward. A hiring manager asks whether you can own the decision the
role carries. Both read the top third of the first page first, and both stop when the evidence
does not appear.

The translation problem is specific to regulated biomedical work. A clinical research
coordinator's actual output is a clean database and a passed monitoring visit, but the resume
line says "managed study documents." A postdoc's actual output is a validated pipeline that
three other labs now use, but the resume line says "performed bioinformatics analysis." A
pharmacovigilance analyst's actual output is a signal assessment that changed a label, but the
resume line says "conducted case reviews." In each case the work is strong and the claim is
invisible. The reader cannot infer what was not written.

### 1.1 The two readers

**The recruiter** is screening against a requisition, often across several roles, and usually
has 30 to 90 seconds per resume. They look for title match, years, location, work authorisation,
and a small number of required skills. They are not evaluating your statistics. Their risk is
forwarding a candidate who will be rejected by the hiring manager.

**The hiring manager** reads a shorter stack, more carefully, and is evaluating risk. Can this
person do the work in the first 90 days? Will they need supervision I cannot provide? Do they
understand the domain well enough to be trusted with clinical or regulatory stakeholders? They
read for ownership verbs, scale, and outcomes, and they discount adjectives.

A resume that satisfies the recruiter and fails the hiring manager is common: it is a keyword-
dense list of tools with no evidence of judgment. A resume that satisfies the manager and fails
the recruiter is also common: it is a dense technical narrative with no title match and no
location line. The document has to satisfy both, which is why the ordering of information
matters more than the wording of any single bullet.

### 1.2 Why the seven-second finding matters, and what it does not prove

A widely cited vendor study by Ladders (2018) used eye-tracking to estimate that recruiters spend
about 7.4 seconds on an initial resume screen, up from about 6 seconds in the company's 2012
study. The study is a vendor marketing asset with a small sample recruited for the exercise, and
it measures an initial screen rather than a hiring decision. It should not be treated as a
precise benchmark. It is useful for one design conclusion: the first screen is a pattern-matching
task, so the pattern has to be legible without reading.

The pattern includes a clear title line, a recent role with a recognisable employer and dates, a
short summary that names the target role, and bullets whose first four words carry the claim.
Everything else is read only if the pattern matches.

### 1.3 Who this blocks

**Candidates from academia** write for reviewers who will read the whole document. They lead
with publications, use passive constructions, and describe methods rather than decisions. Their
resume reads as a CV, which signals "still an academic" to a recruiter even when the skills fit.

**Candidates from clinical roles** undersell operational evidence. A nurse who built a
departmental audit that changed a handoff protocol has evidence of influencing clinical
practice, which is exactly what a clinical informatics role wants. The resume says "participated
in quality improvement."

**Career changers from outside health** over-claim domain fluency and under-claim transferable
evidence. They write "passionate about healthcare" and omit the operations analytics that would
actually transfer.

**International candidates** face format expectations that differ by country (a two-page CV is
normal in much of Europe; a one-page resume is expected in the United States), plus work
authorisation statements that must be accurate and are frequently mishandled.

### 1.4 What this topic produces

By the end of Section 5 you will have a one- or two-page resume with a target-role summary, six
to ten rewritten bullets, a projects section built from portfolio artifacts, a skills section
that is defensible in an interview, and a LinkedIn profile whose headline, about section, and
experience entries use the same evidence in a searchable form. You will also have a keyword
audit against three real postings and a two-person readability test.

It does not cover cover letters, which are increasingly optional and are covered briefly in
Section 4, or interview performance, which is Topics 04 to 08. It does not cover salary
negotiation (Topic 09). It does not claim that any wording guarantees a callback; the evidence
on resume interventions is thin and mostly vendor-produced.

### 1.5 The honesty constraint

Every claim on a resume is a claim you will be asked to defend, and some are verifiable. Dates,
titles, degrees, licences, and certifications are checked by background screeners. Publications
are checked by DOI. Metrics are checked by asking you to explain the denominator. A resume that
inflates is not a clever optimisation; it is a liability that surfaces after the offer, when the
cost of withdrawing is highest.

The rule used throughout this topic: every bullet must survive three questions. What was the
decision? What did you personally do? How would you know the number is right?

## 3. The core mental model

A resume is a stack of claims ordered by expected reader attention, and every claim must be
traceable to evidence you can produce on request. The document is not a summary of your history;
it is a curated set of proofs for the role in front of you.

```text
  READER PATH (top third of page 1)

  [ Name | target-role line | location | contact ]
        |
        v
  [ Summary: 2-3 lines, role + evidence + domain ]
        |
        v
  [ Most recent role: title, employer, dates, 3-5 bullets ]
        |
        v
  [ Earlier roles: fewer bullets, older = shorter ]
        |
        v
  [ Projects: portfolio artifacts, one line each ]
        |
        v
  [ Skills: only what you can defend ]
        |
        v
  [ Education, licences, publications ]
```

### 3.1 The evidence ladder

Each bullet sits somewhere on a ladder from activity to consequence. The higher the rung, the
more it survives screening.

| Rung | Form | Example | Reader inference |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Activity | "Responsible for data cleaning" | Can follow instructions |
| 2 | Method | "Cleaned data using Python and SQL" | Has used tools |
| 3 | Output | "Built a cleaning pipeline for 14 sites" | Delivers artifacts |
| 4 | Metric | "Reduced query rate from 8% to 2%" | Measures work |
| 5 | Decision | "Changed site inclusion criteria based on the query pattern" | Owns judgment |
| 6 | Consequence | "Cut database lock time by three weeks across two studies" | Affects the organisation |

Most biomedical resumes sit at rungs 1 to 3. The candidates who get interviews sit at 4 to 6,
and they keep rungs 5 and 6 for the two or three bullets that matter most. A page of rung-6
claims reads as inflation; a page of rung-2 claims reads as junior.

### 3.2 Two dialects, one document

The same work is described differently by different functions.

| Work | Academic dialect | Industry dialect |
|---|---|---|
| Analysed trial data | "Conducted statistical analysis of longitudinal outcomes" | "Owned the primary analysis for a 640-patient Phase II trial; the result supported the endpoint selection for Phase III" |
| Built a model | "Developed a machine learning model achieving 0.87 AUROC" | "Built a readmission model that improved AUPRC from 0.21 to 0.29 over the incumbent screen; validated on the most recent 12 months" |
| Managed data | "Maintained the study database" | "Owned data quality for a 14-site registry; query rate at lock was 1.8%, with zero critical findings at inspection" |
| Wrote a protocol | "Contributed to study design" | "Drafted the estimand and sensitivity analysis section of the SAP for a 1,200-patient study" |

The industry dialect is not more impressive; it is more specific. Specificity is what survives a
seven-second read.

### 3.3 What the document is competing with

For any given posting, the shortlist is built from three pools: referrals, recruiter-sourced
candidates, and inbound applicants. Inbound is the largest and least differentiated pool. A
resume can move you within the inbound pool; it cannot beat a referral on its own. That is why
Topic 01's targeting and this topic's positioning are complementary: the resume is necessary and
insufficient.

### 3.4 LinkedIn is a search index, not a poster

LinkedIn works differently from a resume because the first reader is often a search query, not a
person. Recruiters filter by title, location, current employer, skills, and keywords, then read
the headline and the first two lines of the current role. The profile therefore needs the terms
a recruiter would search for in the places the search indexes: headline, job titles, skills, and
the first lines of the about section.

This is why the same evidence is written twice. The resume is optimised for a human reading
top-down in seven seconds. The profile is optimised for a Boolean search and then a human
reading the first two lines. The claims must be identical; the packaging differs.

> **Note:** Keyword matching is a discovery mechanism, not a persuasion mechanism. A profile
> stuffed with every tool in the field ranks in searches and converts poorly, because the human
> who opens it finds no evidence. Match the vocabulary, then prove two or three claims.

## 4. Definitions and vocabulary

| Term | Definition | Common misuse | Unit or coding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Applicant tracking system (ATS) | Software that receives, parses, and filters applications | Treated as an automatic rejection engine | System |
| Parsing | Extraction of fields from a document into structured data | Assumed to be perfect for every format | Process |
| Keyword | A term a recruiter or ATS searches for | Stuffed into white text or a hidden section | Term |
| Boolean search | Recruiter query using AND, OR, NOT, and quoted phrases | Confused with keyword frequency | Query |
| Requisition | An approved, funded opening | Confused with a posted ad | Count |
| Shortlist | Candidates forwarded to the hiring manager | Confused with the interview list | Count |
| Sourcing | Proactive identification of candidates by recruiters | Confused with inbound applications | Activity |
| Referral | A candidate introduced by an employee | Assumed to guarantee an interview | Channel |
| Achievement bullet | A statement of action, method, metric, and consequence | Written as a job description | Bullet |
| Responsibility statement | A description of duties | Used as the default bullet form | Bullet |
| Scope | The size of what you influenced: patients, sites, budget, team | Omitted, leaving scale unknown | Number |
| Quantification | A number attached to an outcome or scale | Applied to inputs rather than results | Number |
| Denominator | The base against which a percentage is computed | Left unstated, making the metric unverifiable | Definition |
| Reverse chronological | Ordering roles from most to least recent | Abandoned in favour of a functional layout | Format |
| Functional resume | A layout organised by skill rather than chronology | Used to hide gaps, which it highlights | Format |
| Curriculum vitae (CV) | An exhaustive academic record | Used for industry applications in the US | Document |
| Summary | Two to three lines naming the target role and the core evidence | Written as an objective statement | Lines |
| Objective statement | A statement of what the candidate wants | Considered self-centred by most readers | Lines |
| Headline | The line under a LinkedIn name | Left as the default job title | Text |
| About section | The narrative section of a LinkedIn profile | Used for a life story | Paragraphs |
| Open to work | A LinkedIn setting that signals availability | Assumed to be private when public | Setting |
| Skills section | A list of competencies, ideally endorsed | Used as a synonym for tools | List |
| Endorsement | A connection's confirmation of a skill | Treated as evidence of proficiency | Count |
| Title normalisation | Restating an internal title in market language | Inflating a title to match a target | Text |
| Employment gap | A period without employment | Assumed to require an excuse | Months |
| Background check | Verification of employment, education, and criminal record | Confused with a reference check | Process |
| Right to work | Legal authorisation to work in a jurisdiction | Confused with citizenship | Status |
| Redaction | Removal of confidential details from a work sample | Applied inconsistently | Process |
| ORCID | A persistent identifier for researchers | Omitted by candidates with publications | Identifier |
| DOI | A persistent identifier for a publication or dataset | Quoted without the article title | Identifier |
