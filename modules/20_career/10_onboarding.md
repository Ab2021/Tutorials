## 1. Why this topic exists

The hiring decision is a prediction, and onboarding is where the prediction is tested. The first 90
days determine whether the new hire becomes the person who owns the analysis, or the person who
leaves in month seven. The difference is rarely ability. It is whether they found the data, the
people, and the decision in time to contribute something real.

Most onboarding advice is written for the employer. This topic is written for the new hire, because
the new hire is the person with the least information and the most to lose. A manager may have a
plan; many do not. A structured 90-day plan is a way to take responsibility for your own ramp without
waiting for the organisation to provide one.

### 1.1 What goes wrong

Four failure patterns account for most slow starts.

**No data access.** The hire spends three weeks waiting for credentials, then discovers the extract
they need is governed by a committee that meets monthly. The first deliverable slips, and the
manager's confidence slips with it.

**No stakeholder map.** The hire builds a useful analysis for the wrong person. The medical director
who sponsored the role never sees it, and the analyst who needed it has already moved on.

**Premature delivery.** The hire ships a model in week three to demonstrate value, using a cohort
definition no one reviewed. The numbers are wrong, and the correction costs more credibility than
the delay would have.

**No defined success.** Nobody wrote down what a good first 90 days looks like. The hire optimises
for visible activity, the manager evaluates against an unstated expectation, and the review in month
four is a surprise to both.

### 1.2 Why the first 90 days are different

Three properties make the period unusual.

- **You have credit and no evidence.** The organisation has just chosen you, and it will extend
  goodwill for a short period. That goodwill is spent by errors and earned by reliability.
- **You cannot yet tell what matters.** The formal org chart, the data dictionary, and the job
  description are all incomplete. The real map is held by people, and it takes weeks to extract.
- **Your first impression is forming while you are least competent.** The people you meet in week
  one will remember the questions you asked. Good questions buy patience; confident wrong answers
  spend it.

### 1.3 The cost

The Mellon Financial research reported in MIT Sloan Management Review estimated that lost
productivity from the learning curve costs 1 to 2.5 percent of total revenue, and that average
time-to-full-productivity is about 8 weeks for clerical roles, 20 weeks for professionals, and 26
weeks or more for executives. The figures are old and come from one company's analysis, but the
pattern is consistent with what managers report: a professional role takes months, not weeks, to
reach full output.

The implication for a new hire is not to rush. It is to shorten the period in which you are
unproductive by finding the data, the people, and the decision early, and to make your early output
visible enough that the organisation can see the ramp working.

### 1.4 What this topic produces

A 90-day plan with weekly milestones, three maps (data, stakeholders, decisions), a quick-win
selection method, a review script for day 90, and a worked example with the arithmetic of a real
ramp. It also covers the first 30 days in a regulated environment, where access and training are
gated.

### 1.5 The first 90 days are a second interview

The organisation made a prediction and is now testing it with real work. The difference from the
interview is that the evidence is behavioural and observed over time: whether you ask before you
build, whether you disclose a mistake, whether your numbers hold.

That framing is useful because it turns onboarding from a passive experience into a set of choices.
You are not waiting to be onboarded; you are producing the evidence that the hiring decision was
right.

### 1.6 What this topic does not cover

It does not cover performance management, promotion, or managing a team; those are later-career
topics. It does not cover the technical content of the role, which the methods modules provide. It
does not replace your employer's mandatory training, which is binding and usually takes precedence
over any plan you write.

## 3. The core mental model

Onboarding is the construction of three maps and the conversion of one of them into a delivered
decision.

```text
  PHASE 1: LEARN (days 1-30)        PHASE 2: CONTRIBUTE (31-60)     PHASE 3: OWN (61-90)
  -------------------------         --------------------------      --------------------
  Data map                          Reproduce one analysis          Own one decision
  - sources, custodians             - same numbers or explain       - with a named owner
  - latency, quality                  the difference                - with a review
  - access route                    Deliver one quick win           Propose the next step
  Stakeholder map                   - useful, low-risk, visible     - with evidence
  - influence, interest             Write one memo                  Establish a cadence
  - cadence                         - what you found                - 1:1s, review, demo
  Decision map
  - who decides what
  - where the role fits
        |                                   |                              |
        v                                   v                              v
  OUTPUT: three maps                OUTPUT: first credible result   OUTPUT: a mandate
```

The phases overlap in practice. The point is the output of each: maps, a result, a mandate. A new
hire who reaches day 90 with a mandate has converted the hiring prediction into a role. One who
reaches day 90 with only activity has not.

### 3.1 The three maps

| Map | Purpose | Minimum content | How to build it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data | Know what exists and how to get it | 8 to 12 sources with custodian, latency, quality, access route | Interviews, data catalogue, one extract per source |
| Stakeholder | Know who decides and who influences | 12 to 20 people with role, influence, interest, current concern | 1:1s, org chart, project history |
| Decision | Know where the role changes something | 3 to 5 decisions with owner, cadence, current evidence | Shadowing, meeting attendance, document review |

The maps are not documentation for its own sake. They are the instrument you use to find the first
useful thing to do.

### 3.2 The credibility bank

Think of the first 90 days as a bank account. The organisation opens it with a deposit: the goodwill
of having chosen you. Every reliable delivery adds to it. Every surprise, missed commitment, or
overstated result withdraws from it.

The rules that follow:

1. **Under-promise on dates, over-deliver on quality.** A date missed is remembered; a result that
   is right the first time compounds.
2. **Say what you do not know.** A new hire who says "I do not know yet, I will find out by Thursday"
   is trusted more than one who guesses.
3. **Never present a number you cannot defend.** The first number you present sets the standard for
   every number after it.
4. **Close loops.** If you say you will send something, send it, even if it is a one-line update.

### 3.3 Diagnose the situation

The same plan does not fit every start. Michael Watkins' STARS framework (start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment, sustaining success) distinguishes five
situations: start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment, and sustaining success. The
situation determines whether the first 90 days should be about building, fixing, scaling, persuading,
or refining.

| Situation | What the organisation needs | Your first 90 days |
|---|---|---|
| Start-up | Build something from nothing | Define the first decision, ship a minimal artifact, avoid over-building |
| Turnaround | Fix a failing system under pressure | Diagnose fast, stabilise, communicate the plan early |
| Accelerated growth | Scale without breaking | Document, automate, hire or train, protect quality |
| Realignment | Revive a drifting function | Listen, build a coalition, propose a small number of changes |
| Sustaining success | Preserve and refine | Learn the existing standard, improve one thing, do not disrupt |

A new data scientist joining a team that has never had one is in a start-up situation. The same
title at a team with a mature pipeline is sustaining success. The plan differs.

### 3.4 The five conversations with your manager

Watkins describes five conversations that should happen in the first 90 days: situational diagnosis,
expectations, style, resources, and personal development. They are not five meetings; they are
threads that recur.

For a data role, the two that matter most are expectations and style.

**Expectations.** What does success look like at 30, 60, and 90 days? Who judges it? What is the
first decision the role should influence? Write the answers down and send them to your manager for
confirmation. A plan that exists only in a meeting is a plan that will be reinterpreted.

**Style.** How does your manager want to communicate? What decisions require consultation, and which
are yours? How often should you check in? A manager who wants a weekly written update and a
manager who wants a daily stand-up require different working habits, and neither is wrong.

### 3.5 The week-one note

The most useful artifact of the first week is a one-page note to your manager that records what you
heard. It contains the three decisions, the success criteria, the cadence, the access you need, and
the questions you have not answered.

The note does three things. It tests your understanding while correction is cheap. It creates a
record that both of you can refer to in month three. And it signals that you work in writing, which
is the operating mode of most senior roles.

### 3.6 What the first month is not

It is not a sprint to prove you were worth hiring. Candidates who treat month one as an audition
produce work that is fast and under-contextualised, and the correction costs more than the delay.

It is also not a passive orientation. Waiting for a plan, a buddy, and a perfect data environment is
how a ramp becomes three months of meetings. The plan in this topic exists because nobody else will
build it for you at the level of detail your first 90 days need.

### 3.7 The quick win

A quick win is a small, useful, low-risk deliverable that a named person uses within the first 60
days. It is not the biggest thing you can do; it is the thing that proves the ramp is working.

Criteria:

- **Useful.** Someone changes a decision or saves time because of it.
- **Low-risk.** If it is wrong, the consequence is a correction, not a patient-safety event.
- **Visible.** The person who sponsored the role knows it happened.
- **Finishable.** It can be completed in 10 to 15 working days with the access you have.

Examples: a reconciled cohort count, a reproducible version of a monthly report, a data-quality
dashboard for a known problem, a validated extract that replaces a manual process.

> **Note:** A quick win is not a shortcut around validation. If the deliverable affects a clinical
> or regulatory decision, it goes through the same review as any other artifact. Choose a quick win
> whose risk profile allows a short review.

## 5. The mechanics, step by step

The plan runs from two weeks before the start date to the day-90 review. Each phase has an output
that the next phase depends on.

### Step 1. Before the start: provision access and read the public record

**Input:** the offer letter, the employer's public documents, and the hiring manager's contact.
**Output:** an access checklist submitted, and a one-page context brief.
**Rationale:** access provisioning is the longest lead-time item in a health system. Starting it
before day one is the single highest-leverage onboarding action.

Do three things:

1. **Submit access requests early.** Ask your manager which systems you will need and who approves
   each. Typical: electronic health record reporting, the data warehouse, the analytics environment, the ticket system,
   the collaboration suite. Each may have its own training prerequisite.

| Access | Typical approver | Typical lead time | Common blocker |
|---|---|---|---|
| EHR reporting | Informatics or HIM | 1 to 3 weeks | Training module not completed |
| Data warehouse | Data governance committee | 2 to 6 weeks | Committee meeting schedule |
| Analytics environment | Platform team | 1 to 2 weeks | Licence allocation |
| Claims or external data | Data use agreement owner | 4 to 12 weeks | Contract review |
| Production deployment | Change advisory board | 2 to 4 weeks | Change freeze |
2. **Complete mandatory training before day one if permitted.** Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act training, research ethics, and
   information security modules are often online and can be done early.
3. **Read the public record.** The annual report, quality reports, strategic plan, recent press
   releases, and any published analytics work. Write one page: the organisation's three priorities,
   the metrics it reports publicly, and the decisions your role might influence.

### Step 2. Week 1: meet people and confirm expectations

**Input:** the context brief and the manager's calendar.
**Output:** 8 to 12 conversations booked or completed, and a written expectations note confirmed by
your manager.
**Rationale:** the first week is for orientation and relationships, not delivery. The goal is to
learn who decides what and to agree on what success means.

Schedule, in order: your manager (60 minutes), your immediate team (30 minutes each), two or three
key stakeholders (30 minutes each), and one person who has been in the organisation less than a year
(they remember what is confusing).

A workable first-week agenda:

| Day | Morning | Afternoon |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Manager, expectations and access | Team introductions, tooling setup |
| 2 | Immediate team, one to one | Read the last three months of team documents |
| 3 | Two stakeholders | Attempt one simple query end to end |
| 4 | Two stakeholders | Write the context brief and the open questions |
| 5 | Governance or data custodian | Send the expectations note to your manager |

Questions for the manager:

- What are the three decisions this role should influence in the first year?
- What does success look like at 30, 60, and 90 days, and who judges it?
- What does the team expect from me that is not in the job description?
- What has gone wrong with previous hires or projects, and what should I avoid?
- How do you prefer updates, and how often?
- Which meetings should I attend, and which are optional?

Write the answers into a one-page note and send it: "This is what I heard; please correct anything I
have wrong." The correction is the point.

### Step 3. Days 8 to 30: build the three maps

**Input:** the conversations and any access granted.
**Output:** a data map, a stakeholder map, and a decision map.
**Rationale:** the maps are how you convert a list of names and systems into a model of how the
organisation works. They are also the artifact you show at the 30-day review.

Data map fields, for 8 to 12 sources: name, custodian, steward, refresh frequency, latency, known
quality issues, access route, and the decisions it supports.

Stakeholder map fields, for 12 to 20 people: role, influence (high, medium, low), interest (high,
medium, low), current concern, preferred channel, and the last thing they asked for.

Decision map fields, for 3 to 5 decisions: the decision, the owner, the cadence, the evidence used
today, and where your role could improve it.

The mapping work is interviews plus one extract per data source. Do not trust the catalogue; open
the data and count rows.

### Step 4. Days 8 to 30: shadow the workflow

**Input:** the decision map.
**Output:** a decision log from two or three observed sessions.
**Rationale:** the decision map records what people say happens. Shadowing records what happens. The
gap is where the useful work is.

Attend the meeting where the decision is made. Log the trigger, the information used, the time
taken, the alternatives considered, and the outcome. One or two sessions are enough to see whether
the decision is as described.

If you cannot attend, ask for the last three examples of the decision and read the artifacts: the
report, the email, the ticket.

### Step 4b. Day 30: run the first review

**Input:** the three maps and the reproduction in progress.
**Output:** a 30-minute review with your manager and a corrected plan.
**Rationale:** the 30-day review is the first checkpoint at which the plan can be corrected before
the quick win consumes the second month.

Agenda: what I have learned (maps), what surprised me, what I have not been able to access, and what
I propose to deliver by day 60. Ask for one correction and one priority. Write the outcome down.

### Step 5. Days 8 to 30: reproduce one existing analysis

**Input:** an existing report or analysis with a known result.
**Output:** a reproduction that either matches or explains the difference.
**Rationale:** reproduction is the fastest way to learn the data, the definitions, and the
organisation's quality standard. It also produces the first credible evidence that you can be
trusted with the numbers.

Choose an analysis that someone relies on monthly. Reproduce it from source, compare the outputs,
and document every discrepancy with a cause. The memo is short: what matched, what did not, why, and
what should change.

This is the deliverable that most impresses a manager in month one, because it demonstrates both
technical skill and respect for existing work.

### Step 6. Days 31 to 60: deliver the quick win

**Input:** the maps, the reproduction memo, and the manager's priorities.
**Output:** one delivered artifact used by a named person.
**Rationale:** the quick win converts the learning phase into evidence. It should be small enough to
finish and significant enough to matter.

Selection criteria, scored 1 to 5: usefulness, visibility, risk (inverted), and finishability within
15 working days. Choose the highest total that your manager agrees is worth doing.

Delivery includes a short note: what it does, how to use it, what it does not do, and who to contact.
A deliverable without a note is a file; a deliverable with a note is a product.

The note is four paragraphs:

```text
What it does:      one sentence, in the user's language.
How to use it:     where it lives, when it refreshes, what to check.
What it does not:  the exclusions and the known limitations.
Who to contact:    you, and the person who covers when you are away.
```

### Step 7. Days 31 to 60: establish the operating cadence

**Input:** the stakeholder map.
**Output:** a weekly and monthly rhythm that others can rely on.
**Rationale:** reliability is built through predictable cadence, not heroic effort. A weekly update
that always arrives is worth more than an occasional brilliant analysis.

Minimum cadence:

- **Weekly:** a written update to your manager (five lines: what moved, what is blocked, what you
  need). The five-line format matters: it takes ten minutes, it is skimmable, and it creates a record
  that both of you can refer to when the quarter is reviewed.
- **Weekly or biweekly:** a 1:1 with your manager, agenda owned jointly.
- **Monthly:** a short review with the primary stakeholder, showing what changed.
- **Quarterly:** a review with the sponsor, connecting the work to the decision.

The cadence is a commitment, not a preference. If you cannot make the weekly update, send three lines
saying why. The reliability of the rhythm is what earns the autonomy to miss it occasionally.

### Decision table: stakeholder cadence

| Influence | Interest | Cadence | Channel |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | High | Weekly or biweekly | 1:1 plus written update |
| High | Low | Monthly | Short written brief |
| Low | High | Monthly | Working session |
| Low | Low | Quarterly | Group update |

The table prevents two errors: over-engaging people who do not need it, and under-engaging the
person whose approval you will need in month four.

### Step 8. Days 61 to 90: own one decision

**Input:** the decision map and the credibility built in the first two months.
**Output:** one decision where your analysis is the evidence, with a named owner and a review.
**Rationale:** ownership is the transition from doing tasks to being accountable. It is what the
day-90 review is actually assessing.

Pick a decision small enough to influence and important enough to matter. Prepare the evidence, walk
the owner through it, and record the outcome. If the owner decides against your recommendation, that
is still a successful transition; the role influenced a decision.

The conversation has four parts: the decision and its deadline, the evidence and its uncertainty, the
recommendation with the alternative, and what would change your mind. Keep it to 15 minutes and leave
the document with the owner. A decision that is only discussed in a meeting has not been influenced;
it has been mentioned.

### Step 9. Days 61 to 90: propose the next quarter

**Input:** everything above.
**Output:** a two-page plan for the next 90 days, with three priorities and a measure for each.
**Rationale:** the day-90 review is a forward-looking conversation. The organisation wants to know
what you will do next, not only what you learned.

The plan should name: the decision you will influence, the evidence you will produce, the
stakeholder you will work with, and the measure that will tell you whether it worked.

A one-page template:

```text
Priority 1: [decision] for [owner], measured by [metric], by [date].
  Evidence: [artifact] validated by [method].
  Risk: [what could make it fail] mitigated by [action].
Priority 2: ...
Priority 3: ...
What I will stop doing: [one thing], because [reason].
```

The last line is the one managers remember. A plan that adds without subtracting is a wish list.

### Step 10. Day 90: run the review

**Input:** the maps, the deliverables, and the next-quarter plan.
**Output:** a written summary, an agreed development plan, and a confirmed mandate.
**Rationale:** the review is where the informal understanding becomes explicit. A review without a
written output is a conversation that will be remembered differently by each participant.

Agenda, 60 minutes:

1. What I learned (the three maps, five minutes).
2. What I delivered (two artifacts, ten minutes).
3. What I got wrong and what I changed (five minutes).
4. What I propose for the next 90 days (fifteen minutes).
5. What I need from you (resources, access, sponsorship, ten minutes).
6. How we will work together (cadence, decision rights, ten minutes).

Send the written summary within 48 hours.

### What good looks like at each checkpoint

| Checkpoint | Weak | Adequate | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Day 30 | Waiting for access | Access granted, team met | Three maps drafted, one analysis reproduced |
| Day 60 | Still learning | First deliverable in progress | Quick win delivered and used, cadence established |
| Day 90 | Activity report | Two deliverables, no owner | One decision influenced, next quarter planned |

### Branch points and edge cases

**Remote onboarding.** The maps take longer because incidental conversations do not happen. Schedule
them deliberately: 20 conversations in the first three weeks, each 30 minutes, with a written
follow-up. Ask for a buddy who can answer the small questions.

**A regulated role.** Access and training are gated. Sequence: mandatory training, then read-only
access, then production access, then delivery. Do not promise a date before the access is confirmed.

**A clinical environment.** Shadowing requires permission and patient-privacy awareness. Ask for
observational access rather than system access, and do not record identifiers.

**A managerial start.** Your first 90 days include your team's expectations of you. Add one-to-ones
with each direct report in week one, and do not change anything structural in the first 30 days
unless it is a safety issue.

**A career change.** You will be slower on the domain and faster on the methods, or the reverse. Say
which one you are learning, and ask for a domain mentor explicitly. The request is normal and usually
granted.

**A visa-related start delay.** Keep the pre-start work going: reading, training, and introductions.
A delayed start with preparation is better than an on-time start with none.

**An over-levelled start.** Ask for written success criteria and a named mentor in week one. If the
scope is above your evidence, the ramp is the mitigation.

**Your manager is new too.** Two people learning at once is a common situation. Propose the cadence
explicitly, write the expectations note, and confirm it in writing. The structure substitutes for the
experience neither of you has in the organisation.

**The role was misdescribed.** If the work is materially different from the interviews, raise it in
week two with the manager, not in month three. State what you expected, what you have found, and what
you propose. Some differences are normal; a role that is 80 percent something else is a scope
problem that gets harder to fix later.

**A return after a gap.** Use the same plan, but front-load the technical refresh: reproduce an
analysis in week two rather than week four, and ask for feedback on it.

### Decision table: what to do when access is delayed

| Delay | Action | Communication |
|---|---|---|
| Under 1 week | Continue interviews and reading | Mention in the weekly update |
| 1 to 3 weeks | Reproduce an analysis on a public or sample extract | Tell your manager what is blocked |
| 3 to 6 weeks | Escalate to the sponsor; propose a interim access route | Written escalation with the impact |
| Over 6 weeks | Ask for a formal decision on the access model | Escalate above your manager |

### Decision table: quick-win selection

| Candidate | Useful | Visible | Risk (low = 5) | Finishable (5 = yes) | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Reconcile the monthly readmission report | 5 | 4 | 4 | 5 | 18 |
| Build a new prediction model | 3 | 5 | 1 | 2 | 11 |
| Automate a manual extract | 4 | 3 | 5 | 5 | 17 |
| Redesign the data catalogue | 2 | 2 | 4 | 2 | 10 |

Choose the highest total that your manager agrees is worth doing. The model scores low because it is
high-risk and not finishable in the window, not because it is unimportant.

## 6. Worked example

Ibrahim is 38, with seven years of experience in payer analytics. He starts as a senior clinical data
scientist at a 600-bed health system, reporting to the director of analytics. The role is new; the
team has three analysts and no data scientists.

### 6.1 The situation diagnosis

The team has reporting but no predictive work, and the director wants to move from descriptive to
prescriptive analytics. In STARS terms, this is a start-up situation inside an established
organisation: the capability is being built, and the first 90 days should establish a decision, a
method, and a reputation, not a platform.

### 6.2 The pre-start fortnight

He submits access requests for the EHR reporting environment, the data warehouse, the analytics
notebook environment, and the ticket system. Two require training modules; he completes them before
day one. He reads the annual report, the public quality report, and the strategic plan, and writes a
one-page brief: the system's three public priorities are readmissions, emergency department
throughput, and sepsis mortality.

By day one, two of four access requests are approved. The data warehouse request is waiting on a
governance committee that meets on the 15th.

| Access | Approved by day 1 | Lead time | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| EHR reporting | Yes | 2 weeks | Could run basic counts |
| Analytics notebook | Yes | 1 week | Could reproduce simple queries |
| Data warehouse | No | 5 weeks | Blocked cohort work until week 6 |
| Claims extract | No | 10 weeks | Deferred to Q2 |

### 6.3 Diagnostic 1: the week-three model

In his first plan, Ibrahim intended to deliver a readmission model by week six. He began building it
in week three on a partial extract, before the warehouse access was granted and before he had spoken
to the care management team.

The mock review, with a former colleague, surfaced two problems. The cohort used encounters rather
than discharges, and the care management team's actual capacity was 40 enrolments a month, which made
the model's threshold meaningless.

The corrected plan moved the model to the second quarter and replaced the week-six deliverable with a
reproduction and a reconciliation. The lesson he wrote in his own plan: "Do not model a decision you
have not observed."

### 6.4 Diagnostic 2: the missing stakeholder

His first stakeholder list had 11 names, all from analytics and quality. It did not include the chief
medical information officer, who controlled the data roadmap, or the care management director, who
owned the readmission programme.

The gap was visible in week four, when he presented a data-quality finding to the quality director
and was asked, "Does the CMIO know about this?" The answer was no. The finding was correct and the
escalation was missing.

He rebuilt the map with 14 names across four groups: analytics, clinical leadership, operations, and
governance. He scheduled 30-minute conversations with the four he had missed, and added a monthly
note to the CMIO.

### 6.5 The 90 days, week by week

| Weeks | Activity | Output |
|---|---|---|
| -2 to 0 | Access requests, mandatory training, public reading | Access checklist, context brief |
| 1 | 12 conversations, manager expectations note | Confirmed success criteria |
| 2 to 4 | Data map, stakeholder map, decision map | Three maps, 30-day review |
| 3 to 5 | Reproduce the monthly readmission report | Reconciliation memo |
| 5 to 7 | Fix the report: deduplicate encounters, exclude planned readmissions | Corrected report, 6 users |
| 7 to 9 | Shadow the care management intake meeting | Decision log, capacity confirmed at 40/month |
| 9 to 12 | Automate the report pipeline | Runtime from 40 to 9 minutes |
| 10 to 12 | Propose the Q2 model with the capacity constraint | Two-page plan |
| 13 | Day-90 review | Written summary, development plan |

### 6.6 The numbers

The reconciliation found two defects in the monthly readmission report:

| Defect | Effect | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Encounters counted instead of discharges | About 4 percent inflation | Deduplicate to one row per discharge |
| Planned readmissions included | About 8 percent inflation | Apply the planned-readmission exclusion |
| Combined | Reported 12.4 percent, corrected 10.9 percent | 1.5 percentage points on 800 monthly discharges |

The 1.5-point correction is about 12 admissions a month that were being counted as unplanned
readmissions. Over a year, that is about 144 events reclassified, which is large enough to change a
quality report and small enough that no clinician had noticed.

The care management team had been using the higher number to justify its staffing request, which
made the correction politically sensitive. Ibrahim presented it with the method, the
counts, and a recommendation to restate the historical series, and let the director decide how to
communicate it.

The automation reduced the report runtime from 40 minutes of manual work to 9 minutes of pipeline
run time, and removed two manual transcription steps. Six users received the report monthly, so the
saving was about 3.1 hours per month plus the transcription risk.

### 6.7 The day-90 review

He presented:

1. **The maps.** Nine data sources, fourteen stakeholders, three decisions.
2. **Two deliverables.** The reconciliation memo and the corrected, automated report.
3. **One thing he got wrong.** The week-three model attempt, and the rule he now follows.
4. **The next quarter.** A readmission model scoped to the 40-slot capacity, with the care management
   director as the decision owner and a stepped-wedge pilot proposal.

The director agreed to the plan and added one thing Ibrahim had not asked for: a monthly slot at the
clinical leadership meeting to present the work. That slot was the mandate. It also created a
recurring obligation, which is the point: a mandate is a commitment to show up with something true,
not a reward for the first 90 days. He put the slot in his calendar for the next six months and wrote
the first agenda the same week.

### 6.8 Vary one assumption

Change one input: Ibrahim starts fully remote, with the team in another state.

The plan changes in four ways.

1. **The conversation count rises from 12 to 20 in the first three weeks.** Incidental contact does
   not happen, so the map has to be built deliberately.
2. **A buddy is requested explicitly.** The buddy answers the small questions: where the data
   dictionary lives, who approves access, which meeting actually decides. Without one, the same
   questions consume the manager's time.
3. **The shadowing is replaced by recorded meetings and document review.** He attends the intake
   meeting by video for two sessions, then reads the last six months of decisions from the ticket
   system.
4. **The quick win is chosen for written visibility.** The reconciliation memo is sent to a wider
   distribution than it would be in person, because remote work reduces the informal transmission of
   good work.

### 6.9 What he would do differently

Three changes, in order of value:

1. **Start the data warehouse request before accepting.** He learned after day one that the form
   required a named project and a data steward signature. A two-week head start would have moved the
   access from week six to week four.
2. **Meet the care management director in week one, not week seven.** The capacity constraint was the
   single most important fact in the plan, and it was available in a 30-minute conversation he
   scheduled late because he did not know the role existed.
3. **Write the reconciliation memo before fixing the report.** He fixed first and documented after,
   which made the restatement conversation harder. The memo should have come first, with the fix as
   its recommendation.

### 6.10 The mandate conversation

At the day-90 review, the director asked what Ibrahim needed. He had prepared three asks, in order:

1. **A monthly slot at clinical leadership.** To present the work and hear the decisions it should
   inform.
2. **A named clinical owner for the readmission model.** The care management director, with 10
   percent of their time committed.
3. **Access to the claims extract for Q2.** To extend the cohort beyond the EHR.

He received the first and the second. The third was deferred pending a data use agreement. The asks
were specific, costed in time, and framed as what the work needed rather than what he wanted, which
is why two of three were granted in the meeting.

The general lesson: remote onboarding does not change the outputs, it changes the effort required to
get them. The maps still have to be built; they are built through scheduled conversations instead of
corridors.

### 6.11 What the 90 days cost

The plan consumed about 35 percent of his working time in weeks 1 to 4 (maps, conversations,
reproduction), about 20 percent in weeks 5 to 8 (quick win, cadence), and about 10 percent in weeks 9
to 12 (ownership, next-quarter plan). The remaining time went to the role's routine work, which began
to arrive in week three.

That distribution is normal and worth planning for. A new hire who spends 100 percent of month one on
maps is not doing the job; one who spends none of it on maps is doing the job without context.

## 7. Code, query, or calculation

The script builds the stakeholder engagement plan from Section 5, Step 7. It assigns a cadence from
influence and interest, flags overdue contacts, summarises coverage by group, and estimates the weekly
time the cadence requires.

Assumptions:

- Influence and interest are scored 1 to 5 by the new hire, based on the first three weeks of
  conversations. They are judgements, not measurements.
- Cadence rules are a convention: high influence and high interest gets a weekly contact; high on
  either gets monthly; the rest quarterly.
- Each contact is assumed to take 30 minutes, including preparation and follow-up.

```python
"""Stakeholder engagement planner for the first 90 days, standard library only.

Run: python stakeholder_plan.py
Given stakeholders scored on influence and interest, assigns an engagement
cadence, flags overdue contacts, and summarises coverage by group.
"""

STAKEHOLDERS = [
    # role, group, influence 1-5, interest 1-5, days since last contact
    ("Director of analytics", "analytics", 5, 5, 3),
    ("CMIO", "clinical leadership", 5, 4, 18),
    ("Care management director", "operations", 4, 5, 9),
    ("Quality director", "operations", 4, 3, 22),
    ("Data governance chair", "governance", 5, 3, 12),
    ("Privacy officer", "governance", 4, 2, 30),
    ("EHR reporting lead", "analytics", 3, 5, 4),
    ("Two analysts on the team", "analytics", 2, 5, 2),
    ("Nurse informatics lead", "clinical leadership", 3, 4, 16),
    ("Finance partner", "operations", 3, 2, 45),
    ("Research director", "clinical leadership", 3, 3, 27),
    ("IT platform owner", "analytics", 4, 3, 11),
    ("Population health lead", "operations", 4, 4, 20),
    ("Executive sponsor", "leadership", 5, 5, 25),
]


def cadence_days(influence, interest):
    if influence >= 4 and interest >= 4:
        return 7
    if influence >= 4:
        return 30
    if interest >= 4:
        return 30
    return 90


def main():
    rows = []
    for role, group, influence, interest, last in STAKEHOLDERS:
        cadence = cadence_days(influence, interest)
        overdue = max(0, last - cadence)
        rows.append((role, group, influence, interest, last, cadence, overdue))

    print("STAKEHOLDER ENGAGEMENT PLAN")
    print(f"  {'role':28s} {'group':20s} {'inf':>4s} {'int':>4s} {'last':>5s} {'cad':>5s} {'over':>5s}")
    for role, group, influence, interest, last, cadence, overdue in rows:
        print(f"  {role:28s} {group:20s} {influence:4d} {interest:4d} {last:5d} {cadence:5d} {overdue:5d}")

    overdue_rows = sorted([r for r in rows if r[6] > 0], key=lambda r: -r[6])
    print()
    print("OVERDUE, MOST OVERDUE FIRST")
    if not overdue_rows:
        print("  none")
    for role, group, influence, interest, last, cadence, overdue in overdue_rows:
        print(f"  {role:28s} {overdue:3d} days past a {cadence}-day cadence")

    print()
    print("COVERAGE BY GROUP")
    groups = {}
    for role, group, influence, interest, last, cadence, overdue in rows:
        g = groups.setdefault(group, {"n": 0, "overdue": 0, "high": 0})
        g["n"] += 1
        g["overdue"] += 1 if overdue > 0 else 0
        g["high"] += 1 if influence >= 4 else 0
    for group, g in sorted(groups.items()):
        print(f"  {group:22s} people {g['n']:2d}  high influence {g['high']:2d}  overdue {g['overdue']:2d}")

    print()
    print("WEEKLY TIME BUDGET")
    weekly = sum(1 for r in rows if r[5] == 7)
    monthly = sum(1 for r in rows if r[5] == 30)
    quarterly = sum(1 for r in rows if r[5] == 90)
    hours = weekly * 0.5 + monthly * 0.5 / 4 + quarterly * 0.5 / 12
    print(f"  weekly contacts: {weekly}, monthly: {monthly}, quarterly: {quarterly}")
    print(f"  estimated hours per week at 30 minutes per contact: {hours:.1f}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

## 8. Data, standards, and regulatory context

Onboarding in a health system is gated by access, training, and governance. The gates are not
bureaucratic decoration; they are the mechanism by which the organisation can let a new person touch
patient data. Understanding them shortens the ramp and prevents the most common early mistake, which
is assuming access will arrive when you need it.

### 8.1 Access and privacy

| Requirement | What it constrains | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| HIPAA minimum necessary | Access to protected health information limited to what the role requires | Role-based access request, approval |
| HIPAA workforce training | Anyone with PHI access must be trained | Completion record, often annual |
| Audit logging | Access to PHI is logged and reviewable | System configuration, audit reports |
| Business associate agreement | Vendors handling PHI on your behalf | Signed BAA |
| Data use agreement | Research or secondary use of data | Signed agreement, IRB determination |
| Institutional review board | Research use of human data | Approval or exemption determination |

The practical sequence for a new analyst is: complete training, receive role-based read access, work
under supervision on de-identified or limited data, then receive production access. Compressing the
sequence is possible with an approved exception; skipping it is not.

### 8.2 Regulated systems and records

If the role touches systems that support regulatory submissions, additional requirements apply.
Electronic records and signatures used in drug development are governed by 21 CFR Part 11 in the
United States, and good clinical practice (ICH E6) governs trial conduct. Access to those systems is
usually validated, individual, and audited; sharing credentials is a serious violation.

For device software, the quality management system (for example ISO 13485) defines training,
document control, and change control. A new engineer may be required to complete training on the QMS
before committing code to a regulated branch.

### 8.3 Information blocking and interoperability

In the United States, the 21st Century Cures Act and the ONC information blocking rules restrict
practices that interfere with access to electronic health information, with exceptions. A new hire
does not need to interpret the rule, but should know that requests for data sharing have a formal
process and a timeline, and that "the vendor will not let us" is not a sufficient answer inside a
governed organisation.

### 8.4 What is binding and what is convention

Binding: the privacy rule, the training requirement, the audit requirement, the data use agreement,
and the QMS procedures where they apply.

Convention: the 30-60-90 template, the cadence rules in Section 7, the shape of the maps, and the
day-90 review agenda. These are useful structures, not requirements. Adapt them to the organisation.

### 8.5 The access request as a test of the organisation

How an organisation handles a new hire's access request is a diagnostic. A clear form, a named
approver, and a stated lead time indicate a governed process. A request that circulates without an
owner, or requires a personal favour, indicates that access is political.

For a new hire, the practical response is the same in both cases: document the request, the date, the
approver, and the blocker; escalate with the impact rather than the frustration; and propose an
interim route. The documentation is also the evidence you will need if the delay is raised in your
review.

### 8.6 What the rules do not do

They do not tell you which decision to influence, how to build a stakeholder map, or what a good
first 90 days looks like. Those are judgement, and they are what the rest of this topic is about.

They also do not guarantee that the organisation will give you the access, the time, or the
sponsorship the plan assumes. The plan is a proposal; the organisation's response to it is
information about whether the role can succeed, and it is better to learn that in week three than in
month nine.

## 9. Industry practice and case evidence

### 9.1 Documented: time to proficiency is months, not weeks

The MIT Sloan Management Review article "Getting New Hires Up to Speed Quickly" (Rollag, Parise and
Cross) reports Mellon Financial research estimating that lost productivity from the learning curve
cost 1 to 2.5 percent of total revenue, and that average time-to-full-productivity was about 8 weeks
for clerical roles, 20 weeks for professionals, and 26 weeks or more for executives. Internal
transfers ramped about twice as fast as external hires.

The figures come from one financial services company and are not a universal benchmark. The pattern is
consistent with what managers report in health systems, and it supports a plan that expects a
professional role to take about five months to reach full output, with the first 90 days focused on
access, context, and one credible delivery.

### 9.2 Documented: structured onboarding plans are used in clinical research

A 2023 case study in Frontiers in Medicine described a competency-based, on-demand 90-day onboarding
learning plan for clinical research professionals at Duke University. The report describes satisfaction
above 70 percent and completion of 76 percent for new-to-Duke hires, and concludes that the template
alone is insufficient: manager involvement in tailoring the plan is essential.

The study is a single-institution case report, not a controlled evaluation. Its useful finding for a
new hire is the conclusion about manager involvement: the plan works when it is discussed, not when it
is issued.

### 9.3 Documented: situation diagnosis is a published framework

Michael Watkins' *The First 90 Days* sets out the STARS framework (start-up, turnaround, accelerated
growth, realignment, sustaining success) and five conversations with a new manager: situational
diagnosis, expectations, style, resources, and personal development. The framework is a practitioner
model, widely used in leadership transitions, and it is not a validated psychometric instrument.

Its value for a data role is the situational diagnosis. A new data scientist joining a team with no
data function is in a start-up situation; the same title in a mature team is sustaining success. The
plan, the pace, and the tolerance for disruption differ.

### 9.4 What the studies agree on

The three sources differ in method and population, but they converge on three points. Ramp time is
measured in months for professional roles, not weeks. Structure shortens the ramp, but only when a
manager engages with it. And the most important early task is relational: finding the people who hold
the context you cannot yet see.

For a new hire, the practical implication is to invest the first month in relationships and access,
not in output. The output follows, and it is judged against the context you built.

### 9.5 Inference, labelled

- **Inference:** access provisioning is the single largest source of delay in the first 30 days at
  health systems, because it depends on committees that meet on a schedule.
- **Inference:** the quick win matters less for its output than for the evidence it gives the manager
  that the ramp is working.
- **Inference:** a new hire who reproduces an existing analysis in the first month is trusted faster
  than one who starts something new, because reproduction demonstrates respect for the existing work.
- **Inference:** remote onboarding lengthens the stakeholder map by two to three weeks, because the
  incidental conversations that build it do not happen.

> **Note:** No onboarding success rate or retention statistic is quoted. The available studies are
> single-institution case reports or vendor surveys, and the variation by role and organisation is
> larger than any reported average.

## 10. Failure modes and red flags

| Failure | Early warning sign | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Waiting for access | Week two with no data | Request submitted on day one | Nothing to deliver in month one | Submit requests before the start date |
| Modelling a decision you have not observed | The cohort definition is invented | Enthusiasm to deliver | Wrong analysis, lost credibility | Shadow the decision first |
| Missing a key stakeholder | A senior name appears late | Map built from the org chart | Work is ignored or blocked | Ask "who else should I meet" in every conversation |
| Premature delivery | A model in week three | Pressure to prove value | Errors presented to decision-makers | Choose a quick win with a review path |
| Over-promising dates | You give a date before access | Optimism | Missed commitment in month one | Give ranges and confirm after access |
| No written expectations | Success criteria exist only in conversation | Assumed shared understanding | Review in month four is a surprise | Send the one-page note in week one |
| Skipping mandatory training | Access delayed by a module | Training treated as optional | Compliance issue | Complete before day one if permitted |
| No cadence | Updates arrive irregularly | No rhythm agreed | Manager assumes disengagement | Weekly five-line update |
| Hiding a mistake | The correction is discovered later | Fear in the first months | Trust damage disproportionate to the error | Disclose early with a correction plan |
| Scope drift | You are doing a different job by month two | No mandate agreed | The role becomes something else | Day-90 mandate conversation |
| Ignoring the informal network | You only know your team | Remote or shy start | Dependence on one person | 20 conversations in three weeks |
| Burning the credibility bank | Two missed commitments in month one | Overcommitment | Autonomy is withdrawn | Under-promise, deliver, communicate |
| No written record | Decisions are remembered differently | Conversations only | Disputes in month three | Send the week-one note and the review summary |

| No day-90 summary | The review is a conversation with no record | No written artifact | Mandate stays implicit | Send the summary within 48 hours |

Two red flags are about the employer. A start with no access plan and no named buddy usually means
the team has not onboarded anyone in a long time, and you will spend political capital asking for
basics. A manager who cannot state what success looks like at 90 days is a manager who will evaluate
you on impression; propose the criteria yourself and get them confirmed in writing.

## 11. Assumptions and limits

| Assumption | Why it is needed | What breaks it | How to test it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Access takes weeks | It justifies pre-start requests | Some organisations provision in days | Ask the hiring manager for the typical lead time |
| The manager has a plan | It shapes the first conversation | Many managers do not | Ask in week one and adapt |
| 90 days is enough to own a decision | It sets the phase goal | Highly regulated roles need longer | Ask what a realistic first decision is |
| A quick win is available | It gives month two a deliverable | Some roles are purely exploratory | Ask the manager to nominate candidates |
| Reproduction is welcome | It justifies the first deliverable | Some teams feel audited | Frame it as learning, share the memo first |
| Stakeholders will meet | It enables the map | Senior people are unavailable | Ask for 20 minutes, not 60 |
| The maps stay current | They guide the next quarter | Reorganisations change the map | Review monthly, rebuild quarterly |
| Remote onboarding can be scheduled | It substitutes for proximity | Time zones make scheduling hard | Book recurring slots in week one |
| The role is as described | It justifies the plan | Scope changes after hiring | Raise discrepancies in week two |
| The manager wants a written cadence | It justifies the weekly update | Some managers prefer verbal | Ask in week one and adapt |
| The first 90 days are reviewable | It justifies the artifacts | Some organisations have no review | Ask whether there is a formal check-in |

The main limit is that the plan assumes a reasonably functional organisation. In a dysfunctional
one, access is political, the manager is absent, and the stakeholder map is contested. The same
artifacts still help, but they become evidence for a conversation about whether the role can work,
rather than a ramp plan.

## 12. Exercises

**Exercise 1. Situation diagnosis (recall, 20 minutes).**
Deliverable: a STARS classification for three roles you have held or interviewed for, with the
evidence for each.
Acceptance criteria: each classification is justified by the state of the function, not the size of
the company; one role is classified as mixed, with the mix explained; the plan implication of each
classification is stated in one line.

**Exercise 2. Data map (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: a table of eight data sources with custodian, refresh, latency, quality issue, access
route, and decision supported.
Acceptance criteria: every source has a named custodian role; at least two sources have a known
quality issue; the access route is specific enough to act on.

**Exercise 3. Stakeholder map (application, 45 minutes).**
Deliverable: 12 to 20 stakeholders with influence, interest, concern, and cadence.
Acceptance criteria: at least three groups are represented; each high-influence person has a cadence
and a first conversation date; the map includes one person who can block access.

**Exercise 4. 30-60-90 plan (design, 60 minutes).**
Deliverable: a one-page plan with milestones at 30, 60, and 90 days, each with a measure and an owner.
Acceptance criteria: each milestone is verifiable; the day-90 milestone is a decision, not an
activity; the plan fits on one page; the manager's confirmation is recorded.

**Exercise 5. Quick-win scoring (application, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: four candidates scored on usefulness, visibility, risk, and finishability, with a
recommendation.
Acceptance criteria: scores are justified in one line each; the recommendation is the highest total
the manager would approve; one candidate is explicitly rejected for risk.

**Exercise 6. Reproduction memo (application, 90 minutes).**
Deliverable: a one-page memo comparing a reproduced analysis with the original, listing matches,
discrepancies, causes, and a recommendation.
Acceptance criteria: every discrepancy has a cause; the recommendation is specific; the memo is
written for the analysis owner, not for a statistician.

**Exercise 7. Day-90 review pack (design, 60 minutes).**
Deliverable: a six-slide pack and a one-page written summary following the Section 5, Step 10 agenda.
Acceptance criteria: the pack includes what you got wrong; the next-quarter plan has three priorities
with measures; the asks are specific and costed.

**Exercise 8. Remote adaptation (design, 30 minutes).**
Deliverable: a revised first-three-weeks schedule for a fully remote start, with 20 conversations and
a buddy request.
Acceptance criteria: the schedule is specific to days; the buddy request names what the buddy will
do; the shadowing is replaced with a concrete alternative; the plan includes one in-person visit if
travel is possible.

## 13. Self-check answers

**1. What are the three maps, and what does each contain?**
The data map (sources, custodians, latency, quality, access), the stakeholder map (influence,
interest, concern, cadence), and the decision map (decisions, owners, cadence, current evidence).

**2. Why reproduce an existing analysis before building something new?**
It teaches the data, the definitions, and the quality standard, and it produces credible evidence
that you can be trusted with the numbers. It also demonstrates respect for the existing work, which
matters to the people who built it.

**3. What makes a good quick win?**
Useful to a named person, low risk if wrong, visible to the sponsor, and finishable in 10 to 15
working days with the access you have.

**4. What should the week-one expectations note contain?**
The three decisions the role should influence, the definition of success at 30, 60, and 90 days, who
judges it, the manager's preferred cadence, and the things the manager says to avoid.

**5. Why is the day-90 milestone a decision rather than a deliverable?**
Because ownership is the transition the review is assessing. A deliverable shows you can produce; a
decision shows the role has a mandate.

**6. What do you do when access is delayed by six weeks?**
Escalate to the sponsor with the impact stated, propose an interim route (sample or public data), and
ask for a formal decision on the access model. Do not silently wait.

**7. How does remote onboarding change the plan?**
It increases the number of scheduled conversations, requires an explicit buddy, replaces incidental
shadowing with recorded meetings and document review, and favours deliverables with written
visibility. The outputs do not change; the effort to produce them does.

**8. What goes in the day-90 written summary?**
The three maps, the two deliverables, one thing you got wrong and changed, the next-quarter plan with
three priorities, the asks, and the agreed cadence and decision rights.

**9. What is the first thing you do if the data you need are not accessible?**
Identify the custodian and the approval route, submit a request naming the project and the purpose,
and propose an interim route using sample or public data. Waiting is not a plan.

**Rubric for Exercise 2.** Full marks: eight sources; a named custodian role for each; refresh and
latency stated; at least two quality issues; access route specific enough to submit a request.

**Rubric for Exercise 4.** Full marks: milestones at 30, 60, and 90 days; each has a measure and an
owner; the 90-day milestone is a decision; the plan fits one page; the manager's confirmation is
recorded with a date.

**Rubric for Exercise 6.** Full marks: every discrepancy has a cause; the recommendation is specific;
the memo is written for the analysis owner; the reproduction is rerun from source rather than from a
cached output.

**Rubric for Exercise 7.** Full marks: six slides or fewer; includes a mistake; next-quarter plan has
three priorities with measures; asks are specific and costed; the written summary is under one page.

## 14. Interview questions

**1. "What would you do in your first 30 days?"**
Strong: access, conversations, maps, reproduction. Weak: "learn the codebase" or "meet the team."

**2. "How would you find out what the organisation needs?"**
Strong: shadow the decision, map the stakeholders, ask who else to meet. Weak: read the strategy
document.

**3. "What is a quick win you would target here?"**
Strong: a small, useful, low-risk deliverable tied to a named person. Weak: a large model.

**4. "How do you handle being new and not knowing?"**
Strong: say what you do not know, commit to a date to find out, and close the loop. Weak: guess
confidently.

**5. "How would you get access to the data?"**
Strong: identifies the custodian, the committee, the training prerequisite, and the lead time. Weak:
says they would ask IT.

**6. "How do you build credibility in a new team?"**
Strong: reliable cadence, reproduction first, disclosure of mistakes. Weak: "work hard."

**7. "What would you do if the role turned out different from the interview?"**
Strong: raise it in week two with evidence and a proposal. Weak: wait and see.

**8. "How do you onboard remotely?"**
Strong: scheduled conversations, a buddy, written visibility, deliberate shadowing. Weak: "video
calls."

**9. "What does success look like at 90 days?"**
Strong: a decision influenced with a named owner and a next-quarter plan. Weak: a list of
deliverables.

**10. "How would you handle a mistake in your first month?"**
Strong: disclose early, correct, document, and explain what changed. Weak: hide it until asked.

**11. "How would you build a stakeholder map in your first month?"**
Strong: 12 to 20 conversations, influence and interest scored, cadence set, and a review at 30 days.
Weak: says they would read the org chart.

**12. "What would make you leave in the first year?"**
Strong: a specific condition such as no access, no mandate, or a safety concern. Weak: says nothing
would.

## 15. Key terms

- **30-60-90 plan** — a phased plan with milestones at 30, 60, and 90 days.
- **Access provisioning** — the process of granting system and data permissions.
- **Baseline** — current performance of a process or metric.
- **Credibility bank** — accumulated trust from reliable delivery.
- **Data custodian** — role accountable for a data source.
- **Data map** — inventory of sources, custodians, latency, quality, and access.
- **Data steward** — role responsible for data quality and definitions.
- **Decision map** — decisions the role influences and who owns them.
- **Handover** — transfer of a process or system between people.
- **Incident** — unplanned event affecting a service or decision.
- **Influence** — ability to change a decision without authority.
- **Interest** — how much a stakeholder cares about the outcome.
- **Mandate** — agreed scope and authority of the role.
- **Minimum necessary** — privacy principle limiting access to what is needed.
- **OKR** — objectives and key results.
- **On-call** — rotation responding to production issues.
- **Onboarding** — structured process of becoming effective in a role.
- **Post-incident review** — structured analysis after an incident.
- **Probation** — contractual period with different notice terms.
- **Quick win** — small, useful, low-risk deliverable in the first 60 days.
- **RACI** — responsible, accountable, consulted, informed.
- **Runbook** — operational instructions for a process or service.
- **Shadowing** — observing a role during real work.
- **Skip-level** — meeting with your manager's manager.
- **Stakeholder cadence** — how often you engage each stakeholder.
- **Stakeholder map** — people with influence and interest, and how to engage them.
- **STARS** — start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment, sustaining success.
- **Time to proficiency** — period before a new hire reaches full output.
