## Learning outcomes

Learners can define a training objective in terms of inputs, targets, conditioning context and loss; derive token-level cross-entropy; compare autoregressive, masked, denoising and contrastive objectives at a conceptual level; identify data/formatting requirements and failure modes; and distinguish pretraining loss from downstream task performance.

## 1. Objective, data, and evaluation are separate choices

A training objective specifies what the model predicts or optimizes from training examples. The training data specifies the examples and distributions. The evaluation protocol estimates performance on selected tasks or populations. These components interact, but none can substitute for another.

A low training loss may indicate that the model fits its training distribution under the selected objective. It does not by itself establish factuality, instruction following, safety, calibration, retrieval accuracy, or performance under deployment conditions. A benchmark score is meaningful only with a defined dataset, split, prompt/format, metric, and contamination boundary.

Before choosing an objective, define:

- the model input and conditioning context;
- the target variable or target positions;
- masking and weighting rules;
- the intended deployment task and expected distribution;
- the evidence required to evaluate useful behavior;
- data and compute constraints.

## 2. Conditional probability and next-token prediction

An autoregressive language model factorizes a token sequence into conditional next-token probabilities:

```text
p(x_1, ..., x_T) = product_{t=1..T} p(x_t | x_1, ..., x_{t-1})
```

The training objective commonly maximizes the log probability of observed next tokens, equivalently minimizes negative log-likelihood (NLL) or cross-entropy over target positions. The context is causal: token `x_t` is predicted from prior tokens, subject to the model's start/boundary convention.

For a context representation `h_t`, a linear output layer produces logits `z_t` over vocabulary size `V`; softmax maps logits to a distribution:

```text
p(x_t = j | context) = exp(z_{t,j}) / sum_{v=1..V} exp(z_{t,v})
```

Large logits do not directly mean high probability if competing logits are larger. Stable implementations compute log-softmax/cross-entropy without explicitly forming numerically unstable exponentials for every operation.

## 3. Cross-entropy derivation

For target token `y_t` and predicted distribution `p_t`, one-hot cross-entropy is `-log p_t(y_t)`. Across valid target positions, the token-average loss is the sum of these negative log probabilities divided by the declared denominator.

```text
FORMULA [OBJ-01]: L_token = -sum_{t in M} log p_theta(y_t | c_t) / |M|
WHERE: M = valid loss-bearing target positions; y_t = observed target token;
       c_t = context available under the objective; theta = model parameters.
CONSTRAINT: denominator and token mask must be stated; example-, token-, and weight-normalized losses differ.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE: target probabilities for three tokens are 0.5, 0.25, 0.1.
                   NLL sum = -ln(0.5)-ln(0.25)-ln(0.1)=4.3820 nats.
                   Mean NLL = 4.3820/3 = 1.4607 nats/token.
INTERPRETATION: lower NLL on the same target distribution means greater assigned probability on average; it is not a direct downstream quality score.
```

Independent check: the sequence probability under the chain rule is `0.5*0.25*0.1=0.0125`; `-ln(0.0125)=4.3820` nats, matching the sum. If the target positions or denominator differ, the reported mean changes even when per-token probabilities are unchanged.

Perplexity is `exp(mean NLL)` when the loss is measured in natural-log units over the same tokenization and target set. For this example it is `exp(1.4607) ≈ 4.31`. Perplexity depends on tokenizer, data, masking, and distribution; it is not directly comparable across mismatched setups.

## 4. Autoregressive language modeling

Autoregressive training predicts future tokens from a prefix. It naturally supports generation by repeatedly feeding generated tokens back as context. A causal attention mask prevents a position from using future target tokens during training.

Choices that affect the objective include:

- whether all tokens or only selected spans are loss-bearing;
- treatment of document boundaries and end-of-sequence tokens;
- whether examples are concatenated or isolated;
- token-level versus example-level loss normalization;
- context length and truncation;
- whether the model is trained on text, structured conversations, code, or multimodal streams.

The prediction task can be technically correct while the data is unsuitable or the model learns undesirable shortcuts. Inspect loss by data slice, source, length, language and token category; compare to held-out evidence.

## 5. Masked and denoising objectives

Masked language modeling (MLM) corrupts or hides selected input positions and trains the model to predict the original content at those positions, typically using bidirectional context where the architecture allows it. The corruption strategy and selected-target policy are part of the objective. MLM can be useful for representation learning, but its training interface differs from left-to-right generation.

Denoising objectives corrupt an input sequence and train a model to reconstruct the original or a target sequence. Corruption may remove, mask, permute, or span-corrupt content. The exact objective affects which dependencies are learned and how inference is performed.

Neither label is sufficient to reproduce an experiment. Record corruption function, target construction, special tokens, mask rate/span policy, data preprocessing, and architecture. A model trained with bidirectional context cannot automatically be evaluated as a causal generator without an explicit method and validation.

## 6. Contrastive and pairwise objectives

Contrastive objectives learn representations by scoring related examples above selected negatives or alternatives. Pairwise preference objectives compare outputs according to a label or preference relation. These objectives differ from token-level next-token likelihood in their target structure and data requirements.

Negative selection, temperature, sampling, pair construction, annotator instructions and batch composition can strongly affect contrastive training. Preference pairs can encode ambiguous or population-specific judgments. Evaluate representation or preference objectives on independent tasks and slices; do not assume an auxiliary loss automatically improves generative behavior.

The exact contrastive/preference formulas and optimization procedures are outside this submodule's detailed derivation. The core lesson: name the target, conditioning, sampling process and evaluation before interpreting a loss.

## 7. Objective choice by task

| Intended behavior | Candidate objective family | Key data/format requirement | Evaluation question |
|---|---|---|---|
| General text continuation | Autoregressive next-token | Ordered token stream and boundaries | Does held-out likelihood track useful language behavior on target slices? |
| Fill-in-the-blank representations | Masked prediction | Corruption mask and original-token targets | Are representations useful on independent downstream tasks? |
| Sequence reconstruction | Denoising/seq2seq | Input corruption and target alignment | Does reconstruction support the intended transfer behavior? |
| Similarity or retrieval embeddings | Contrastive/pairwise | Positive/negative pairs and sampling rationale | Does retrieval quality improve under a held-out query/corpus split? |
| Task-specific assistant behavior | Supervised targets under a chosen generative objective | Correct roles, targets, masks and template | Does behavior improve without unacceptable regressions? |

A large model can learn from multiple objectives or stages. The order matters: later training can overwrite or shift behavior from earlier stages. Track objective and data lineage per stage, and evaluate cumulative effects.

## 8. Loss masks, weights, and reported metrics

A loss mask selects target positions. For next-token training, the mask must align with the trainer's shift convention. Padding, prompt tokens, corrupted inputs, and non-target roles may be treated differently across objectives. Inspect actual labels after collation.

If token losses are weighted, report the weighting and reduction denominator. An example-average objective weights short and long examples differently from a token-average objective. Perplexity should only be calculated for an appropriately normalized token NLL, not from an arbitrary weighted or mixed loss without explanation.

Useful training diagnostics include training and held-out loss, loss by source/domain/language/length, token counts, target-mask counts, gradient health and learning-rate trajectory. These are diagnostics, not substitutes for task evaluations.

## 9. Objective mismatch and data leakage

Objective mismatch arises when training predicts a different target, uses different context or format, or optimizes a proxy that does not reflect deployment. Examples include training on plain text but serving a role-marked chat format without enough transition data; masking prompts when inference depends on learning prompt continuations; or optimizing a similarity loss while evaluating generation quality.

Leakage occurs when held-out targets or close derivatives enter training, tuning, prompt demonstrations, synthetic data generation, or iterative model selection. It can inflate apparent performance without improving generalization. Maintain split lineage and inspect high-scoring or suspicious examples for overlap.

Training and evaluation tokenizers must be compatible; otherwise, changes in segmentation alter both inputs and loss denominators. Compare matched tokenization and data versions whenever claiming objective or model improvements.

## 10. Compute proxy and objective cost

For dense autoregressive training, a commonly used rough compute proxy is `C ≈ 6ND`, where `N` is parameter count and `D` is the number of training tokens. It is a planning approximation under stated assumptions, not a profiler result. Architecture, activation checkpointing, attention implementation, non-embedding parameter conventions, sequence lengths, optimizer work, evaluation, communication and hardware utilization affect actual cost.

Illustrative values: `N=1e9` parameters, `D=20e9` tokens yield `C≈6*1e9*20e9=1.2e20` FLOPs. At an assumed sustained `1e14` FLOP/s, idealized arithmetic time is `1.2e6` seconds, or 333.33 hours (13.89 days). This excludes evaluation, checkpointing, data input, communication, downtime and utilization variation. The assumed sustained rate must be measured on the intended workload to estimate real elapsed time.

Different objectives may have different compute/memory costs and different useful token counts. A fair comparison states the budget unit: processed input tokens, target tokens, training FLOPs, wall time, accelerator-hours, monetary cost, or another operational constraint.

## 11. Failure diagnostics

| Symptom | Candidate causes | Discriminating check | Bounded response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Training loss falls but task success does not | Proxy mismatch; evaluation shift; data labels/format wrong | Held-out task metrics and per-slice error review | Change data/objective only after identifying the gap |
| Loss is unexpectedly low | Train/eval overlap; duplicate targets; target leakage into context | Split lineage, near-duplicate and mask audit | Repair evaluation boundary; remeasure without the contaminated set |
| Loss is high or unstable | Wrong labels/shift; learning-rate issue; malformed targets; numerical instability | Inspect tiny batch IDs/labels and per-step loss/gradients | Fix verified data or optimization defect; rerun controlled check |
| Perplexity comparisons disagree | Tokenizer, data, mask, denominator or log base differs | Recompute on identical text, tokens and normalization | Restrict comparison to matched conditions or clearly qualify it |
| Autoregressive model sees future target | Causal mask or packed-boundary bug | Sentinel future-token perturbation test | Correct attention/mask path and verify invariance |
| MLM/denoising model performs poorly at generation | Objective/architecture does not match generation interface | Evaluate intended transfer path and generation procedure | Use an objective compatible with deployment or validate a conversion method |
| Reported compute misses budget | `N,D` convention mismatch; proxy omits overhead; actual throughput lower | Count exact training targets and profile representative steps | Reforecast using measured utilization and all-in costs |
| One slice dominates average loss | Mixture imbalance or tokenization/quality differences | Slice token counts and losses by source/language/length | Investigate data and objective fit; report slice-level uncertainty |

## 12. Interview and self-check questions

### Q1. What does cross-entropy measure?
**Answer:** Negative log probability assigned to observed target labels under the model distribution, averaged according to a stated mask and denominator.
**Reasoning:** It rewards probability assigned to targets and penalizes low target probability.
**Follow-up:** Does lower training cross-entropy prove better deployment quality?
**Answer:** No; it is evidence about the measured target distribution only.

### Q2. Compute mean NLL for target probabilities 0.5, 0.25, and 0.1.
**Answer:** Approximately 1.4607 nats/token.
**Reasoning:** Sum of negative natural logs is 4.3820; divide by three.
**Follow-up:** What is the corresponding perplexity?
**Answer:** Approximately `exp(1.4607)=4.31`, for the same tokenization and target set.

### Q3. How does autoregressive training differ from masked prediction?
**Answer:** Autoregressive predicts next tokens from prior context; masked prediction reconstructs selected corrupted positions using its permitted context.
**Reasoning:** Target locations and context availability differ.
**Follow-up:** Are their losses directly comparable?
**Answer:** Not without matching target, data, tokenization, and denominator conventions.

### Q4. What does a loss mask specify?
**Answer:** Which target positions contribute to the training objective.
**Reasoning:** Input visibility and supervision are distinct.
**Follow-up:** What must be checked in a next-token trainer?
**Answer:** Whether labels are shifted internally and how ignored positions are reduced.

### Q5. Why can held-out loss be misleading?
**Answer:** The held-out set may be contaminated, mismatched, too narrow, or use a different tokenization/mask.
**Reasoning:** The estimate only applies to its governed distribution and protocol.
**Follow-up:** What strengthens it?
**Answer:** Sealed provenance, representative slices, matched tokenizer/objective and independent task measures.

### Q6. For `N=1e9`, `D=20e9`, what is the `6ND` proxy?
**Answer:** `1.2e20` FLOPs.
**Reasoning:** Multiply 6 by one billion parameters and 20 billion tokens.
**Follow-up:** At 100 TFLOP/s idealized, how long?
**Answer:** `1.2e6` seconds, about 333.3 hours; actual wall time is higher or otherwise workload-dependent.

### Q7. Why is `6ND` not a measured runtime?
**Answer:** It abstracts architecture and assumes a compute relation; it omits utilization, communication, I/O, evaluation and checkpoint overhead.
**Reasoning:** Hardware peak throughput is not sustained application throughput.
**Follow-up:** What should replace the assumption?
**Answer:** Profile representative training steps on the intended system and account for all stages.

### Q8. What makes a contrastive objective depend on negative selection?
**Answer:** The model is trained to distinguish positive relations from sampled alternatives, so negative distribution defines the learning signal.
**Reasoning:** Easy, false, or biased negatives change the objective.
**Follow-up:** How to evaluate?
**Answer:** Use independent task-relevant retrieval or representation benchmarks and audit data construction.

### Q9. What is an objective mismatch?
**Answer:** The training targets/context do not support the behavior measured or required at deployment.
**Reasoning:** Optimizing a proxy does not guarantee the target capability.
**Follow-up:** How do you identify one?
**Answer:** Trace the deployment failure to data, objective, format, or evaluation with controlled tests.

### Q10. What must be reported for a loss comparison?
**Answer:** Data, tokenizer, objective, target mask, denominator, model/context, and evaluation split.
**Reasoning:** Changing any of these can change loss independently of model quality.
**Follow-up:** Can perplexity compare different tokenizers directly?
**Answer:** Generally not without a carefully matched protocol and interpretation.
