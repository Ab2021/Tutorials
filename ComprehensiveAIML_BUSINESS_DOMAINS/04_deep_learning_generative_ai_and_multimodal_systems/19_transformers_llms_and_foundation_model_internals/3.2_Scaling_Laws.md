## Learning outcomes

Learners can describe empirical scaling relations and their limits; distinguish parameter, token, compute, data quality and evaluation effects; fit a simple power-law exponent to a stated dataset; compare model/data budgets under an explicit compute proxy; and avoid treating estimates from one regime as universal constants.

## 1. What a scaling law means

A scaling law is an empirical relationship between measured outcomes and quantities such as model parameters, training tokens, compute, or data quality over a defined experimental regime. The word “law” does not imply an exact physical invariant. A fitted curve summarizes measurements under specific architectures, objectives, tokenizers, data distributions, optimization settings, and evaluation procedures.

Scaling studies can help prioritize experiments and estimate resource needs. They do not replace measurement when a decision depends on a new domain, model family, context length, data mixture, training system, or deployment metric.

Always name:

- response variable: held-out cross-entropy, task score, or another outcome;
- predictor variables: parameters, unique/processed tokens, estimated/real compute, or data-quality measures;
- data and evaluation distribution;
- model family and training procedure;
- fit range and excluded runs;
- uncertainty and residual diagnostics;
- decision where the fit will be used.

## 2. Model, data, and compute axes

Let `N` denote a clearly defined parameter count, `D` a stated training-token count, and `C` a compute budget. A rough dense-training proxy is `C≈6ND` under common assumptions; actual compute depends on architecture, sequence length, attention implementation, training stage, and counting conventions. Using this proxy, fixed `C` constrains the product `N*D`, not a unique pair.

Two configurations can satisfy the same proxy while differing in undertraining, data repetition, optimization stability, memory feasibility, communication cost, and downstream quality. The compute constraint is a planning boundary, not a quality prediction by itself.

Data must be defined carefully: unique retained tokens, repeated exposures, loss-bearing tokens, and processed token positions are different quantities. A scaling fit that counts all replayed tokens may not match one that counts unique text. Record tokenizer and data mixture, not just `D`.

## 3. Power-law fit form and interpretation

A common empirical form for loss versus model size or data is a power law with a floor, for example:

```text
L(N) = L_inf + A * N^(-alpha)
```

Here `L_inf` is an asymptotic floor over the studied regime, `A` is a fitted scale parameter, and `alpha` is a fitted exponent. These coefficients depend on measurement units and experimental conditions. `N` must use the same units used in the fit. A fit over model sizes in billions cannot be evaluated with raw parameter counts unless the coefficient is transformed accordingly.

For joint model/data variation, a simplified expression may include both terms, such as `L(N,D)=L_inf + A*N^(-alpha)+B*D^(-beta)`. This is an empirical approximation; additional interactions, optimization effects, finite data quality, and distribution shifts can matter.

The floor is not automatically irreducible Bayes loss. It can absorb model misspecification, measurement noise, finite experiment range, and unmodeled data/optimization effects. Do not interpret it as an eternal lower bound.

## 4. Fitting an exponent

If the same response floor is known or fixed, then:

```text
log(L(N) - L_inf) = log(A) - alpha * log(N)
```

A straight-line fit in log space estimates a slope of `-alpha` for that restricted form. If `L_inf` is estimated from the same small dataset, it can be strongly correlated with `A` and `alpha`; different floors can produce different exponents. Validate residuals and use multiple runs, not two convenient points alone.

Illustrative synthetic data: let `N` be measured in billions of parameters, set `L_inf=1.5`, `A=0.8`, and `alpha=0.2`. At `N=1`, loss is `2.3`. At `N=2`, loss is `1.5 + 0.8*2^-0.2 ≈ 2.197`. The residual ratios are `0.8/0.697 ≈ 1.148`, matching `2^0.2 ≈ 1.149` within rounding. The exponent is constructed, not fitted from a model run.

A robust analysis considers repeated seeds, uncertainty intervals, fit sensitivity to endpoints, residual trends, and alternative model forms. Log transforms also change the effective weighting of errors; select the fit objective to match the measurement noise and decision needs.

## 5. Compute-optimal allocation is conditional

Compute-optimal scaling asks how to divide a fixed compute budget between model size and training data to minimize a specified loss under measured assumptions. The optimum depends on the fitted relationship, data/compute definitions, training efficiency, and the budget range. It is not an architecture-independent prescription.

A historical study may find that some models in its tested range were trained with fewer tokens than later compute-optimal analyses recommend. That conclusion motivates testing a different allocation; it does not establish that all future models should use one fixed tokens-per-parameter ratio.

Before applying a published result, compare its model family, parameter definition, tokenizer, data mixture, training objective, compute regime, optimizer schedule, and evaluation loss with the target setting. Report where the target lies inside or outside the fit range.

## 6. Same-compute illustrative comparison

Under the rough proxy `C≈6ND`, compare two illustrative choices:

- A: `N=1e9`, `D=20e9`; `ND=2e19`; `C≈1.2e20` FLOPs.
- B: `N=2e9`, `D=10e9`; `ND=2e19`; `C≈1.2e20` FLOPs.

The arithmetic says these have the same proxy compute under the assumptions. It does not say their losses are equal or identify the better allocation. That requires measured or otherwise defensible fits in the relevant regime, plus memory, data availability, optimizer, throughput, and downstream-quality constraints.

If the model is too large for available memory or fails to reach useful optimization progress in the chosen token budget, the nominal budget comparison is not actionable. If the data is repeated or low quality, `D` overstates distinct useful exposure. If actual sustained throughput differs, equal proxy FLOPs need not mean equal wall time or cost.

## 7. Experimental design for scaling estimates

A useful scaling experiment varies one or more resource axes while controlling or recording the rest. It should:

1. define an objective and held-out evaluation protocol before the sweep;
2. pin tokenizer, data snapshots, sampling, model family, optimizer and code revision;
3. choose a planned range with enough points to detect curvature and regime changes;
4. include repeated seeds or uncertainty estimates where feasible;
5. record actual training tokens, updates, compute, throughput, and failures;
6. measure loss and task performance by relevant slices, not only an aggregate;
7. account for model initialization, checkpoint selection and tuning budget;
8. reserve validation/test boundaries against selection leakage;
9. report exclusions, failed runs and fit residuals;
10. state the fit's scope and extrapolation distance.

Changing model size and data simultaneously can be necessary for fixed-compute studies, but it complicates causal interpretation. Use a design that answers the resource-allocation decision rather than pretending to isolate one factor when several changed.

## 8. Scaling loss is not scaling capability

Cross-entropy is a useful dense metric, but task capabilities can emerge, plateau, regress, or vary non-smoothly with benchmark setup. A small average-loss change may not produce a practical improvement; an aggregate task gain may hide regressions in rare languages, safety, factuality, or latency.

Measure multiple deployment-relevant outcomes, including task success, calibration where needed, robustness, refusal/safety behavior, latency, memory, and cost. Use confidence intervals or repeated evaluation where sampling noise matters. Avoid choosing a model solely because a fitted loss curve predicts a lower next-token loss.

Benchmark contamination, prompt engineering, evaluation tuning, and test-set reuse can all distort apparent scaling. Preserve evaluation provenance and independently review surprising step changes.

## 9. Data quality and distribution shifts

Scaling more tokens from a weak or mismatched source may not help the deployment objective. Data quality, source authority, deduplication, language coverage, time validity, target construction, and synthetic-data provenance affect the effective training signal. A quality shift can look like a token-count effect if not controlled.

Train/test distribution differences can alter the observed curve. Report performance by domain, language, time period, sequence length, and data-source strata where relevant. A global fit should not be treated as evidence for a small population or a new domain without slice measurements.

Tokenizer changes alter sequence length, target count, and NLL denominator. Tokenizer mismatch can make apparent token-efficiency comparisons invalid. If tokenization changes, either re-evaluate a compatible model and protocol or qualify that the comparison includes a tokenizer change.

## 10. Extrapolation and uncertainty

Extrapolation means predicting beyond the measured range. Uncertainty grows with distance from data, model-form uncertainty, hardware/algorithm changes, and shifts in data or evaluation. A narrow sweep cannot support precise forecasts several orders of magnitude away.

Use ranges, not false precision. Identify assumptions such as unchanged architecture, optimizer, data quality, tokenizer, and system efficiency. Compare alternative plausible fits and inspect how the target decision changes. If the decision is sensitive to model form or endpoints, run another experiment before committing the full budget.

Do not extrapolate across a changed objective or checkpoint regime without evidence. Continued pretraining, instruction tuning, and preference optimization are not interchangeable points on one pretraining scaling curve.

## 11. Failure diagnostics

| Symptom | Candidate causes | Discriminating check | Bounded response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fit predicts poor held-out values | Wrong functional form; distribution shift; implementation change | Residuals by scale/slice; inspect held-out regime | Restrict scope, revise fit with new data, report uncertainty |
| Estimated exponent changes sharply | Floor/endpoint sensitivity; too few points; mixed training regimes | Refit with alternate floor and leave-one-out analysis | Add runs or present a range rather than one coefficient |
| Same proxy compute has different runtime | Utilization, memory, sequence, communication or data bottleneck | Profile representative steps and all-in pipeline | Compare measured accelerator-hours/cost, not FLOP proxy alone |
| Larger model does not improve loss | Undertraining; bad data; optimizer instability; failed throughput | Check updates/tokens, learning curves, data quality and gradients | Correct confirmed bottleneck; do not assume model scaling alone fixes it |
| More tokens do not improve target task | Repeated/low-value data; evaluation mismatch; objective gap | Unique-token analysis and task-slice evaluation | Improve data/objective or stop spending on low-value scale |
| Aggregate loss improves but critical slice regresses | Mixture shift, tokenizer coverage or label quality | Per-slice loss and task outcomes | Add targeted checks and govern tradeoff; preserve regression gate |
| Published allocation fails on target system | Regime, architecture, tokenizer or compute convention mismatch | Reconcile experimental setup and target constraints | Use publication as hypothesis; run a local bounded sweep |
| Reported optimal ratio appears precise | Fit uncertainty ignored; definition mismatch; point extrapolated | Trace parameter/token definitions and confidence range | State scope and uncertainty; avoid universal ratio claims |

## 12. Interview and self-check questions

### Q1. What does a scaling exponent describe?
**Answer:** The slope parameter of a specified empirical model relating a response to a predictor over a defined regime.
**Reasoning:** It depends on units, response definition, floor and data range.
**Follow-up:** Is it a universal constant?
**Answer:** No; it is fitted under experimental assumptions.

### Q2. Why is an asymptotic loss floor uncertain?
**Answer:** It may absorb model misspecification, noise, finite range, and unmodeled effects, not only irreducible uncertainty.
**Reasoning:** A fitted intercept is not proof of a fundamental bound.
**Follow-up:** What should be reported?
**Answer:** Fit form, range, residuals, uncertainty and alternative-floor sensitivity.

### Q3. How do you estimate a power-law exponent with a fixed floor?
**Answer:** Fit `log(L-L_inf)` against `log(N)`; slope equals `-alpha` for the stated model.
**Reasoning:** Log transforms turn the power relation into a line.
**Follow-up:** Why not use two points only?
**Answer:** Noise, floor uncertainty and curvature can make a two-point slope unstable.

### Q4. Under `C≈6ND`, do 1B parameters/20B tokens and 2B/10B tokens have equal proxy compute?
**Answer:** Yes, both have `ND=2e19` and a `1.2e20` FLOP proxy.
**Reasoning:** Their parameter-token products match.
**Follow-up:** Are their losses equal?
**Answer:** Not implied; requires relevant measurement or validated fit.

### Q5. Why can tokens-per-parameter ratios mislead?
**Answer:** Token definitions, data quality, repetitions, objective, model family and compute regime differ.
**Reasoning:** A ratio compresses multiple assumptions into one number.
**Follow-up:** How should it be used?
**Answer:** As a hypothesis for a scoped experiment, not a universal recipe.

### Q6. What is a compute-optimal allocation?
**Answer:** A model/data choice minimizing a specified outcome under a compute and other resource constraint, given a fitted regime.
**Reasoning:** The optimum depends on data, model, objective and system assumptions.
**Follow-up:** What if memory is limiting?
**Answer:** The nominal compute optimum may be infeasible; include memory and throughput constraints.

### Q7. Why can aggregate held-out loss hide a bad scaling result?
**Answer:** It can improve on common data while regressing on a critical language/domain/task slice.
**Reasoning:** Weighted aggregates conceal distribution-specific outcomes.
**Follow-up:** What evidence is needed?
**Answer:** Slice-level loss and task evaluation under the target distribution.

### Q8. What does extrapolation risk depend on?
**Answer:** Distance beyond measurements, model-form uncertainty, regime changes, and data/compute/evaluation shifts.
**Reasoning:** The fit is empirical, not a law of nature.
**Follow-up:** How reduce risk?
**Answer:** Add targeted runs and test alternate fits before high-cost commitment.

### Q9. Why pin tokenizer and data when comparing scaling runs?
**Answer:** They change token counts, sequence lengths, target distributions and loss denominators.
**Reasoning:** Apparent scale effects can be tokenization/data effects.
**Follow-up:** What if changed intentionally?
**Answer:** Report it as a combined intervention and evaluate a compatible protocol.

### Q10. What should a scaling report include?
**Answer:** Fit form, data/model/compute definitions, range, evaluation, uncertainties, residuals, excluded runs and extrapolation scope.
**Reasoning:** Coefficients without context are not transferable.
**Follow-up:** Is one curve enough to decide deployment?
**Answer:** No; include task quality, system cost and risk evidence.

## Quick reference card

| Principle | Practice |
|---|---|
| Empirical scope | State objective, data, model family, tokenizer, compute and evaluation. |
| Power law | Fit only with declared units, floor, range and uncertainty. |
| Compute proxy | `C≈6ND` is a rough dense-training estimate; profile actual workload. |
| Allocation | Same compute does not imply same loss; measure resource tradeoffs. |
| Data | Distinguish unique tokens, repeated exposure, loss tokens and quality. |
| Fit quality | Inspect residuals, endpoints, seed variation and alternative forms. |
| Extrapolation | Use ranges and assumptions; run more experiments when decisions are sensitive. |
| Capability | Evaluate task, safety, slice, latency, memory and cost separately from loss. |
| Comparability | Pin tokenizer/data/objective and evaluation protocol. |
| Evidence | Synthetic values here; no scale sweep or model run measured. |
