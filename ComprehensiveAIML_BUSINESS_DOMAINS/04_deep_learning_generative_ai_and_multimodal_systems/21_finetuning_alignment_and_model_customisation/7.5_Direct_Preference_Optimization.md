## Learning outcomes

Given a prompt and preferred/rejected response pairs, learners can define the policy/reference log-ratio objective; derive its logistic preference form from a KL-regularized reward representation; calculate the implied preference probability and loss; explain beta and reference-policy roles; identify data, length and reference failure modes; and evaluate DPO against SFT or a reward-model/PPO baseline.

## 1. What DPO optimizes

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) trains a policy directly from labeled preference pairs using a frozen reference policy. It does not require a separately trained scalar reward model or an online PPO rollout loop in its basic form. Given a prompt `x`, a preferred completion `y_w` and a rejected completion `y_l`, DPO increases the policy's relative log probability for the preferred response versus the rejected one, measured against the corresponding reference-policy log probabilities.

DPO still requires valid preference labels and a meaningful reference checkpoint. It does not eliminate reward misspecification; it expresses preferences through a different training objective. It can inherit label noise, length bias, prompt leakage and rubric ambiguity, and it may regress outside the pair distribution. “Direct” means the preference objective is optimized without the conventional separate reward-model-plus-policy-optimization workflow, not that no reference or assumptions are used.

## 2. From KL-regularized reward to log-ratios

A common theoretical starting point considers a policy that maximizes expected reward while remaining close to a reference distribution under a KL penalty. Under a fixed prompt distribution, positive regularization coefficient `beta`, sufficient support, and the corresponding optimum, the regularized optimum has a Boltzmann form proportional to the reference policy multiplied by an exponential of reward divided by beta. Rearranging gives an implicit reward up to a prompt-specific additive normalization constant:

```text
FORMULA [DPO-01]: r(x,y) = beta * log( pi(y|x) / pi_ref(y|x) ) + C(x)
WHERE: pi is the candidate policy; pi_ref is a fixed reference policy;
       beta > 0 is the regularization/temperature parameter under this convention;
       C(x) is a prompt-dependent additive constant; r is an implicit reward.
CONSTRAINT: derives from the KL-regularized optimum and shared action support;
            beta convention varies across literature/code. It is a modeling
            identity/assumption, not evidence that an arbitrary trained policy is optimal.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE: ILLUSTRATIVE beta=0.1 and log(pi/pi_ref)=2;
                   implicit reward contribution excluding C(x)=0.2.
INTERPRETATION: relative log-probability change encodes a reward-like quantity
                under this formulation; the prompt constant cancels in same-prompt comparisons.
```

For preferred and rejected responses to the same prompt, `C(x)` cancels. Let `q_w = log pi(y_w|x) - log pi_ref(y_w|x)` and `q_l = log pi(y_l|x) - log pi_ref(y_l|x)`. The DPO logit is `z=beta(q_w-q_l)`. Substituting the implicit rewards into a Bradley–Terry preference likelihood produces a logistic loss:

```text
FORMULA [DPO-02]: z = beta * [ log(pi(y_w|x)/pi_ref(y_w|x))
                                 - log(pi(y_l|x)/pi_ref(y_l|x)) ]
                  L_DPO = -log sigmoid(z) = log(1 + exp(-z))
WHERE: y_w is labeled preferred; y_l is rejected; z dimensionless under the
       chosen beta/log-ratio convention; L is NLL [nats/pair].
CONSTRAINT: paired candidates share prompt and context; reference log-probabilities
            are computed with the same tokenization, serialization, masks and
            sequence scoring convention; use stable log-sigmoid computation.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE: beta=0.1, q_w=2, q_l=-1; z=0.1*(2-(-1))=0.3;
                   P_model(preferred)=sigmoid(0.3)≈0.5744;
                   L_DPO=log(1+exp(-0.3))≈0.5544 nats/pair.
INTERPRETATION: this pair's policy/reference log-ratios favor the preferred
                response by a modest implied margin under the selected beta.
```

The objective commonly aggregates this pair loss across a dataset. If each pair is weighted equally, take the mean over valid pairs; if prompts, annotators, sources or examples receive weights, state the weighting unit and denominator. Summed token log probabilities make response length relevant. A chosen/rejected pair with unequal lengths can be affected by length-related likelihood behavior even if per-token normalization is not explicit.

## 3. Worked log-ratio check

For one ILLUSTRATIVE pair, suppose the policy/reference log-ratio for the preferred response is `q_w=2` and for the rejected response `q_l=-1`, with `beta=0.1`.

1. Relative log-ratio: `q_w-q_l=2-(-1)=3`.
2. DPO logit: `z=0.1*3=0.3`.
3. Implied preference probability: `sigmoid(0.3)=1/(1+exp(-0.3))≈0.5744`.
4. Pair loss: `-ln(0.5744)≈0.5544` nats.
5. If the relative log-ratio reverses to `-3`, then `z=-0.3`, probability is approximately `0.4256`, and loss is approximately `0.8544` nats.

**Independent checks:** `sigmoid(-z)=1-sigmoid(z)`, so `0.4256≈1-0.5744`; the loss increases when the labeled preferred response receives a lower implied logit. A common additive shift to both responses' log policy/reference ratios cancels in `q_w-q_l`. Arithmetic verifies the logistic transformation only; it does not verify preferences or training outcomes.

## 4. Reference policy and beta

The reference policy supplies the baseline likelihood ratio. It is usually held fixed during a DPO run so that the objective's anchor does not move with every update. The initial policy is often initialized from the same checkpoint, commonly an SFT model, but the precise artifacts and tokenizer/template must be recorded. A mistaken or mismatched reference changes the objective even if the preference data is unchanged.

Under the displayed convention, `beta` multiplies the log-ratio difference. Changing beta changes the logit scale and therefore the gradient/saturation behavior. Some implementations or papers use reciprocal parameterizations or name the coefficient differently; compare equations, not parameter names. Do not assume a larger value always means “more conservative” without checking the exact convention and accompanying objective.

DPO has no independent explicit KL term in the pairwise logistic loss as written; its reference log-ratio is built into the objective's derivation. That does not guarantee a hard KL bound in a finite training run. Monitor actual policy/reference divergence and held-out behavior. Training duration, optimizer, data mixture and approximation can still move the policy substantially.

## 5. Preference data: examples define the target

A comparison is meaningful only with a stable rubric and matched prompt/context. Preserve the reason for choosing one response when available, but do not assume a free-form rationale is correct or part of the DPO loss. Specify:

- what preference dimension is labeled (correctness, groundedness, style, safety, etc.);
- how criteria conflict and how ties, abstentions and unjudgeable pairs are handled;
- whether both responses saw the same policy/source context and constraints;
- annotation population, blinding, display randomization and adjudication;
- data rights, privacy, provenance and policy/template version;
- exact/near-duplicate and prompt/source/time leakage controls;
- response generation source/version and any synthetic/AI-judge label provenance;
- length distribution, truncation, EOS handling and maximum context.

If the chosen response is longer in most pairs, the policy may learn length-associated token likelihood patterns even if the rubric intended concise correctness. Diagnose pairwise outcomes by prompt type and length; inspect log probability sums, any normalization and truncation effects. Adding length normalization is not a neutral correction: it changes the optimization target and must be tested rather than assumed.

## 6. Training and implementation checks

A DPO batch generally contains prompt tokens, preferred completion tokens and rejected completion tokens. For each completion, the trainer obtains policy and reference sequence log probabilities under a consistent response mask and computes the pairwise difference. Prompt tokens condition both completions but are ordinarily excluded from completion log-probability sums; the exact shift and EOS policy must match the intended objective.

Before a full run:

1. Confirm the policy and reference artifacts, tokenizer and chat template.
2. Inspect a small pair after tokenization/truncation; verify prompt equality and completion boundaries.
3. Verify that only intended completion tokens contribute and each model uses identical target IDs/masks.
4. Compute policy and reference log probabilities by hand on a tiny deterministic example and reconcile sign/order.
5. Evaluate initial DPO loss and one optimizer step; confirm expected preferred/rejected direction on a toy pair.
6. Confirm reference weights do not receive gradients or change.
7. Test unequal-length pairs, padding, EOS, empty/malformed completions and distributed reduction.
8. Track train/validation loss, log-ratio distributions, approximate KL, gradient norms and response lengths.

A low loss can reflect memorization, leakage or easy preference pairs. It is not sufficient evidence of better assistant behavior. Keep validation prompts independent and reserve test data for final decisions.

## 7. Evaluation and method choice

Compare DPO with the SFT baseline on the same held-out prompts and task contract. Use blinded preference evaluation and task-specific outcomes; include correctness/grounding, safety/refusal slices, response length, calibration where probabilities are used, and regression checks. Track divergence from the reference, training cost, throughput and failure rate. Report uncertainty with dependence-aware grouping by prompt/source/annotator.

DPO may be attractive when fixed preference pairs are available and avoiding a separate reward-model/PPO pipeline is useful. PPO may be appropriate where online sampling and reward-driven optimization are justified and controllable. SFT may be enough when desired behavior can be demonstrated directly. These methods differ in data, assumptions, compute and failure modes; benchmark them against the same baseline rather than treating one as a universal replacement.

Do not compare DPO's pairwise loss numerically with SFT token NLL or PPO reward as if they were the same metric. Their units, objectives and normalization differ. A DPO score/logit is not automatically calibrated preference probability for production or an absolute response quality value.

## 8. Failure modes and diagnostics

| Symptom | Candidate causes | Discriminating check | Bounded response |
|---|---|---|---|
| DPO loss falls but held-out preference does not improve | Prompt leakage, repeated pairs, overfit, noisy labels | Prompt/source-grouped split audit and independent blinded labels | Repair splits/data, collect discriminating pairs, retrain and preserve a fresh test set |
| Chosen response probability falls relative to rejected | Sign/order reversed, labels swapped, policy/reference logprob mismatch | Hand-calculate one pair's `q_w`, `q_l`, `z`, gradient direction and decoded label | Correct pair serialization/sign and run deterministic toy update before full training |
| Training loss is huge or non-finite | Incorrect beta convention, empty masks, extreme log ratios, unstable arithmetic | Inspect target counts, per-sequence logprobs, dtypes and stable softplus path | Fix objective/masks or numerical implementation; reject invalid batches explicitly |
| Responses become excessively long or short | Unequal length preference bias, summed token logprobs, EOS/truncation artifacts | Stratify preference/error by length; inspect token masks and completion boundaries | Fix data/template or test a justified length-aware objective; evaluate independently |
| Policy drifts far from reference | Long training, large updates, mismatched reference or data shift | Measure reference KL/divergence and compare artifact hashes/logprobs | Verify reference and reduce update pressure; stop if quality/guardrails regress |
| Model improves preference but fails task correctness | Rubric proxies style or annotators lack domain facts | Blind task evaluation with source-grounded or executable checks | Correct rubric/labels or retain SFT; preference alone is not acceptance |
| Performance reverses across groups | Aggregate labels hide subgroup preferences or data imbalance | Slice by declared population/task/language and annotate uncertainty | Narrow claim or collect representative labels; do not average away severe harm |
| Reference model unexpectedly changes | Optimizer includes reference parameters or state reused incorrectly | Hash/reference parameter check before/after; inspect grad and optimizer groups | Freeze and isolate reference; repeat from known-good state |
| Loss reduction changes across ranks | Different valid-token masks, pair counts or rank-local normalization | Compare per-rank pair counts, token counts and denominators | Use documented global weighting and assert equality/aggregation semantics |
| DPO has no gain over SFT | Preference signal is weak/redundant, wrong gap, or need is better served by retrieval/control | Compare failure examples and independent metrics by intervention | Retain simpler baseline; improve data or test alternative only if justified |

## 9. Bounded DPO decision

Use DPO for a narrowly stated task only after confirming that preference labels express the intended tradeoff, that fixed pairs cover relevant prompts, and that a frozen reference and independent evaluation are available. Define a baseline, budget, success threshold, safety/regression constraints, and rollback path before training. Compare equal data/compute budgets where possible and record when comparability is impossible.

Promote only if DPO improves held-out user/task outcomes within the declared guardrails and operational constraints. If only pairwise training loss improves, keep the result experimental. If preferred labels encode unsafe, stale or biased judgments, stop and repair the data specification rather than increasing epochs or beta.

## 10. Interview and self-check questions

### Q1. What log-ratio enters the DPO preference logit?
**Answer:** Beta times the difference between preferred and rejected policy/reference log-ratios.
**Reasoning:** `z=beta[(log pi_w-log pi_ref,w)-(log pi_l-log pi_ref,l)]`.
**Follow-up:** Why does a prompt-specific additive constant cancel?
**Answer:** Both completions share the same prompt, so it subtracts out.

### Q2. Given beta 0.1, preferred ratio 2 and rejected ratio -1, what is the logit?
**Answer:** `0.1*(2-(-1))=0.3`.
**Reasoning:** The difference is 3 before scaling.
**Follow-up:** What is the model preference probability?
**Answer:** `sigmoid(0.3)≈0.5744`.

### Q3. What is the pair loss for that logit?
**Answer:** `log(1+exp(-0.3))≈0.5544` nats.
**Reasoning:** Negative log of `sigmoid(0.3)`.
**Follow-up:** What happens to loss if the logit becomes -0.3?
**Answer:** It rises to about 0.8544 nats.

### Q4. Does basic DPO require a separate scalar reward model?
**Answer:** No; the objective directly trains from preference pairs relative to a reference policy.
**Reasoning:** Reward-like values are implicit in policy/reference log-ratios under the derivation.
**Follow-up:** Are preference labels still required?
**Answer:** Yes.

### Q5. Does DPO guarantee a hard KL bound?
**Answer:** No.
**Reasoning:** The reference log-ratio is in the objective, but finite optimization does not project the policy into a strict divergence set.
**Follow-up:** What should be monitored?
**Answer:** Actual reference divergence and independent quality/guardrail metrics.

### Q6. Why can response length bias DPO?
**Answer:** Completion sequence likelihood sums and truncation can vary with token count; preference labels may correlate with length.
**Reasoning:** Unequal lengths change the numerical log probability and learned signal.
**Follow-up:** Is length normalization automatically correct?
**Answer:** No; it changes the objective and needs evidence.

### Q7. Why keep reference weights frozen?
**Answer:** They define the baseline likelihood ratio and anchor in the objective.
**Reasoning:** If reference changes unintentionally, the optimized comparison changes.
**Follow-up:** How verify?
**Answer:** Check reference gradients/optimizer membership and parameter hashes.

### Q8. Why cannot DPO loss be compared directly with SFT NLL?
**Answer:** They have different objectives, units and reductions.
**Reasoning:** DPO is a pairwise logistic loss; SFT NLL is token likelihood under a target sequence.
**Follow-up:** What is a meaningful comparison?
**Answer:** Independent held-out task/preference quality, with equal-budget context.

### Q9. What does preference accuracy measure?
**Answer:** Agreement with labels on the evaluated pairs under their rubric and sample.
**Reasoning:** It does not prove correctness or universal preference.
**Follow-up:** Name a separate quality gate.
**Answer:** Grounded correctness, safety, or task success on an independent test.

### Q10. When should the team prefer SFT or a simpler control?
**Answer:** When demonstrations or deterministic controls solve the behavior reliably without added preference-optimization complexity.
**Reasoning:** Method choice should target the diagnosed gap and minimize unneeded risk/cost.
**Follow-up:** If DPO does not beat the baseline?
**Answer:** Retain the baseline and report no demonstrated gain.

## 11. Quick reference

| Component | Role | Verify |
|---|---|---|
| Preference pair | Defines preferred/rejected comparison under a rubric | Same prompt/context; labels, ties, provenance, leakage controls |
| Reference policy | Baseline for response log likelihood | Frozen artifact, tokenizer/template and mask parity |
| Beta | Scales log-ratio difference under the chosen equation | Confirm implementation convention; do not infer meaning from parameter name |
| DPO logit/loss | Pairwise logistic objective | Hand-check sign, preferred order, stable log-sigmoid and reduction |
