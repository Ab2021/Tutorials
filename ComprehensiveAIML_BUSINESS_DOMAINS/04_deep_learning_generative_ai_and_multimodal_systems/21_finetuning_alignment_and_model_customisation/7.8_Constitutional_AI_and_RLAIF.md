> **Status:** Draft. Constitutional AI (CAI) and reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF) are related but distinct concepts. Implementations vary; this lesson does not assert one canonical pipeline.

## Learning outcomes

Given a behavior policy and written principles, learners can distinguish principle-guided critique/revision from AI-generated preference labels; specify a testable constitution and feedback protocol; audit judge reliability, bias, self-reinforcement and source leakage; choose human, AI-assisted or hybrid feedback for a bounded task; and retain independent safety, quality and governance controls.

## 1. Terms and boundaries

A **constitution** is an explicit set of principles, rules or rubric criteria used to guide or evaluate model behavior. It should be versioned, scoped and testable. A written constitution is not a guarantee that a model follows it, that principles are mutually consistent, or that all relevant harms are covered.

**Constitutional AI (CAI)** refers broadly to approaches that use principles to shape model behavior, often including self-critique and revision or feedback-driven training. Exact phases, data and optimization objectives differ. **AI feedback (AIF)** means an AI system produces judgments or critiques for training/evaluation data. **RLAIF** uses AI-generated feedback in a reinforcement-learning-from-feedback pipeline; variants may train a preference/reward signal and optimize a policy. RLAIF is not synonymous with every CAI workflow: one describes a feedback source/optimization family, the other a principle-guided approach.

Keep four artifacts distinct:

1. **Constitution:** principles and precedence rules.
2. **Judge prompt/model:** process that applies principles to a particular case.
3. **Feedback label:** critique, ranking, score or approval attached to examples.
4. **Training objective:** SFT, pairwise preference loss, PPO or another method that consumes the feedback.

Changing one does not imply the others changed. Preserve this lineage so a behavior regression can be traced to principle text, judge version, feedback labels or optimizer.

## 2. Make principles operational

A principle such as “be helpful and safe” is too broad to audit by itself. Convert it into observable criteria with scope, examples, conflict ordering and escalation rules. Each criterion should define:

- behavior and population where it applies;
- positive examples, prohibited outcomes and ambiguous cases;
- evidence available to judge (prompt, supplied source, tool output, answer);
- severity and precedence over competing goals;
- label options: pass/fail, ordinal rating, pairwise preference, abstain;
- what counts as a justified refusal or safe alternative;
- review owner and version/change date.

Avoid averaging hard safety constraints into a compensatory score where high helpfulness can numerically cancel an unacceptable unsafe action. Use veto/guardrail criteria or independent gates where the risk requires it. Make principle conflicts explicit: privacy, accuracy, user autonomy and refusal may pull in different directions.

## 3. Principle-guided critique and revision

A critique/revision workflow can ask a model to inspect a candidate response against each relevant principle, identify a concrete violation or uncertainty, and propose a revision. The process may create training demonstrations or candidate labels. It can improve consistency with the supplied rules but may also reproduce the critique model's blind spots, over-refuse, invent violations or make a fluent but incorrect revision.

A reviewable trace should include:

- original prompt/context and candidate response;
- applicable principle IDs and constitution version;
- critique text, cited evidence and uncertainty/abstention;
- revised answer and which critique caused each change;
- independent validation result and reviewer/adjudication;
- whether the example enters training, evaluation or neither.

Do not treat self-critique as self-verification. The critic and reviser may share the same model, training data and systematic error. For factual tasks, compare claims to source material or a qualified independent checker; for safety cases, involve appropriately trained reviewers and adversarial test cases.

## 4. AI feedback and RLAIF loop

An AI-feedback process can generate one or more critiques, rankings or scores for model responses under a constitution. Those labels may feed supervised training, a reward model or a policy-optimization pipeline. A simplified lineage is:

1. Define task and constitutional rubric; freeze a version.
2. Generate candidate responses under a logged model/context configuration.
3. Present candidates and the exact available evidence to a judge model using a versioned prompt.
4. Collect judgment, rationale, uncertainty and abstention; validate against human-reviewed samples.
5. Exclude, adjudicate or separately route ambiguous/unsafe cases according to policy.
6. Build training pairs/targets with provenance and split protection.
7. Train the chosen objective (SFT, reward model/PPO, DPO, or another stated method).
8. Test the trained model with independent human/task checks, safety cases and out-of-distribution prompts.

A judge can be wrong, biased, prompt-sensitive, vulnerable to order/verbosity effects, or systematically aligned to its own preferred style. Using its own judgments recursively as both training signal and final evaluation creates circular evidence. Keep a held-out human/external test panel or task-grounded verifier and measure where judge labels disagree.

## 5. Constructed scoring example

Suppose a rubric has two non-compensatory checks for a response: (A) does not reveal a protected secret and (B) answers the allowed portion using supplied evidence. On an ILLUSTRATIVE response, an AI judge marks A=pass and B=pass, while an independent human reviewer marks A=pass and B=fail because one cited claim is unsupported. The disagreement is 1 of 2 checks for this response (50% item-level disagreement in this tiny constructed example), not a population estimate.

Do not average the two votes into “75% safe” or treat majority as truth. The safety criterion is a gate; the factual/grounding criterion has conflicting judgments and needs adjudication or a stronger source-grounded test. Record the failure, judge version, exact evidence seen, and final disposition. In a real study, report agreement and uncertainty over a sufficiently sampled, blinded set, with clustering by prompt and reviewer as appropriate.

**Arithmetic check:** one disputed check out of two is `1/2=50%` within the toy example. The denominator is two criteria on one response; it is not the number of users, requests, reviewers in deployment, or a reliable judge-accuracy rate. This count demonstrates why criteria and populations must be explicit.

## 6. Human, AI and hybrid feedback

| Feedback source | Potential use | Main risks / controls |
|---|---|---|
| Human expert/annotator | Nuanced judgments, domain interpretation, harm review | Cost, fatigue, inconsistency, expertise coverage; use clear rubric, calibration, blinding and adjudication |
| AI judge | Scale candidate triage or structured critique | Shared-model bias, prompt sensitivity, self-preference and correlated errors; audit on independent human gold and abstain on uncertainty |
| Hybrid | AI pre-labeling with human review of high-risk, uncertain or sampled cases | Automation bias, hidden selection bias and review overload; log routing, random audits and untouched human test set |
| Deterministic verifier | Exact schema, code execution, arithmetic or policy conditions with formal rules | Incomplete tests, false acceptance, environment differences and exploit paths; use hidden tests, secure sandbox and human review for semantic gaps |

Choose feedback based on task stakes, rubric verifiability, volume, expertise and error cost. AI feedback may reduce marginal labeling effort but does not remove data governance, model evaluation, human oversight or incident response. For high-impact or rights-affecting outcomes, automated feedback is not a substitute for applicable human accountability and legal review.

## 7. Failure modes and safeguards

| Symptom | Candidate causes | Discriminating check | Bounded response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Model becomes excessively cautious | Principle wording broad/absolute; judge penalizes benign risk; reward averages incompatible rules | Measure false-refusal rate on allowed prompts; inspect criterion-level critiques | Narrow scope and add allowed counterexamples; require safety and helpfulness gates separately |
| AI judge prefers its own style | Judge/model family correlated with candidates; verbosity or phrasing bias | Blind identity, reorder candidates, compare matched-content responses and human ratings | Use independent or diverse judges, calibrate rubric and maintain human review |
| Self-critique repeats the same error | Critic shares model knowledge/error; evidence absent; critique prompt ineffective | Compare critique with source/external checker and known adversarial cases | Add source-grounded evidence or independent reviewer; do not promote self-confirmation |
| Safety pass masks factual failure | Criteria are averaged or one score obscures another | Inspect per-criterion outcomes and failure severity | Use non-compensatory gates and independent factuality evaluation |
| Feedback quality degrades after judge update | Judge version/prompt changed; calibration drift | Versioned paired audit set and judge/human disagreement by slice | Freeze versions during dataset creation or revalidate/relabel affected examples |
| RLAIF reinforces a model's bias | Feedback generated from similar model or narrow population; synthetic data self-training | Audit label sources, demographic/task slices, style preference and counterexamples | Diversify independent evidence and human review; restrict claim/scope until validated |
| Judge is fooled by prompt injection in candidate | Candidate content treated as instructions; unsafe tool/context exposure | Adversarial candidate tests; separate untrusted response text from judge instructions | Harden judge prompt/schema and sandbox; verify with held-out attacks |
| Constitutional principles conflict | No precedence, scope or escalation rule | Construct paired cases exercising each conflict and get policy-owner review | Define priority/abstention; keep irreducible tradeoffs explicit rather than auto-scoring |
| AI-labeled evaluation appears unusually strong | Training judge also grades final model; feedback contamination | Evaluate with disjoint human panel or independent verifier and hidden prompts | Remove circular metric from promotion; preserve independent acceptance gates |
| Rationale is persuasive but label is wrong | Language fluency mistaken for evidence; model fabricates citations | Verify every cited source and compare rubric decision with underlying evidence | Treat rationale as audit artifact, not proof; require evidence pointers/independent checks |

## 8. Evaluation and governance

Before scaling automated feedback, create a judge-validation set with human labels, including routine, borderline, conflicting-principle, adversarial and out-of-scope examples. Blind judge identity where possible; randomize presentation; record abstention and confidence without assuming they are calibrated. Evaluate agreement by criterion and slice, false-positive/false-negative costs, rationale evidence accuracy, order sensitivity and changes after judge updates.

For a candidate trained on AI feedback, use:

- untouched human-reviewed or task-grounded test cases;
- separate allowed/disallowed/ambiguous behavior slices;
- source-grounding and factual checks where relevant;
- subgroup and language analysis with uncertainty and privacy review;
- regression tests against the pre-training baseline;
- canary rollout, monitoring owner, user-report channel and rollback trigger.

Preserve provenance: constitutional version, judge checkpoint/prompt, candidate generator, raw judgments, human audit, transformations, training objective and resulting checkpoint. Keep private/protected information out of feedback prompts unless authorized and necessary. Access-control the judge inputs and outputs; synthetic data can still contain memorized or sensitive content.

## 9. Choosing CAI/RLAIF versus alternatives

Choose principle-guided critique when rules can be written clearly and model revisions can be independently checked. Choose AI-generated comparative feedback when judgments are repeatable enough to scale, but retain human gold data and audit for correlated errors. Prefer SFT when high-quality demonstrations directly specify behavior; use a separately justified preference or policy-optimization objective when rankings matter and simpler alternatives fail. Use deterministic validators for properties that can be enforced exactly.

A bounded pilot should define one behavior, constitution version, judge, candidate set, independent reference labels, safety gates, compute/annotation budget and stop rule. If AI feedback disagrees materially with independent review or creates unacceptable subgroup/false-refusal harm, stop and revise the rubric/judge/data process. Do not respond to failed validation by merely generating more self-labeled examples.

## 10. Interview and self-check questions

### Q1. Is RLAIF the same as Constitutional AI?
**Answer:** No.
**Reasoning:** RLAIF describes a reinforcement-learning-from-AI-feedback family; CAI describes principle-guided approaches that may use critique/revision or feedback training.
**Follow-up:** Can a workflow include both?
**Answer:** Yes, if the principles guide AI feedback used in policy optimization.

### Q2. What does a constitution contribute?
**Answer:** Explicit criteria or principles for shaping/judging behavior.
**Reasoning:** It makes the intended policy more inspectable, though not automatically complete or followed.
**Follow-up:** What makes a principle operational?
**Answer:** Scope, observable criteria, examples, conflict rules and escalation/abstention behavior.

### Q3. Why not trust model self-critique as proof of correctness?
**Answer:** Critic and generator may share knowledge gaps, biases and systematic errors.
**Reasoning:** Agreement from a correlated model is not independent validation.
**Follow-up:** Give an external check.
**Answer:** Source-grounded verification, an independent expert or a secure deterministic test where applicable.

### Q4. In the toy audit, what is the disagreement fraction?
**Answer:** One disputed criterion out of two, or 50% for that single response's two checks.
**Reasoning:** `1/2=0.5`.
**Follow-up:** Is that judge accuracy?
**Answer:** No; it is not a population sample or a gold-standard estimate.

### Q5. Why use non-compensatory safety gates?
**Answer:** A high score on helpfulness should not numerically cancel an unacceptable harm.
**Reasoning:** Averaging allows tradeoffs that policy may forbid.
**Follow-up:** What should be separated?
**Answer:** Safety acceptance and task-quality metrics.

### Q6. What causes circular evaluation in RLAIF?
**Answer:** The same judge or model family generates training labels and grades final performance.
**Reasoning:** Shared errors can make the candidate appear successful by matching its judge.
**Follow-up:** What breaks the circle?
**Answer:** Independent human evaluation or task-grounded tests not used to train the policy.

### Q7. What is a useful AI-judge order-bias test?
**Answer:** Swap response presentation order while keeping content and prompt fixed.
**Reasoning:** Preference changes reveal position sensitivity.
**Follow-up:** What else can be controlled?
**Answer:** Blind model identity and match verbosity/content where possible.

### Q8. Should model confidence be treated as calibrated abstention probability?
**Answer:** No, not without independent calibration evidence.
**Reasoning:** Self-reported certainty can be systematically miscalibrated.
**Follow-up:** What should an abstention policy use?
**Answer:** A validated threshold or rule with measured coverage/error tradeoff.

### Q9. How do you respond to an AI-feedback update that increases false refusals?
**Answer:** Stop or roll back, inspect principle/judge changes, and re-evaluate on allowed-task slices.
**Reasoning:** Aggregate preference gains cannot erase a guardrail regression.
**Follow-up:** Is more self-generated data automatically a fix?
**Answer:** No; it may reinforce the same failure.

### Q10. What records make feedback data auditable?
**Answer:** Constitution and judge versions, prompt/context, candidates, raw labels/rationales, human audits, transformations and training lineage.
**Reasoning:** These distinguish principle, judge, label and optimizer failures.
**Follow-up:** Why retain disagreement?
**Answer:** It reveals ambiguity and uncertainty rather than hiding it behind a final label.

| Constitution | Version, scope, criteria, precedence, ambiguity and accountable owner |
