## Learning outcomes

Learners can distinguish the behavior policy, reference policy and value function; trace the RLHF rollout-to-update loop; derive the clipped probability-ratio surrogate; compute clipped and unclipped contributions for positive and negative advantages; explain reference-policy KL control; and diagnose reward hacking, policy drift, data/optimization bugs and mismatched token/sequence units.

## 1. RLHF with a learned reward signal

In a common RLHF pipeline, a supervised-fine-tuned (SFT) policy generates responses to prompts. A reward model scores those responses according to learned preference data. A reinforcement-learning optimizer then updates the policy to increase reward while controlling changes relative to a reference policy, often the SFT checkpoint. PPO is one possible policy-optimization algorithm; it is not the reward-model training objective and does not turn a learned score into ground truth.

Important model roles:

- **Current policy `pi_theta`:** the model being updated and used for future rollouts.
- **Behavior/old policy `pi_old`:** the policy that generated a rollout and whose action probabilities define the denominator of PPO's importance ratio for that update batch.
- **Reference policy `pi_ref`:** a usually frozen comparison model used by a KL regularizer or related drift penalty; it is not necessarily identical in role to `pi_old`, though initial checkpoints may match.
- **Reward model `r_phi`:** scores prompt-response behavior; it is a learned proxy with distribution limits.
- **Value function `V_psi`:** estimates expected return from a state/prefix to help compute lower-variance advantages; it is not the reward model.

A simplified cycle:

1. Sample prompts from a declared training prompt distribution.
2. Generate responses from the behavior policy and store tokens, masks and old log probabilities.
3. Score each response with the reward model and calculate explicit KL/control terms against the reference policy.
4. Construct token- or sequence-level rewards, returns and advantages using the configured value estimator.
5. Perform a bounded number of PPO updates on the collected samples, with clipping and other configured constraints.
6. Measure task quality, preference agreement, KL/drift, length, safety and optimization health on independent evaluations.
7. Refresh rollouts from the updated policy; record exact policy/reference/reward/value versions and data lineage.

The data reused for a PPO update is on-policy only relative to the policy that generated it; multiple optimization epochs increase reuse but can also move the current policy away from the behavior policy. PPO's clipped ratio is intended to limit a class of update changes; it does not guarantee safe or small distribution shift in every implementation or across epochs.

## 2. Probability ratio and clipped surrogate

For a sampled state/token prefix `s_t` and sampled action/token `a_t`, define the probability ratio between current and behavior policies:

```text
FORMULA [PPO-01]: rho_t(theta) = pi_theta(a_t | s_t) / pi_old(a_t | s_t)
WHERE: pi_theta and pi_old are action/token probabilities conditional on the same
       state/context; rho is dimensionless; t indexes an action in a rollout.
CONSTRAINT: denominator probability > 0 and old log-probability corresponds to
            the actual rollout policy, tokenizer, mask and temperature path.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE: old probability 0.20, new probability 0.25;
                   rho=0.25/0.20=1.25.
INTERPRETATION: under the new policy, this sampled action has 1.25 times its old
                probability for that state; the ratio alone is not an absolute
                quality or sequence-probability claim.
```

Let `A_t` be the advantage for the sampled action and `epsilon` a positive clipping parameter. The commonly used clipped surrogate is maximized:

```text
FORMULA [PPO-02]: L_clip(theta) = E_t[min( rho_t*A_t,
                                      clip(rho_t,1-epsilon,1+epsilon)*A_t )]
WHERE: A_t = estimated return minus baseline/value [reward units]; epsilon is
       dimensionless; expectation is over sampled rollout positions and masks.
CONSTRAINT: maximize this surrogate; implementations often minimize its negative.
            The min has sign-sensitive behavior when A_t < 0. This clipping term
            is not a hard constraint that every policy probability stays in range.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE: rho=1.25, epsilon=0.2, A=2; clipped rho=1.2;
                   min(1.25*2,1.2*2)=min(2.5,2.4)=2.4.
INTERPRETATION: for this positive-advantage sample, increasing the ratio beyond
                the upper clip no longer increases the clipped objective.
```

For `A_t<0`, the min selects the smaller (more negative) term. With `rho=1.25`, `epsilon=0.2`, `A=-2`, the unclipped term is `-2.5`, the clipped term is `-2.4`, and the minimum is `-2.5`. Thus PPO clipping does not symmetrically cap every contribution in both directions. It is a surrogate-objective design, not a projection or universal trust-region guarantee.

## 3. KL control relative to a reference policy

The policy may be rewarded for producing outputs that the learned reward model likes, while a reference-policy penalty discourages excessive drift from a known starting behavior. A conceptual objective is:

```text
FORMULA [PPO-03]: J(theta) = E[ R(x,y) - beta * K(x,y) ]
WHERE: R is scalar response reward [reward units]; K is a nonnegative KL-like
       divergence estimate or penalty [nats/sequence or nats/token]; beta is a
       coefficient converting the penalty to reward units.
CONSTRAINT: exact estimator, sign, token/sequence reduction, masking and whether
            the penalty is added to per-token reward or final response reward
            are implementation-specific and must be specified.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE: ILLUSTRATIVE R=1.5, K=0.4 nats and beta=0.5 reward/nat;
                   shaped value=1.5-(0.5*0.4)=1.3 reward units.
INTERPRETATION: stronger beta increases the cost of divergence under this
                convention; it trades reward pursuit against reference proximity.
```

The KL estimate may be computed on sampled tokens or by another estimator; implementations differ. Do not assume a logged “KL” is exact full-distribution divergence, nor that it has the same reduction as reward. Record estimator, token mask, response-length treatment, coefficient, target/adaptation rule and update cadence. A coefficient that is too small can permit reward hacking/drift; too large can suppress the behavior change or cause optimization to stall.

The KL term's units and reduction matter. If a sequence receives one terminal reward but incurs a per-token KL penalty, longer sequences can accumulate more penalty. If reward is normalized per token while KL is a sequence sum, changing response length changes their relative weights. State whether each is per token, per response, normalized by valid tokens, or scaled otherwise.

## 4. Returns, advantages and value baseline

A reward may be assigned per token, at end of response, or from a mixture. A value model estimates expected future return from each state/prefix. An advantage estimator compares observed returns with the value baseline, optionally using temporal-difference residuals and generalized advantage estimation (GAE). The exact return convention, discount, lambda, terminal handling and KL shaping affect the policy gradient; they must match the token/action masks and episode boundaries.

A simplified advantage definition is `A_t = G_t - V(s_t)`, where `G_t` is the return from time `t`. This lesson does not prescribe a particular GAE variant. In language tasks, the response is a sequence of actions with variable length; padding, early stop, truncation and terminal rewards must not be treated as real actions/rewards. Value predictions should be tested against independently computed returns on a toy trajectory.

Advantage normalization (subtracting batch mean and dividing by a batch standard deviation) can stabilize optimization but changes sample weighting and behavior for small or mixed groups. Record the normalization population, epsilon, mask, and zero-variance handling. Do not conflate a normalized advantage with the reward model's score or a calibrated user preference.

## 5. Worked clipped-surrogate example

ILLUSTRATIVE sampled action has `pi_old=0.20`, `pi_new=0.25`, positive advantage `A=2`, and `epsilon=0.2`.

1. Probability ratio: `rho=0.25/0.20=1.25`.
2. Clip interval: `[0.8,1.2]`; clipped ratio `1.2`.
3. Unclipped contribution: `rho*A=1.25*2=2.5`.
4. Clipped contribution: `1.2*2=2.4`.
5. PPO maximization contribution: `min(2.5,2.4)=2.4`.

Now change only the sign of the advantage: `A=-2`. The unclipped contribution is `-2.5`, the clipped contribution is `-2.4`, and the minimum is `-2.5`. This asymmetry follows from `min`; implementations that report a negative loss reverse the sign for minimization, so inspect whether a metric is the maximization objective or its negation.

**Independent checks:** ratio is dimensionless (`probability/probability`); clipping bounds are `0.8` and `1.2`; multiplication yields reward-scaled objective terms; `min(2.5,2.4)=2.4`, `min(-2.5,-2.4)=-2.5`. The example does not show a full minibatch gradient or prove that a policy update obeys a hard bound.

## 6. End-to-end RLHF experiment design

A credible comparison starts with an SFT baseline and a fixed prompt distribution. Freeze an independent held-out evaluation set and rubric before training. Record every component checkpoint: tokenizer, policy, behavior policy, reference policy, RM, value model, rollout generator and data preprocessing.

For each rollout/update, retain enough lineage to reproduce:

- prompt ID/provenance and any authorized context;
- sampled response tokens, stop reason, truncation and action mask;
- behavior-policy log probabilities and sampling temperature/top-p or other decoding settings;
- reward model output and any independent reward components;
- reference-policy log probabilities and KL estimator/reduction;
- reward shaping, value predictions, returns and advantages;
- current/old/reference model versions and optimizer/update count;
- clipping fraction, entropy, gradient norm, value loss, reward mean/variance, KL and response length;
- skipped/invalid rollouts, filtering policy and all exclusions.

Evaluate on multiple axes: independent human preference under a fixed rubric; task correctness/grounding; policy and safety boundary regressions; held-out distribution slices; verbosity/refusal behavior; generated lengths; KL/drift; training stability; and equal-budget/time-to-quality compared with SFT. A rising reward mean alone is not an acceptance criterion.

Use staged experiments: first validate reward/value data flow and toy ratios, then a small controlled policy update, then longer training only if independent evaluation supports it. Keep a baseline checkpoint and a predefined stop/rollback trigger. Do not deploy a policy that only performs well on prompts sampled by its own training loop.

## 7. Common failure modes

| Symptom | Candidate causes | Discriminating check | Bounded response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reward increases while human/task quality falls | Reward hacking, RM distribution shift, rubric mismatch | Blind independent evaluation; inspect high-reward failures and RM uncertainty | Stop or revert policy; improve RM/evaluation or reduce optimization pressure |
| KL rises sharply | Beta too small, bad reference log-probs, reduction/mask mismatch, policy update too large | Verify reference identity/logprobs and exact KL mask/units; inspect per-token and per-sequence trends | Correct implementation or bound update; validate reward-quality tradeoff before resuming |
| KL stays near zero and behavior does not change | Beta too high, stale rollout, no policy gradients, reward signal weak | Check policy/reference checkpoint identity, gradient norms, rollout freshness, reward variation | Repair data/update path or tune one control under an independent evaluation plan |
| Clip fraction near zero or near one | Ratios remain tiny, or updates repeatedly hit clip range; stale old log-probs | Compare new/old logprob on unchanged policy and inspect ratio histogram | Fix policy snapshot/logprob computation; adjust epochs/step size based on measured diagnostics |
| PPO loss has unexpected sign | Logger reports negative objective; action/label sign or advantage convention reversed | Hand-check one sample's `rho`, `A`, unclipped/clipped values and reduction | Correct sign/convention; validate a unit test before expensive training |
| Policy collapses to short/long answers | Reward length bias, per-token versus terminal reward mismatch, EOS/length handling | Plot reward, KL, length and quality by prompt; inspect stop and token masks | Correct reward/termination/reduction policy; test on length-controlled slices |
| Value loss explodes or advantages are extreme | Incorrect reward scale, return boundaries, padding mask, value target mismatch | Trace toy sequence returns and compare value targets/masks; inspect scale quantiles | Fix reward/terminal/mask semantics and normalize only with documented convention |
| Same prompt leads to inconsistent updates across ranks | Different data masks, reward normalization, skipped update or collective ordering | Compare per-rank prompt IDs, denominators, finite flags and optimizer steps | Synchronize rollout/update validity; fail fast on divergence and test resumed state |
| Results depend strongly on seed | Small prompt sample, noisy reward/labels, unstable optimization | Repeat controlled runs and report spread; compare per-prompt outcomes | Increase evidence or reduce claim; do not report only the best run |
| RM high scores favor unsafe completions | Preference labels/rubric omit harm constraints; adversarial gap | Safety-specific independent challenge set and expert review | Stop; repair reward/safety objective and retain hard external policy controls |

## 8. Is PPO warranted?

PPO adds a reward model, rollout generation, value estimation, policy optimization, KL control and operational monitoring. It is warranted only if a measured behavior gap cannot be adequately addressed by SFT, prompting, retrieval, deterministic checks or a simpler preference method, and if the team can provide valid feedback plus independent evaluation. Complexity is not evidence of alignment.

A bounded decision compares the SFT baseline and PPO candidate on the same prompts, task constraints, budget accounting and held-out review. State what benefit would justify PPO (for example, better performance on a defined multi-criterion behavior without safety regression), what is unacceptable, and when to stop. Include compute/annotation costs and failure recovery. If no independent metric improves or tradeoffs violate policy, retain the baseline.

## 9. Interview and self-check questions

### Q1. What is the PPO probability ratio?
**Answer:** Current-policy action probability divided by behavior/old-policy probability for the same state and sampled action.
**Reasoning:** It reweights sampled actions under the updated policy.
**Follow-up:** What makes it invalid?
**Answer:** Stale/mismatched old log probabilities, different tokenization/masks, or a zero denominator.

### Q2. Compute the positive-advantage clipped term for ratio 1.25, epsilon 0.2, A=2.
**Answer:** `min(2.5,2.4)=2.4`.
**Reasoning:** Ratio clips to 1.2; multiply each candidate by 2 and take minimum.
**Follow-up:** Is 1.2 a hard policy constraint?
**Answer:** No; it clips this surrogate contribution.

### Q3. What does the minimum choose when A=-2 in the same example?
**Answer:** `min(-2.5,-2.4)=-2.5`.
**Reasoning:** Negative advantage reverses the effect of multiplying by ratio.
**Follow-up:** Is clipping symmetric for both signs?
**Answer:** No; the min objective is sign-sensitive.

### Q4. What is the difference between old and reference policy?
**Answer:** Old policy generated the rollout and defines the PPO ratio denominator; reference policy anchors a drift/KL penalty.
**Reasoning:** Their functions differ even if initial weights happen to match.
**Follow-up:** Which one is typically frozen as a comparison anchor?
**Answer:** The reference policy.

### Q5. What does a reward model provide?
**Answer:** A learned score proxy trained from preference data under a rubric.
**Reasoning:** It represents observed labeled comparisons, not objective truth.
**Follow-up:** What must validate policy quality?
**Answer:** Independent task/human/safety evaluations.

### Q6. Why monitor KL and reward together?
**Answer:** Reward can rise while policy moves far from the reference or exploits the RM.
**Reasoning:** The pair reveals reward/drift tradeoffs but neither alone proves quality.
**Follow-up:** What should be done if proxy reward rises as task quality falls?
**Answer:** Stop/revert and investigate the reward or optimization mismatch.

### Q7. How can response length create a unit mismatch?
**Answer:** Terminal sequence reward and per-token KL/reward reductions scale differently with length.
**Reasoning:** The same response-level reward can be offset by more token-level terms on longer outputs.
**Follow-up:** What should the experiment record?
**Answer:** Reward/KL units, masks, reduction denominator and length distribution.

### Q8. Why is PPO clipping not a trust-region guarantee?
**Answer:** It clips the surrogate terms for sampled actions, not every probability/distribution change across epochs and states.
**Reasoning:** The objective is a proxy for update control.
**Follow-up:** What else should be monitored?
**Answer:** KL, ratio distribution, clip fraction, independent quality and safety metrics.

### Q9. Why preserve rollout log probabilities and model versions?
**Answer:** To compute valid ratios and reconstruct exactly which policy generated each sample.
**Reasoning:** Without lineage, an apparent update defect may be impossible to distinguish from stale or mismatched data.
**Follow-up:** Name two additional fields.
**Answer:** Token masks and sampling settings, or reference/RM checkpoint IDs.

### Q10. When is SFT a better choice than PPO?
**Answer:** When demonstrations directly teach the behavior and satisfy the evaluation without needing an online reward-driven update.
**Reasoning:** PPO adds feedback, rollout and optimization complexity.
**Follow-up:** What if SFT fails?
**Answer:** Diagnose the gap and evaluate preference methods against the same baseline; do not assume PPO is the next step.

## 10. Quick reference

| Component | Role | Validate |
|---|---|---|
| SFT policy | Starting behavior and often reference checkpoint | Held-out task/guardrails and artifact identity |
| Behavior policy | Generates current rollout | Stored logprobs, sampler settings, tokenization and masks |
| Reward model | Scores prompt-response samples | Independent human/task agreement, calibration limits and OOD behavior |
| Value function | Estimates return baseline | Toy-return tests, value loss, masks and target scale |
| PPO ratio/clipping | Shapes policy update objective | Hand-computed positive/negative examples, ratio/clip statistics |
| KL control | Penalizes drift relative to reference | Estimator, units, reduction, coefficient and reward-quality tradeoff |
| Promotion gate | Determines whether policy is useful and safe | Independent held-out quality, safety, regressions, cost and rollback |
