## Learning outcomes

Given pairwise response preferences, learners can define the Bradley–Terry reward likelihood, derive its logistic loss, compute a preference probability from a score difference, explain reward-score non-identifiability and calibration limits, design leakage-resistant preference data and evaluate annotator/judge reliability; and identify when reward modeling is not warranted.

## 1. What a reward model represents

A reward model (RM) maps a prompt and candidate response to a scalar score `r_theta(x,y)`. In a common preference-learning setup, raters compare two responses to the same prompt and label which is preferred under a rubric. The model is trained so that preferred responses tend to receive higher scores than rejected responses. The training objective captures relative ordering in the labeled comparisons; it does not make the output a calibrated probability, a universal quality scale, or a complete specification of user welfare.

Reward models are often used as components in a later optimization process, including RLHF with PPO. That downstream use is separate: the reward-model objective learns from comparisons; PPO updates a policy using sampled reward and other control terms. A high held-out preference accuracy is not proof that optimizing the model's scores improves task quality or safety.

A reward-model dataset should record prompt, candidate responses, selected response (or tie/abstention if supported), rubric/version, annotator/judge, provenance, time, model/prompt versions when generated, and any adjudication. Store comparisons as comparisons; do not silently convert ranking labels into absolute-quality values.

## 2. Pairwise likelihood derivation

For a prompt `x`, let `y_w` denote the response preferred by the label and `y_l` the other response. A Bradley–Terry model assumes the preference probability depends on the difference of scalar rewards:

```text
FORMULA [RM-01]: P(y_w ≻ y_l | x) = sigmoid( r_theta(x,y_w) - r_theta(x,y_l) )
WHERE: r_theta(x,y) is a real-valued score [arbitrary reward units];
       sigmoid(u)=1/(1+exp(-u)); ≻ denotes preference under the stated rubric.
CONSTRAINT: binary pairwise comparison, same prompt/context and rubric,
            annotator choices modeled by this logistic difference; label order
            is correct and the comparison is informative.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE: score difference d=1.2 gives P=1/(1+exp(-1.2))≈0.7685.
INTERPRETATION: the fitted model assigns about 76.85% preference probability to
                the higher-scored response under this model, not a universal
                probability that it is objectively better.
```

For a labeled preferred response `y_w`, the negative log likelihood is:

```text
FORMULA [RM-02]: ell_pair = -log sigmoid(d) = log(1 + exp(-d))
WHERE: d = r_theta(x,y_w) - r_theta(x,y_l); ell_pair is NLL [nats/comparison].
CONSTRAINT: use numerically stable log-sigmoid/softplus implementations;
            unweighted formula assumes one binary preference per pair.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE: d=1.2 gives ell=log(1+exp(-1.2))≈0.2633 nats.
INTERPRETATION: a correctly ordered pair receives lower loss as the score gap
                grows, but increasing the gap beyond label evidence can overfit.
```

For a batch of `N` comparisons, mean pairwise loss is `sum_i ell_i/N` when examples have equal weight. Weighted or grouped losses alter the objective and must state the weighting unit. Reversing the preferred/rejected order changes the sign of `d` and therefore trains the opposite ranking.

## 3. Constructed numerical example

Suppose one rubric asks which of two answers better follows a supplied policy while staying grounded in the excerpt. Annotators prefer response A to response B; the reward head scores them 2.0 and 0.8, respectively. Then `d=2.0-0.8=1.2`, and the Bradley–Terry probability is about 0.7685. The pairwise NLL is about 0.2633 nats. If the predicted order were reversed with scores 0.8 and 2.0, then `d=-1.2`, probability for the labeled preference is about 0.2315 and loss about 1.4633 nats.

**Independent checks:** `sigmoid(-d)=1-sigmoid(d)`; thus `0.2315≈1-0.7685`. Pair losses are negatives of logs of probabilities: `-ln(0.7685)≈0.2633`; `-ln(0.2315)≈1.4633`. These are calculated from the model assumption and constructed scores, not annotation evidence or validation results.

The score difference—not either absolute score—determines this preference probability. Add a constant `c` to both scores and `d` is unchanged. The overall reward offset is therefore unidentifiable from pairwise data alone. The scale may also change with parameterization, regularization, dataset and optimization; it is not calibrated just because a sigmoid can transform a difference into a number in `[0,1]`.

## 4. Preference collection and label quality

The rubric defines the meaning of “preferred.” A rubric can ask for correctness, helpfulness, grounding, style or a weighted combination; vague rubrics produce inconsistent labels and obscure tradeoffs. Define which dimension wins when criteria conflict, how to treat ties, unsafe content, missing evidence, verbosity and answer length. Raters need enough prompt and source context to judge the intended behavior.

Collection controls:

- sample prompts from the intended use distribution and declared challenge slices;
- compare responses to the same prompt and provide equal relevant context;
- blind model identity and randomize presentation order where possible;
- preserve ties, abstentions and unjudgeable cases instead of forcing a false binary;
- use independent repeated judgments on a planned subset to estimate agreement;
- record rubric version and adjudication without erasing original votes;
- inspect annotator disagreement and examples with ambiguous policy;
- exclude or separately handle personally identifying or unauthorized data;
- split by prompt/source/user/time and response-generation family to limit leakage.

Inter-rater agreement is not label truth. Agreement can be high on a flawed rubric; disagreement can expose legitimate preference variation rather than careless annotation. Report the actual protocol, population and uncertainty. If the intended product has multiple user groups or values, an average single reward can hide systematic tradeoffs.

## 5. Model construction and training controls

A typical pairwise RM uses a pretrained or SFT model backbone and a scalar scoring head over a defined sequence representation, such as a designated final response position. Exact architecture varies. The prompt and response must be serialized consistently; scores should be taken from the intended response boundary, not padding or the wrong turn. The training loss compares the two scores for each prompt.

Controls to specify:

- initialization checkpoint, tokenizer/template and scoring position;
- train/validation/test grouping and duplicate-contamination checks;
- response-length distribution and any length normalization/penalty;
- optimizer, learning rate, batch unit, weight decay, regularization and stopping rule;
- ties/abstentions, label weights, annotator or group treatment;
- score normalization/calibration only if justified by a separate held-out procedure;
- reproducible manifests, exact preference pairs and checkpoint identity.

A pairwise loss can prefer longer, more verbose or stylistically polished answers if labels do. Length-normalizing scores is not an automatic fix: it changes the utility being learned and may penalize legitimately complete answers. Diagnose by stratifying preference outcomes and score differences by response length and task type, then test a rubric/data/model change against held-out labels and task outcomes.

## 6. Evaluation: ranking, calibration and task relevance

Separate at least three questions:

1. **Pairwise ranking:** Does the model order held-out pairs in agreement with labels under the same rubric? Report accuracy or log loss, and uncertainty, by slice.
2. **Calibration:** Do predicted probabilities match empirical preference frequencies for a defined population and grouping? Test on independent judgments; raw sigmoid scores do not guarantee calibration.
3. **Task relevance:** Do high-scored responses meet external task requirements such as correctness, grounding, safety, or user success? Evaluate independently of RM labels.

Use prompt-disjoint held-out data. If responses from one generation model or one near-identical prompt occur in both train and test, leakage can inflate ranking metrics. Include hard near-ties, distribution-shift slices, response length, languages, refusal/allowed boundaries, and human-review disagreements. Confidence intervals should respect clustering by prompt or annotator rather than treating every comparison as independent when it is not.

Audit score behavior: within-prompt differences, between-prompt offsets, score variance, response-length correlation, prompt-template artifacts and sensitivity to paraphrases. Absolute scores across unrelated prompts may not be comparable. PPO may optimize comparisons among samples for a prompt, but batching, normalization and KL constraints still affect how scores drive updates.

## 7. From reward model to policy optimization

An RM can provide a scalar signal to a policy optimizer. This creates an optimization loop: the policy explores outputs, the RM scores them, and policy updates favor higher-scored outputs. If the RM is imperfect, the optimizer can find outputs that exploit the proxy. Increasing optimization pressure can move the policy away from the RM's training distribution, where its judgments are less reliable.

Practical boundaries:

- keep a reference policy or another explicit baseline for measuring drift where the selected method requires it;
- monitor KL/divergence, response length, refusal, factuality, safety and task metrics as training proceeds;
- evaluate with fresh human or task-grounded judgments not used to train the RM;
- cap or stop optimization when proxy reward rises while independent outcomes degrade;
- consider offline preference methods when online sampling/optimization is unjustified, while evaluating their own assumptions;
- do not expose high-impact actions to learned reward alone; preserve deterministic controls and human review.

This is a handoff to PPO and other preference-optimization methods, not a derivation of those algorithms. Reward is measured in arbitrary units; mixing token-level and sequence-level rewards without explicit normalization changes the objective and can produce length bias.

## 8. Failure modes and diagnostics

| Symptom | Candidate causes | Discriminating check | Bounded response |
|---|---|---|---|
| High train pair accuracy, weak held-out accuracy | Prompt leakage, duplicate pairs, overfit, narrow label coverage | Grouped dedupe/split audit; evaluate by unseen source/time/prompt | Rebuild independent splits; collect targeted labels and retrain |
| Reward prefers verbose answers | Rubric favors perceived helpfulness; length correlates with selected labels; score pooling artifact | Stratify pair outcomes and score gaps by length; compare matched-length pairs | Clarify rubric or rebalance valid examples; retest held-out and task outcomes |
| Reward rises during policy optimization but task quality falls | Reward hacking or out-of-distribution policy samples | Fresh blind human/task evaluation; inspect high-reward failures and RM uncertainty | Stop/revert policy optimization; tighten data/objective or reduce optimization pressure |
| Labelers disagree strongly | Ambiguous rubric, missing context, genuine value tradeoff | Repeat blinded ratings; review disagreement by criterion and subgroup | Clarify criterion, retain ties, report uncertainty; do not force false consensus |
| Predicted 0.8 probabilities occur much less often | Miscalibration, shifted distribution, clustered or biased labels | Reliability curve on independent pairs with cluster-aware uncertainty | Recalibrate only on suitable data or treat score as ranking-only; validate after shift |
| Model ranks stylistic polish above correctness | Rubric omission, annotator expertise/context limitation | Gold task checks and adversarial factual examples | Add correctness criteria and expert review; maintain separate safety/task gates |
| Absolute scores are used to compare unrelated prompts | Pairwise training identifies within-prompt differences, not global scale | Shift both rewards within prompt; test cross-prompt offset variation | Compare candidates within same prompt or establish separate calibrated scale |
| Preference order flips with response position | Presentation/order bias, inconsistent preprocessing | Re-label randomized presentation; audit pair swap invariance | Fix collection/preprocessing; preserve prompt and label semantics |
| Reward model learns model-identity artifacts | Unblinded responses or generator-specific style leakage | Blind identity; evaluate cross-generator and human-authored responses | Remove artifacts/recollect labels and split by generation source |
| No useful policy gain despite reliable ranking | RM optimizes wrong criterion; policy sampling/KL constraints dominate | Compare RM ranking to task metric and inspect policy update path | Revisit target decision; keep SFT/baseline if reward optimization adds no value |

## 9. Decision checklist

Train an RM only when a clearly defined preference criterion exists, valid comparisons can be collected, and the downstream decision benefits from a learned ranking signal. Prefer direct task metrics, rules, SFT or an offline method when they better match the requirement or avoid unnecessary optimization complexity.

Before using an RM in policy training, require:

- rubric with explicit criterion and conflict/tie handling;
- prompt/source/annotator-aware data splits and contamination audit;
- repeatability and uncertainty estimates for human/judge labels;
- held-out ranking and calibrated-probability checks where probabilities are used;
- independent task-quality, safety and regression evaluation;
- monitoring for reward hacking, distribution shift, length bias and policy drift;
- stop/rollback conditions and owner.

If the model's score is described as “quality,” state the population, rubric and limitations that give the word meaning. Otherwise call it a learned reward score.

## 10. Interview and self-check questions

### Q1. What does Bradley–Terry model predict?
**Answer:** The probability that one response is preferred over another as a logistic function of their reward-score difference.
**Reasoning:** Only the difference enters the likelihood.
**Follow-up:** Is that a calibrated probability automatically?
**Answer:** No; calibration requires independent empirical checks.

### Q2. If the preferred response score exceeds the other by 1.2, what is the model preference probability?
**Answer:** `sigmoid(1.2)≈0.7685`.
**Reasoning:** `1/(1+exp(-1.2))`.
**Follow-up:** What is the pairwise NLL?
**Answer:** `log(1+exp(-1.2))≈0.2633` nats.

### Q3. What happens if the two scores are both increased by 10?
**Answer:** The predicted pairwise probability and loss are unchanged.
**Reasoning:** Their difference is unchanged.
**Follow-up:** What does this imply?
**Answer:** Absolute score offset is not identified by pairwise comparisons.

### Q4. Does 90% held-out preference accuracy establish factual correctness?
**Answer:** No.
**Reasoning:** It establishes agreement with labels for that sample/rubric, not independent factual ground truth.
**Follow-up:** Name an independent check.
**Answer:** Source-grounded task evaluation or expert-reviewed correctness set.

### Q5. Why randomize answer order during annotation?
**Answer:** To reduce and measure presentation-position bias.
**Reasoning:** Raters may prefer first/last responses independent of content.
**Follow-up:** What should be audited?
**Answer:** Whether pair labels flip systematically with display order.

### Q6. Why group test splits by prompt/source?
**Answer:** Related comparisons can leak content and inflate held-out performance.
**Reasoning:** Multiple pairs from one prompt are correlated.
**Follow-up:** How should uncertainty account for this?
**Answer:** Cluster by prompt or other dependence unit.

### Q7. Why might PPO exploit a reward model?
**Answer:** It optimizes the model's imperfect proxy, potentially finding high-score outputs unlike the training distribution.
**Reasoning:** Optimization pressure selects for proxy weaknesses.
**Follow-up:** What monitoring can detect it?
**Answer:** Independent task metrics, human review, distribution drift and reward-quality disagreement.

### Q8. What is the danger of comparing raw RM scores from two unrelated prompts?
**Answer:** Pairwise data may not identify a common absolute scale across prompts.
**Reasoning:** Prompt-specific additive offsets cancel in within-prompt comparisons.
**Follow-up:** What comparison is safer?
**Answer:** Compare candidate responses under the same prompt and rubric.

### Q9. How should ties be handled?
**Answer:** Define a principled tie/abstention policy and preserve ambiguity rather than inventing a binary preference.
**Reasoning:** Forced labels add noise and may distort the target.
**Follow-up:** What if the chosen loss cannot model ties?
**Answer:** Filter with documented rules or use a suitable tie-aware method, then validate the changed objective.

### Q10. When should RM training or policy optimization stop?
**Answer:** When labels are unreliable, held-out ranking is weak, or proxy reward improves while independent task/safety outcomes decline.
**Reasoning:** A proxy score is not the end objective.
**Follow-up:** What is a valid fallback?
**Answer:** Retain the SFT/baseline policy and improve the rubric, data or evaluation before another attempt.

## 11. Quick reference

| Question | Required answer |
|---|---|
| What preference is modeled? | Pairwise choice under a named rubric and population |
| What is optimized? | Logistic likelihood of labeled within-prompt score differences |
| Are scores absolute quality? | No; offset/scale and cross-prompt comparability need separate justification |
| Is ranking enough? | No; check calibration and independent task/safety outcomes |
| Can higher reward be harmful? | Yes; policy optimization can exploit proxy errors or shift out of distribution |
| What makes the evidence credible? | Prompt/source-aware splits, blinded reliable labels, uncertainty and independent evaluation |
