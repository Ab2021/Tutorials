# Common issues: optimisation and simulation

> **Evidence.** Every number in this sheet was recomputed here from a stated formula: the interval instance is enumerated exhaustively over all `2^6 = 64` subsets, the queue figures come from the closed-form `M/M/1` expression, and the replication count is the normal-approximation formula evaluated directly. All job values, arrival rates, service rates, and defect rates are **ILLUSTRATIVE** construction, not measurements of any organisation. Where a statement is a claim about the world rather than an arithmetic result it is labelled as an inference. No solver benchmark, no vendor comparison, and no production incident is claimed.

## 1. File Header

> **Purpose.** Turn a failed or distrusted optimisation or simulation result into a located defect. The sheet is organised as a triage: each of the thirty catalogued issues carries a symptom you can observe in output or logs, at least two competing causes that produce the *same* symptom, a check that discriminates between them, a correction, a prevention, and a verification that would catch a recurrence.
> **Prerequisites.** You can read an LP or MIP log and identify the incumbent, the best bound, and the gap; you can state the grain and the timestamp of a table; you can compute a confidence interval from a sample mean and standard deviation; you can explain why `f_i + g <= s_j` is a different feasibility relation from `f_i <= s_j`.
> **Key outcome.** Given an unexpected result, you can say which of the five layers — contract, data, model, algorithm, inference — is responsible, name the cheapest probe that separates the leading candidate causes, and state in one sentence what the corrected answer would be. You can also refuse, with a reason, to call a heuristic answer optimal and to call a simulation conclusion significant when the run was not replicated.
> **Exclusions.** This sheet does not teach formulation from scratch (sheets 01 and 05), solver selection (02), experiment design (03), or the interval algorithms themselves (07). It assumes those and asks only: what went wrong, and how would you know.
> **Takeaway.** Almost every "the optimiser is wrong" report is one of five things: a constraint that never entered the model, a unit or endpoint convention that differs between two systems, a numerical parameter chosen by default rather than by scaling, a statistical claim made from one run, or a heuristic that was described as an optimum.

## 2. Concept Map / Mental Model

The failures in this unit are not uniformly distributed. They cluster in five layers, and the layer is identifiable from *what the output looks like*, before any code is read.

```text
LAYER 1  CONTRACT      what was agreed vs what was coded
         endpoints, buffer, units, timezone, eligibility, snapshot time
         signature: plan is internally feasible and operationally wrong

LAYER 2  DATA          what the model was given
         missing constraints, cycles, leaks, stale snapshot, late arrivals
         signature: model says "optimal" and the plan violates a known rule

LAYER 3  MODEL         what the mathematics can express
         linearisation, big-M, integrality, objective proxy, symmetry
         signature: bound and incumbent far apart, or gap closed at a bad answer

LAYER 4  ALGORITHM     what the procedure guarantees
         greedy on the wrong objective, heuristic labelled optimal, state explosion
         signature: answer is stable, reproducible, and wrong by a measurable factor

LAYER 5  INFERENCE     what a run licenses you to claim
         warm-up, replication, correlation, common random numbers, mean vs tail
         signature: conclusion flips when the seed or the run length changes
```

The mental model that makes the triage fast: **a defect is located by the cheapest observation that is inconsistent with the healthy state.** Feasibility replay at the boundary takes seconds; a full model audit takes days. So the order is always boundary, then units, then the model record, then the algorithm, then the statistics — and each step must *rule something out*, not merely look plausible.

Two structural facts drive most of the catalogue and are worth carrying as invariants:

1. **Adding a constraint can only shrink the feasible set, so an optimum can only fall.** If a reported optimum *rose* after a constraint was added, the two runs did not share a model. This single check catches stale builds, forgotten recompiles, and mismatched input snapshots.
2. **Relaxing integrality can only enlarge the feasible set, so an LP bound can only be at least the integer optimum.** If an "LP relaxation" returns a value *below* the integer optimum, the relaxation was not a relaxation: a constraint was dropped, not relaxed.

```text
   relax integrality ─────────────► bigger set, better objective  (bound valid)
   add a constraint ──────────────► smaller set, worse objective  (monotone down)
   raise a price    ──────────────► better objective for maximisation (monotone up)
   tighten a tolerance ───────────► same or fewer feasible points (monotone down)
```

Four monotonicity arrows. Each is a one-line assertion you can put in a test, and each is violated by a specific, common defect. When a reviewer asks "how do you know the model is right", the honest answer is not "it matches intuition" but "it satisfies these four directions, and here are the instances where each direction would have failed".

The second half of the model is a **diagnosis loop**, because a triage that is not closed by verification is just a guess with headings:

```text
   observe symptom
        |
        v
   list >= 2 causes that predict it      (else you have a guess)
        |
        v
   choose probe with max likelihood ratio per minute of effort
        |
        v
   outcome changes the belief?  --no--> probe is downstream of the suspect transform
        |yes
        v
   correct at the artefact level (contract/data/model/code), not at the output level
        |
        v
   verify: symptom gone AND no other metric moved AND monotonicity battery still holds
```

The "no other metric moved" clause is what separates a correction from a suppression. Removing a constraint that caused infeasibility makes the plan feasible and also makes it non-compliant; the symptom is gone and the defect is worse.
