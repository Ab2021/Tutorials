
## 10. Key Theorems / Results Reference

The results below are the load-bearing ones for graph evaluation. Each gives the statement, the assumption it needs, why it holds, what it licenses you to claim, and the misquotation it is most often subjected to.

### 10.1 Precision is prevalence-dependent; recall is not

**Statement.** For a fixed selection rule with sensitivity `TPR` and false-positive rate `FPR`, the positive predictive value is `PPV = TPR*pi / (TPR*pi + FPR*(1-pi))`, a strictly increasing function of prevalence `pi` for fixed `TPR > 0`, `FPR > 0`. Recall equals `TPR` and is independent of `pi`.

**Assumption.** The selection rule's `TPR` and `FPR` are stable across prevalences being compared — true when the rule is a fixed threshold on a score whose conditional distributions do not change, false when the score was fitted on the higher-prevalence population.

**Why it holds.** Bayes' rule on the two-by-two table, with `P(pos | flagged) = P(flagged | pos) P(pos) / P(flagged)` and `P(flagged) = TPR*pi + FPR*(1-pi)`. Increasing `pi` raises the numerator linearly and the denominator sublinearly because the `FPR*(1-pi)` term shrinks.

**What it licenses.** Reporting precision only with the prevalence at which it was measured. At `TPR = 0.20`, `FPR = 0.003182`: `pi = 0.005` gives PPV `0.240`, `pi = 0.001` gives `0.059`, `pi = 0.02` gives `0.571`.

**Common misquotation.** "Precision was 24 percent in the pilot, so expect 24 percent at the new subsidiary" — where the subsidiary's prevalence is 0.001, the expectation is 5.9 percent.

### 10.2 Capacity-restricted evaluation is a prefix property

**Statement.** For any ranking and any capacity `k`, `P@k` depends only on the multiset of labels in the first `k` positions. Therefore any two models that agree on the top `k` are indistinguishable at `P@k` regardless of what they do below `k`.

**Assumption.** Ties resolved identically on both sides; otherwise the prefix is not well defined.

**Why it holds.** The definition is an average over the prefix. This is trivially true and it is the reason the metric is honest: it cannot be improved by anything outside the region the operation consumes.

**What it licenses.** Claims of the form "with 50 reviews per day, model A finds more confirmed cases than model B". It does not license claims about the model's overall quality, and it does not transfer to a different `k`.

**Common misquotation.** Reporting `P@50` as "precision" and then using it to justify a queue of 400, where the actual precision is `26/400 = 0.065`.

### 10.3 AP is the expectation of precision at a random positive's rank

**Statement.** `AP = E[ precision@r ]` where `r` is the rank of a uniformly chosen positive, and `precision@r` is measured at that positive's rank. Equivalently `AP = (1/P) * sum_i P_i` where `P_i` is the precision at the rank of the i-th positive.

**Assumption.** No ties, or ties resolved by a stated rule; positives counted only within the evaluated list.

**Why it holds.** The sum in the definition is literally the sample mean of `precision@r` over the `P` observed positive ranks.

**What it licenses.** A single number summarising ordering quality over the whole list, comparable between models evaluated on the same list length and the same number of positives.

**Common misquotation.** Using AP as a capacity metric. A model can gain 0.05 AP by improving positions 300-400, which changes nothing operationally, and lose nothing in AP by having its top 3 in a bad order.

### 10.4 The rank-sum identity for AUC

**Statement.** With ascending ranks `r_1..r_P` of the positives among `n` units, `AUC = (sum r_i - P(P+1)/2) / (P*Q)`.

**Assumption.** No ties; with ties, use mid-ranks and interpret the result as `P(score_pos > score_neg) + 0.5 P(equal)`, which is the Mann-Whitney `U` statistic normalised.

**Why it holds.** `sum r_i - P(P+1)/2` counts, for each positive, how many negatives are ranked below it; summing over positives counts concordant pairs, and dividing by all `P*Q` pairs gives the probability.

**What it licenses.** Computing AUC in `O(n log n)` from one sort, which is why libraries use it rather than the quadratic loop. It also makes the tie correction explicit, which the quadratic loop hides.

**Common misquotation.** Treating AUC as invariant to class balance in a way that makes it a substitute for precision. It is invariant to class balance by construction, which is precisely why it cannot answer capacity questions (`0.6.3`).

### 10.5 Hypergeometric variance of `TP@k`

**Statement.** Under random selection of `k` units without replacement from `N` containing `P` positives, `Var[TP_k] = k*pi*(1-pi)*(N-k)/(N-1)`.

**Assumption.** Labels are exhaustive for the population; selection is uniform; no clustering of positives (which would inflate variance).

**Why it holds.** `TP_k` is hypergeometric; the finite-population correction `(N-k)/(N-1)` appears because sampling is without replacement.

**What it licenses.** A chance-baseline interval. At `N = 12,000`, `k = 50`, `pi = 0.005`, the standard deviation of `TP` is `0.498`, i.e. `0.010` in precision units. Any claimed gain smaller than about two of those units is inside chance variation.

**Common misquotation.** Using the binomial variance, which at these parameters is `0.2488` against the correct `0.2477` — a trivial difference here, but at `N = 1,000`, `k = 200` the factor is `(800/999) = 0.801` and the binomial overstates the standard deviation by 12 percent, making a real improvement look insignificant.

### 10.6 Label maturity bounds precision from below and above

**Statement.** Let `m` be the fraction of the window with terminal labels, `p_c` the precision on confirmed units, and `p_u` the unknown precision among immature units. Then true precision `p = m*p_c + (1-m)*p_u`. With `m = 0.5667` and `p_c = 0.24`, `p` ranges over `[0.136, 0.661]` as `p_u` ranges over `[0,1]`.

**Assumption.** Maturity is independent of case characteristics — false if reviewers triage easy cases first, which they usually do, so the realistic direction is that immature cases are harder and `p_u` is lower than `p_c`.

**Why it holds.** Total probability over the partition {terminal, immature}.

**What it licenses.** A statement of the form "precision is 0.24 on the 57 percent of cases that are resolved; the unresolved 43 percent could move it anywhere in [0.14, 0.66], and the direction of likely bias is downward".

**Common misquotation.** Coding immature as negative, which forces `p_u = 0` and reports 0.24 as if it were the whole story — or, worse, reports `m*p_c` as precision without saying so.

### 10.7 Calibration is not ranking, and ECE depends on binning

**Statement.** Any strictly increasing transform of a score preserves all ranking metrics and destroys calibration. ECE with equal-width bins and ECE with equal-count bins differ on the same data whenever the score distribution is skewed.

**Assumption.** ECE is computed on the population where the probabilities will be used; a calibration map fitted on a filtered queue does not transfer.

**Why it holds.** Ranking metrics depend only on order, which monotone transforms preserve; calibration compares values with frequencies, which they do not preserve.

**What it licenses.** You may use AP, NDCG and AUC without calibrating. You may not set a decision threshold from expected cost without calibrating, and you may not quote ECE without stating the binning.

**Common misquotation.** "The model is well calibrated, ECE 0.03" computed with 20 equal-width bins on a score distribution where 95 percent of mass sits in the first bin — the number then describes one bin's fit.

### 10.8 Entity-resolution evaluation needs B-cubed, not pairwise F1

**Statement.** For predicted clustering `C` and gold `G` over `N` records, `B3_precision = (1/N) sum_i |C_i ∩ G_i| / |C_i|` and `B3_recall = (1/N) sum_i |C_i ∩ G_i| / |G_i|`. Pairwise F1 and B-cubed disagree systematically: B-cubed weights each *record* equally, pairwise weights each *pair* equally, so a huge cluster dominates pairwise metrics quadratically.

**Assumption.** Gold clusters are complete and mutually exclusive; partial gold standards need a modified treatment.

**Why it holds.** The per-record ratio is the share of the record's predicted cluster that is correct (precision) and the share of its true cluster that was captured (recall), averaged over records.

**ILLUSTRATIVE check.** Six records, predicted `{1,2,3}, {4}, {5,6}`, gold `{1,2}, {3,4}, {5,6}`. Per record, `|C_i ∩ G_i|`, `|C_i|`, `|G_i|` give:

```text
record  pred   gold   |C∩G|  |C|  |G|   prec    rec
1       123    12      2     3    2    0.6667  1.0000
2       123    12      2     3    2    0.6667  1.0000
3       123    34      1     3    2    0.3333  0.5000
4       4      34      1     1    2    1.0000  0.5000
5       56     56      2     2    2    1.0000  1.0000
6       56     56      2     2    2    1.0000  1.0000
sum                                   4.6667  5.0000
B3 precision = 4.6667 / 6 = 0.7778
B3 recall    = 5.0000 / 6 = 0.8333
```

Note record 4: the predicted singleton is entirely correct (precision 1.0) but captures only half of its true cluster of two. Pairwise metrics cannot express that asymmetry — a singleton has no pairs, so it contributes nothing to pairwise recall — which is exactly why B-cubed exists.

**What it licenses.** Comparing two entity-resolution configurations on the same gold standard, with the gold standard's coverage stated.

**Common misquotation.** Quoting pairwise F1 near 0.99 on a dataset where one predicted cluster contains 60 percent of records: pairwise F1 rewards the enormous cluster because most pairs inside it are true pairs.

### 10.9 The candidate-set ceiling theorem (trivial, universally ignored)

**Statement.** If the candidate generator admits a fraction `c` of the positives, then for every ranker `R` and every capacity `k`, `recall_k(R) <= c`. Consequently a ranker comparison conducted on the candidate set cannot detect a ceiling imposed by the generator.

**Assumption.** The generator runs before the ranker and its output is a superset of what the ranker sees.

**Why it holds.** Recall counts reached positives over all positives; unreached positives are never in the list, so the maximum is `c` by construction.

**What it licenses.** Reporting `recall@k / c` as "share of reachable positives captured" alongside the unconditional recall, and treating generator coverage as its own KPI.

**Common misquotation.** "Recall 0.20, so the model misses 80 percent of fraud" when `c = 0.8667` and the honest statement is "the pipeline reaches 17 percent of the fraud that exists and captures 23 percent of what it reaches".

### 10.10 Temporal split validity is a necessary condition, not sufficient

**Statement.** A temporal split at `t_d` guarantees no future label enters training, but it does not prevent future edges from entering *test* features if those features are computed on a graph built after `t_d`. Validity requires the snapshot timestamp and the decision timestamp to coincide per origin.

**Assumption.** Each origin has its own snapshot; sharing one snapshot across origins reintroduces leakage for the earliest origin.

**Why it holds.** Feature computation is a function of the snapshot; if the snapshot postdates the origin, the function's arguments did not exist at that origin.

**What it licenses.** A claim that no record with `t_a > t_d` influenced the score, verifiable by an audit of the snapshot's edge timestamps.

**Common misquotation.** "We used a temporal split, so there is no leakage", followed by a single snapshot used for all six origins.

### 10.11 Selection bias has no unbiased estimator without audit data

**Statement.** Given labels only on selected units, no estimator of population precision is consistent without additional assumptions; the bias is identified only by sampling unselected units.

**Assumption.** None needed for the impossibility; the audit sample needs exchangeability between sampled and unsampled unselected units, which random sampling provides.

**Why it holds.** The labelled sample is drawn from a region of feature space chosen by the incumbent policy; the target quantity integrates over a region with no observations.

**What it licenses.** Reporting a bound from an audit sample with an interval, or reporting precision conditional on review explicitly, or running a randomised holdout.

**Common misquotation.** "We backtested on all historical fraud cases" — a sample consisting only of cases the old system flagged, i.e. a sample of the old system's beliefs.

### 10.12 The metric-comparison validity condition, in one line

**Statement.** A comparison is interpretable only if the two arms share unit, population, window, capacity, tie rule, label maturity and comparator protocol; the observed difference must exceed the interval induced by the denominator, which for capacity `k` and prevalence `pi` has standard deviation at least `sqrt(pi(1-pi)/k)` under any selection scheme no better than random.

**Why it holds.** Each shared element removes one alternative explanation; the interval is the sampling floor.

**What it licenses.** A defensible sentence: "at 50 reviews per day, on the population defined by P-3, over the 45-day window with 57 percent maturity, the graph model found 12 confirmed cases against 7 for the incumbent; the 95 percent interval on the difference includes zero, so we treat this as a promising but unproven improvement and plan five weeks of parallel running."

**Common misquotation.** Any version of that sentence with a clause deleted.

## 11. Interview / Exam Questions

Questions are grouped by the skill they test: recall of definitions (R), computation (C), diagnosis (D), and judgement (J). At least half require arithmetic or a diagnostic decision. Each question carries its own answer; the scoring line states what a strong response contains.

**Q1 (R).** Define precision at capacity and explain why it, rather than AUC, is the metric a review queue should report.

**Answer.** `P@k = TP_k/k`: confirmed positives among the top `k` selected units, with `k` set by reviewer throughput. AUC integrates over all thresholds, most of which the operation never applies; the queue consumes exactly one prefix, so the metric must be evaluated on that prefix. Reporting AUC for a capacity decision measures a quantity the operation does not spend.

**Reasoning.** The metric must be a function of the decisions actually taken. `k` is an integer fixed by labour, so the evaluation is a prefix property (10.2), and any metric integrating outside the prefix is answering a different question.

**Follow-up.** If the queue sometimes runs short and reviews only 30 of the 50 slots, what changes? (Answer: the metric becomes the realised `k`, and `P@50` overstates the achieved hit rate; report the realised prefix length distribution.)

**Red flag.** Saying "AUC is standard" without connecting the metric to the consumed prefix.

**Scoring.** Full credit requires the formula, the operational justification, and the observation that `k` is exogenous to the model.

**Q2 (C).** A population has 12,000 eligible units and 60 positives. Capacity is 50. What does random selection yield, and what is one standard deviation of the resulting precision?

**Answer.** `E[TP] = 50 * 0.005 = 0.25`. Hypergeometric variance `= 50*0.005*0.995*(11,950/11,999) = 0.2477`, `sd = 0.4977`, and in precision units `0.4977/50 = 0.00995`, about one percentage point. So a single week's `P@50` carries roughly a one-point chance-noise floor before any model comparison begins.

**Reasoning.** Selection is without replacement, hence the finite-population correction. The precision standard deviation is the count standard deviation divided by `k`.

**Follow-up.** What if `k` were 500? (`E[TP] = 2.5`, variance `= 500*0.005*0.995*(11,500/11,999) = 2.384`, `sd = 1.544`, precision `sd = 0.00309`.)

**Red flag.** Using `k*pi*(1-pi)` without the finite-population factor and claiming precision noise of 1.1 points.

**Scoring.** The correction factor and the unit conversion are both required.

**Q3 (C).** Given the ranking `y = [1,0,0,1,1,0,0,0,1,0,0,0]`, compute AP, `P@3`, NDCG@3 and AUC.

**Answer.** Positives at ranks 1, 4, 5, 9. AP terms `1/1, 2/4, 3/5, 4/9` = `1.0, 0.5, 0.6, 0.4444`; sum `2.5444`; AP `0.6361`. `P@3 = 1/3 = 0.3333`. `DCG@3 = 1.0`, `IDCG@3 = 1 + 0.6309 + 0.5 = 2.1309`, `NDCG@3 = 0.4693`. Concordant pairs `23` of `32`, `AUC = 0.7188`.

**Reasoning.** AP's numerator is the running count of positives, its denominator the rank; NDCG's ideal is the best achievable ordering of the same positives; AUC counts ordered pairs.

**Follow-up.** Recompute AP if the positive at rank 4 were at rank 2. (Terms `1, 1.0, 3/5, 4/9` → `(1 + 1 + 0.6 + 0.4444)/4 = 0.7611`; AP rises by 0.125 for a two-position move, which shows AP's sensitivity to the head of the list.)

**Red flag.** Computing AP with precision at every rank rather than at every positive rank.

**Scoring.** All four values with working; a wrong NDCG ideal is the most common failure.

**Q4 (C).** A model has `TPR = 0.20`, `FPR = 0.003182`. Compute PPV at prevalence 0.005 and 0.001, and explain the direction of the change.

**Answer.** At 0.005: `0.001/(0.001 + 0.003182*0.995) = 0.001/0.0041661 = 0.2400`. At 0.001: `0.0002/(0.0002 + 0.003182*0.999) = 0.0002/0.0033788 = 0.0592`. PPV falls by a factor of four because the false-positive term scales with `1-pi` while the true-positive term scales with `pi`; at low prevalence the denominator is dominated by `FPR`.

**Reasoning.** Bayes on the two-by-two table; the `FPR*(1-pi)` term is nearly constant while `TPR*pi` shrinks tenfold.

**Follow-up.** What `FPR` is needed to hold PPV at 0.24 when `pi = 0.001` and `TPR = 0.20`? (`FPR = TPR*pi*(1-PPV)/(PPV*(1-pi)) = 0.2*0.001*0.76/(0.24*0.999) = 0.000634`, i.e. five times fewer false positives.)

**Red flag.** Quoting PPV as a property of the model rather than of the model-and-population pair.

**Scoring.** Both values plus the algebraic direction; the required-FPR follow-up separates strong candidates.

**Q5 (D).** Backtest `P@50 = 0.38`, production `P@50 = 0.24`. List the three checks you run, and say what each result would tell you.

**Answer.** (1) Rebuild features filtered on `t_a <= t_d` and recompute; if precision falls to ~0.24, timing leakage explains the gap. (2) Compare candidate-set coverage and prevalence between backtest and production; if `pi` differs, part of the gap is population. (3) Compare the fraction of top-50 cases that were previously reviewed and confirmed; if production's top-50 is mostly novel, selection bias in labels explains a gap in the opposite direction. Only after all three are quantified is "the model decayed" a live hypothesis.

**Reasoning.** The three mechanisms have distinct signatures: leakage is removed by a timestamp filter, population drift is detected by prevalence and adversarial validation, and selection bias is detected by overlap with historical selections.

**Follow-up.** What if fixing the timestamp filter changes nothing and prevalence matches? (Then the remaining candidates are a feature-pipeline skew — training-time and serving-time implementations differing — and a genuine drift; diff ten individual feature values between offline and online paths.)

**Red flag.** Starting with retraining, which addresses none of the three mechanisms and destroys the evidence.

**Scoring.** All three checks with their discriminating outcomes, plus an explicit ordering rationale.

**Q6 (C).** A 10-point precision improvement (0.24 vs 0.14) at 50 reviews per arm per day. How long must the experiment run for 80 percent power at `alpha = 0.05`?

**Answer.** Pooled `p = 0.19`; `se = sqrt(2*0.19*0.81/50) = 0.0785`; with `z_alpha/2 = 1.96` and `z_beta = 0.8416`, `n` per arm `= 2*(1.96+0.8416)^2*0.19*0.81/0.01 = 241.6`. At 50 per arm per day that is `4.83` weeks, so roughly five weeks of full-capacity parallel running. If either arm cannot run daily, the calendar time scales inversely with its cadence.

**Reasoning.** Standard two-proportion sample-size formula; the difference is 0.10 and the variance is maximised near 0.5 but evaluated at the pooled estimate.

**Follow-up.** The team can only afford two weeks. What are the options? (Reduce the required effect size by targeting a subgroup with higher prevalence, use a paired design on the same cases, or accept a weaker claim with an interval and pre-register it as exploratory.)

**Red flag.** "Run it for a week and see" with no power statement.

**Scoring.** Correct `n` within a few percent, plus the calendar conversion and an acknowledgement of the power trade-off.

**Q7 (C).** A five-bin calibration table has bins `(0.10, 100 units, 6 positives)`, `(0.30, 80, 30)`, `(0.50, 40, 18)`, `(0.70, 25, 21)`, `(0.90, 15, 13)`. Compute ECE and name the bin that contributes most.

**Answer.** For each bin compute `|acc - conf|`: `0.04, 0.075, 0.05, 0.14, 0.0333`. Weights: `100/260 = 0.3846`, `80/260 = 0.3077`, `40/260 = 0.1538`, `25/260 = 0.0962`, `15/260 = 0.0577`. Contributions: `0.01538, 0.02308, 0.00769, 0.01346, 0.00192`. ECE `= 0.06154`. The 0.70 bin contributes `0.01346` — second largest in absolute contribution but the largest per-unit miscalibration at 14 points.

**Reasoning.** ECE is a size-weighted average of absolute bin gaps, so a small bin with a big gap and a large bin with a small gap can contribute similarly.

**Follow-up.** The 0.70 bin is over-confident in the wrong direction for a fraud queue (it predicts 0.70, observes 0.84): what action does that imply? (The score is a valid ranking position but an invalid probability; if thresholds are set on expected loss, recalibrate with isotonic regression on a held-out window before use.)

**Red flag.** Averaging the five gaps unweighted: `0.0677`, close by coincidence here but wrong in general.

**Scoring.** Weighted computation, correct ECE, and the interpretation that the score is not a probability.

**Q8 (C).** Candidate generation admits 52 of 60 positives. What is the recall ceiling, and what is the conditional recall if the model reaches 12?

**Answer.** Ceiling `52/60 = 0.8667`. Unconditional recall `12/60 = 0.20`; conditional on admission `12/52 = 0.2308`. The 0.6667 gap between 0.20 and 0.2308 is the generator's missed positives, not the ranker's fault, and it is invisible in any metric computed on the candidate set.

**Reasoning.** Recall is a ratio over all positives; restricting the denominator to admitted positives measures a different quantity.

**Follow-up.** The generator misses 8 positives. How would you find out whether they were missable? (Score them with an oracle model that uses all features: if the oracle still cannot place them in the candidate set, the generator's blocking keys are the constraint, and the fix is a new key, not a better ranker.)

**Red flag.** Reporting conditional recall without labelling it.

**Scoring.** Both numbers and the explicit statement of which is reportable.

**Q9 (D).** `P@50` is 0.24 on Monday and 0.31 on Tuesday. Nothing in the model changed. Give three explanations and the check for each.

**Answer.** (1) Tie block at the boundary: Monday's tie-break put positives at 51-60, Tuesday's at 40-49. Check: count distinct scores at ranks 45-55 and recompute under three tie rules; a range spanning 0.24-0.31 confirms it. (2) Population shift: Tuesday's eligible set lost 2,000 micro-accounts, raising prevalence. Check: print `N`, `P`, `pi` and lift; stable lift with rising `pi` means the population changed. (3) Label timing: Tuesday's confirmations included a batch closure of older cases that had been pending. Check: count terminal labels per decision date; a spike at the boundary indicates batch processing, not performance.

**Reasoning.** Each explanation leaves a different fingerprint: ties show as score multiplicity, population drift shows as denominator change, label batches show as maturity jumps.

**Follow-up.** All three are absent and the difference persists for a week. What then? (Check for case-deduplication changes — a merged-case rule alteration changes the unit and therefore the denominator — and for a graph snapshot job that ran late, which changes the neighbourhoods themselves.)

**Red flag.** Attributing it to "variance" without computing the interval; a 7-point swing on 50 cases is inside the noise band from 4.6, so the first task is to check whether there is anything to explain.

**Scoring.** Three mechanisms with three distinct checks, plus the recognition that the swing may be noise.

**Q10 (J).** AUC improves by 0.12 and `P@50` is unchanged. Ship or not?

**Answer.** Do not ship on that evidence. AUC's added area is spread across thresholds the queue never uses; a 0.12 AUC gain can come entirely from better ordering at rank 2,000, where nothing is consumed. The decision needs one of three additional facts: a measured `P@k` improvement at the production `k`, an improvement at a capacity the operation intends to adopt, or a demonstrable benefit in another consumed quantity such as queue-age stability or case-size reduction.

**Reasoning.** The metrics integrate different regions; only the prefix is consumed (`0.6.3` gives the geometric argument that a model perfect in the consumed region gains at most 0.0032 AUC).

**Follow-up.** The team proposes doubling capacity to 100 to capture the AUC gain. What must be shown? (`P@100` beating the incumbent's `P@100`, and the marginal precision on slots 51-100 exceeding prevalence; otherwise the added slots are worse than random sampling.)

**Red flag.** "AUC is the industry standard for ranking."

**Scoring.** Recognition that the decision needs a consumed-quantity improvement, plus one concrete way to test it.

**Q11 (D).** Your `P@50` is 0.30 but reviewers say most cases are worthless. Reconcile.

**Answer.** Three reconciliations. (1) The confirmed labels include cases closed for reasons unrelated to fraud — a policy closure counted as a positive inflates precision without producing useful reviews. Check: sample 20 confirmations and read the closure reasons; measure the share that are fraud-attributable. (2) The unit is a case, and one case can hold 40 accounts, so 50 cases is 100+ reviews of near-duplicate material; check mean case size. (3) The positives are concentrated in a few large structures already known to the team, so precision is high and novelty is zero; check the overlap between the top 50 and the last 30 days' selections.

**Reasoning.** Reviewer dissatisfaction is a signal about *marginal value*, which `P@k` does not measure; it measures hit rate against a label definition that may be broader than usefulness.

**Follow-up.** Define a metric that would capture the complaint. (Precision restricted to fraud-attributable outcomes, times novelty, at fixed `k` — or simply "distinct previously unreviewed confirmed rings per 50 reviews".)

**Red flag.** Assuming the reviewers are wrong, or that the model needs retraining.

**Scoring.** At least two mechanisms with checks, and a proposal for a metric aligned with the complaint.

**Q12 (C).** A component partition places a 406-node component in the test fold. It holds 9 of the 12 top-50 hits. What is the effective sample size and what does that do to your interval?

**Answer.** The effective number of independent units is one, not 406, for that structure: 9 of 12 hits come from a single connected object, so the interval computed as if the 50 cases were independent overstates the evidence. The design effect is large: with 9 of 12 positives in one cluster, `n_eff ≈ 50 / (1 + (9/12)*(406-1))`, which is far below 1 — practically speaking, the estimate rests on one observation. The honest report states precision with and without that component and declines to compute a confidence interval that pretends independence.

**Reasoning.** Clustered sampling inflates variance by a design effect that grows with intra-cluster correlation and cluster size; with one cluster holding most positives, the correlation is near 1.

**Follow-up.** What would a defensible protocol do? (Component-level holdout with the largest component excluded, plus a separate statement about how the giant component would be handled in production, since it will also dominate the live queue.)

**Red flag.** Treating 406 nodes as 406 independent observations.

**Scoring.** Explicit recognition of clustering, an attempt at a design-effect argument, and a protocol fix.

**Q13 (C).** Compute the precision needed at 250 capacity for the extra 200 slots to be better than random, given prevalence 0.005.

**Answer.** Marginal precision on slots 51-250 is `(TP_250 - TP_50)/200`. Random would give `0.005`, so the condition is `(TP_250 - TP_50)/200 > 0.005`, i.e. `TP_250 - TP_50 > 1`. With `TP_50 = 12` and `TP_250 = 22`, marginal precision is `10/200 = 0.05`, ten times prevalence — so the expansion is justified on this ranking. The condition is weak, which is why the interesting question is not "better than random" but "better than the next best use of 200 reviews".

**Reasoning.** Marginal precision is a difference of counts over a difference of capacities.

**Follow-up.** Where does marginal precision cross prevalence on this ranking? (Between 250 and 400: `(26-22)/150 = 0.0267`, still above 0.005, so the crossover is beyond 400; the queue is worth expanding further than intuition suggests, which is itself a finding.)

**Red flag.** Comparing average precision at 250 to prevalence rather than the marginal precision.

**Scoring.** The marginal formulation and the numeric comparison; the follow-up distinguishes careful from casual.

**Q14 (J).** A vendor reports `P@50 = 0.61` on "graph-enhanced fraud detection". Which four facts do you demand before believing any of it?

**Answer.** (1) The population and eligibility predicate, with counts — 61 percent precision at what prevalence? At prevalence 0.3, 61 percent is unremarkable; at 0.005 it is extraordinary. (2) The candidate-set construction and its coverage, since a hand-curated candidate set can manufacture any precision. (3) The label definition and maturity — who confirmed, over what window, and what share is terminal. (4) The comparator protocol, measured on the identical population, window and capacity, ideally with the same-features-minus-graph ablation. Anything short of these four makes the number unauditable.

**Reasoning.** Each fact removes one of the four ways a precision figure can be manufactured without dishonesty: favourable prevalence, favourable candidate set, favourable labels, absent comparator.

**Follow-up.** The vendor offers an on-site pilot. What do you measure? (Precision at your capacity on your population with your label process, plus the audit sample that bounds selection bias; and you insist on the ablation, because a graph vendor's model may be a tabular model with three extra columns.)

**Red flag.** Accepting the number because it is high, or rejecting it because it is high.

**Scoring.** All four facts, phrased so that each maps to a distinct failure mode.

**Q15 (D).** Your evaluation pipeline and your serving pipeline disagree by 6 precision points. Name the check that separates implementation skew from data skew.

**Answer.** Score a frozen, identical input batch through both paths and diff feature values per unit before diffing scores. If features agree and scores differ, the skew is in the model artefact or its preprocessing (implementation skew). If features differ, the skew is in the data path: a join that resolves differently, a null-handling default, a timezone conversion, or a stale cache — and the per-feature diff names which. This ordering matters because a score diff alone cannot distinguish a wrong feature from a wrong model, and teams routinely retrain to fix a join.

**Reasoning.** Skew is compositional: `score = f(features)`. Holding `features` fixed isolates `f`; holding `f` fixed isolates `features`.

**Follow-up.** Features differ on exactly one column, a 30-day count that is one higher online. What is the likely cause? (An off-by-one boundary — `>= t_d - 30d` versus `> t_d - 30d` — which moves every count by roughly one day's volume; check the boundary convention in both code paths.)

**Red flag.** Retraining or re-tuning a threshold to close a pipeline gap.

**Scoring.** The frozen-batch diff and the feature-before-score ordering, plus a concrete skew example.
