
## 10. Key Theorems / Results Reference

### 10.1 Additive-model representation

**Result.** Any function representable by a forest can be represented by a boosted ensemble of the same tree size, and vice versa; both are finite sums of axis-aligned indicator functions. The families differ in *how many* terms each needs and in which terms the selection procedure finds, not in the class of functions reachable at a fixed term count.

**Consequence for practice.** A claim that "trees cannot express X but boosting can" is usually a statement about the selection procedure's efficiency, not about representational capacity. The honest form is: at 300 trees of depth 6, boosting finds a good approximation to X on this data, while an averaging forest of the same size does not.

### 10.2 Why averaging reduces variance

**Result.** For `B` predictors each with variance `sigma^2` and average pairwise correlation `rho`, the variance of their mean is `rho sigma^2 + (1-rho) sigma^2/B`. Derivation is a direct expansion of `Var((1/B) sum Z_b)` and appears as LTB-15.

**Consequence.** Bagging reduces only the component of variance that is independent across members. It cannot reduce the shared component, which is why the limit is `rho sigma^2` and why decorrelation (row sampling, column sampling) is the mechanism that matters once `B` is large. The frequently repeated advice "more trees never hurts for a forest" is true for variance but false for cost, and it is false for bias: averaging biased trees gives a biased ensemble.

### 10.3 Boosting reduces bias, and can increase variance

**Result.** Under the gradient-descent view, each round moves the function along the negative functional gradient, so the training loss is non-increasing while the steps are descent steps and the shrinkage is positive. The population loss is not monotone: it can rise after some round even while the training loss keeps falling.

**Consequence.** "Boosting reduces bias and variance" is a common misstatement. Boosting primarily reduces bias with respect to the target function; its variance behaviour depends on the leaf configuration. The correct operational statement is that adding rounds reduces training loss monotonically and reduces validation loss until the point where the added capacity starts fitting noise, and that this turning point is what early stopping finds.

### 10.4 The equivalence of the exponential-loss boost and reweighting

**Result.** AdaBoost's sample reweighting is the gradient-descent view of boosting under the exponential loss `L(y, F) = exp(-y F)`, with `y` in `{-1, +1}`. The reweighting factor is precisely the negative gradient step size for that loss.

**Consequence.** An AdaBoost implementation is a special case, and the diagnostic follows: if a dataset has label noise, the exponential loss punishes misclassified points exponentially, so AdaBoost's effective sample weight concentrates on the noisy rows. Switching to logistic loss changes that behaviour. This is why "AdaBoost is sensitive to outliers" and "logistic boosting is less so" are statements about the loss, not about the algorithm family name.

### 10.5 The CART greedy optimality gap

**Result.** CART's recursive partitioning finds the optimal split *at each node* for the impurity criterion, but it does not find the optimal tree of a given size: the problem is NP-hard in general, and greedy search is a heuristic. The gap is unbounded in adversarial cases and small in most tabular data.

**Consequence.** Two configurations that differ only in random seed can produce materially different trees with nearly identical training loss, and the one with better validation loss is not reliably the better structure — it is the luckier greedy path. This is the argument for averaging over seeds when comparing families, and for not over-interpreting a single tree's structure.

### 10.6 The bias-variance decomposition of squared error

**Result.** For squared loss at a point `x`, expected error decomposes into irreducible noise, squared bias, and variance. The decomposition is exact for squared loss and does not exist in the same additive form for log-loss.

**Consequence.** Statements copied from squared-error intuition into classification contexts (for example "the ensemble's variance is the average of the trees' variance") are approximations at best. For log-loss the correct statement requires the second-order expansion around the current score, which is exactly what `H` captures in LTB-11.

### 10.7 The no-free-lunch statement, correctly scoped

**Result.** Averaged over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm has the same expected off-training-set error. The statement is about a uniform prior over targets and says nothing about any specific problem.

**Consequence.** The theorem is not an argument that family choice does not matter. It is an argument that family choice must be justified by properties of *this* problem — smoothness, extrapolation need, interaction order, explanation contract — rather than by a universal ranking. Sections 2 and 7 are the operational form of that requirement.

### 10.8 Shrinkage as an implicit reduction of step size

**Result.** For a fixed direction, applying a fraction `nu` of a Newton step reduces the objective by `nu(2 - nu)` times the full-step decrease, to second order. At `nu = 0.3` that factor is `0.51`; at `nu = 1` it is `1.0`; at `nu = 0.1` it is `0.19`.

**Consequence.** Shrinkage is not a mysterious regulariser; it is a step-size choice with a computable effect on the objective. The reason small `nu` often generalises better is that it takes many small, well-conditioned steps rather than a few large ones, and the reason it costs compute is exactly the `nu(2-nu)` factor: halving `nu` roughly halves the per-round progress while keeping per-round cost constant.

```text
shrinkage factor   nu(2-nu)                          LTB-50
   nu = 0.05 -> 0.05(1.95) = 0.0975
   nu = 0.10 -> 0.10(1.90) = 0.1900
   nu = 0.30 -> 0.30(1.70) = 0.5100
   nu = 0.60 -> 0.60(1.40) = 0.8400
   nu = 1.00 -> 1.00(1.00) = 1.0000
```

### 10.9 Degrees of freedom of a penalised fit

**Result.** For a linear smoother with hat matrix `S`, the effective degrees of freedom is `trace(S)`. For ridge, `S = X(X'X + lambda I)^{-1}X'`, so `df = sum_j l_j/(l_j + lambda)` over the eigenvalues of `X'X`.

**Consequence.** Model complexity in the linear family is a continuous quantity, and it can be reported honestly. A ridge fit with `df = 0.75` on 40 candidate features is a one-parameter model for practical purposes; a fit with `df = 38` is not. Comparing two linear models without reporting `df` is comparing two numbers without their denominators.
