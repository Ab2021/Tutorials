
## 11. Interview / Exam Questions

Questions 1–9 require computation or diagnosis; 10–15 test judgement and mechanism recall. Each answer is self-contained.

**Q1. Two standardised features have correlation 0.5. With `lambda = 1`, what are their penalised eigenvalues and the effective degrees of freedom?**

Eigenvalues of a 2×2 correlation matrix are `1 + rho` and `1 - rho`, so `1.5` and `0.5`. Penalised: `1.5/2.5 = 0.6000` and `0.5/1.5 = 0.3333`. `df = 0.6000 + 0.3333 = 0.9333`. Interpretation: the fit spends just under one unit of freedom on two features. If you answered `df = 2` you computed the dimension of the coefficient vector rather than the trace of the hat matrix, which is the quantity that matters.

**Q2. A boosted classifier's leaf holds four rows, all negative, at a node where `G_L = +2.0` and `H_L = 1.0`. What is the leaf weight at `lambda = 0`, `1`, `4`, `20`, and what does each do to a base score of 0?**

`w = -G/(H+lambda)`: at `lambda = 0`, `-2.0/1.0 = -2.0`; at 1, `-1.0`; at 4, `-0.4`; at 20, `-0.0952`. Applied to a base score of 0 at full step, the leaf scores are `-2.0`, `-1.0`, `-0.4`, `-0.0952`, i.e. probabilities `0.1192`, `0.2689`, `0.4013`, `0.4762`. Four rows is a weak estimate, so the correct answer is that `lambda = 0` produces a loud claim from four rows and `lambda = 20` produces almost no claim at all; the defensible middle is around `lambda = 4` combined with a `min_child_weight` that would have prevented this leaf from existing.

**Q3. What is the total hessian `H` of a 20-row leaf at base rate 0.02, and what does a `min_child_weight` of 5 mean there?**

`h_i = p(1-p) = 0.02(0.98) = 0.0196`. Twenty rows: `H = 0.392`. So a threshold of `min_child_weight = 5` interpreted as a sum of `H` requires `5/0.0196 = 255` rows in each leaf. Interpreted as a row count, it requires 20 rows. This factor-of-thirteen difference on an imbalanced problem is the reason a configuration ported from a balanced dataset silently overfits: nothing errors, the leaves are just a thirteenth of the size the author intended.

**Q4. A split has raw gain `2.657143`. With `gamma = 1.0`, is it admitted, and what is the difference between the two numbers?**

The raw gain is `2.657143`; the admission test compares `2.657143 - 1.0 = 1.657143` against zero, so the split is admitted with 1.657143 of surplus. The trap is reporting `2.657143` as "the gain" in a log alongside `gamma = 1.0` and then wondering why a split with a smaller reported gain was refused. The reported quantity must be the post-gamma surplus, or the gamma value must be printed next to it.

**Q5. Compute the Brier score of a model whose four negative rows score 0.208505 and six positive rows score 0.824271.**

`Brier = (1/n) sum (p - y)^2`. Negatives: `0.208505^2 = 0.043474` each, times four = `0.173897`. Positives: `(1 - 0.824271)^2 = 0.175729^2 = 0.030881` each, times six = `0.185285`. Sum `0.359182`, divided by ten = `0.035918`. Note the contrast with log-loss `2.094862`: Brier is bounded and dominated by gross errors, log-loss punishes confidently wrong rows. A model that is systematically under-confident looks fine on Brier and mediocre on log-loss.

**Q6. With shrinkage 0.05, how many rounds are needed to reach 95% of the asymptotic movement, and how does that compare with shrinkage 0.3?**

`R(M) = 1 - (1-nu)^M`. Solve `0.95 = 1 - 0.95^M`, so `M = ln(0.05)/ln(0.95) = 58.4`, rounded up to 59 rounds. At `nu = 0.3`, `M = ln(0.05)/ln(0.7) = 8.4`, so 9 rounds. Six and a half times more rounds for the same total movement, and the fine-resolution benefit is the reason to pay it: small steps spend more iterations near the optimum, where the residual shrinks fastest.

**Q7. Under an orthonormal design with `b_ols = 0.8`, what penalty removes that coefficient under lasso, and what does ridge do at the same penalty?**

Lasso zeroes the coefficient when `lambda >= |b_ols| = 0.8`, by the soft-threshold identity. At `lambda = 0.8` ridge gives `0.8/(1+0.8) = 0.4444`, which is not zero and never will be at any finite penalty. The consequence: if you need a sparse coefficient set for a stated reason (a rule sheet with a fixed set of fields), ridge cannot deliver it no matter how the penalty is tuned.

**Q8. A depth-4 ensemble of 100 trees serves at 5 ns per node visit. What is the per-row latency and single-threaded throughput?**

Visits per row `= 100 × 4 = 400`. Latency `= 400 × 5 ns = 2.0 microseconds`. Throughput `= 1e6/2.0 = 500,000` rows per second per core. If the requirement is 5,000 rows per second, the ensemble uses 1% of a core and latency is irrelevant; if the requirement is 2 million rows per second, you need four cores and the model is now a capacity line item.

**Q9. A split on 100 rows produces children of 40 and 60 rows with Gini 0.375 and 0.4444 from a parent Gini of 0.5. Give the Gini gain, the entropy gain, and explain why they differ.**

Gini gain `= 0.5 - 0.4(0.375) - 0.6(0.4444) = 0.0833`. Entropy: parent 1.0 bit, children `0.4(0.8113) + 0.6(0.9183) = 0.8755`, gain `0.1245` bits. They differ in magnitude because they are different functions of the class proportions, so their numeric gains are not comparable across criteria — only their *rankings* of candidate splits are, and even that only approximately. Never compare a Gini gain to a threshold tuned for entropy.

**Q10. You have 300 boosted trees of depth 6 at 1,800 node visits per row. A new latency budget of 200 microseconds is imposed. What are your options?**

`200 µs / 5 ns = 40,000` visits, so `40,000/6 = 6,666` trees are affordable — the budget is not binding, which is the first thing to check before redesigning anything. If the budget were 200 visits, you would have `200/6 = 33` trees. The options in order of preference: truncate the ensemble at 33 trees and measure the loss change (usually small, because early trees carry most movement); reduce depth to 4 and keep more trees (visits scale with depth, so 50 trees at depth 4 is also 200 visits); or move the scoring to a compiled runtime with better cache behaviour. Rebuilding the model family is the last option, not the first.

**Q11. A random forest has 20 trees with per-tree variance 1.0 and pairwise correlation 0.5. What is the ensemble variance, and what does adding 80 more trees achieve?**

`Var = 0.5(1.0) + 0.5(1.0)/20 = 0.5 + 0.025 = 0.525`. At `B = 100`: `0.5 + 0.005 = 0.505`. The extra 80 trees reduce variance by `(0.525-0.505)/0.525 = 3.8%`. The floor is `0.5`, so the remaining headroom is only `0.025`. Lowering `rho` by increasing column randomisation is the only lever with real room left.

**Q12. A depth-5 tree approximates a diagonal boundary. What is the order of the error, and what depth achieves 1% error?**

With `K = 2^5 = 32` steps the error is about `1/(2K) = 1/64 = 0.015625`, i.e. 1.56% of the unit area. For 1%: `1/(2K) = 0.01` gives `K = 50`, so depth `ceil(log2 50) = 6`. Reaching 0.1% needs `K = 500`, depth 9. The growth is exponential in depth but only linear in the number of cuts, which is why diagonal structure is cheap for linear models and expensive for trees.

**Q13. Your model's validation loss is 0.31 and training loss is 0.09, and the gap does not shrink when you double the training set. What is the diagnosis?**

A gap that persists as `n` grows is not variance. Three competing causes: the validation population differs from training (a period, a segment, a pipeline difference); the validation set is being used for early stopping and therefore the model has been selected on it; or a feature's distribution shifted between the two windows. The discriminating check is to refit using only data from the validation period and compare. If the gap collapses, it is population shift; if it survives with the stopping set kept separate, it is a genuine capacity problem.

**Q14. Why can a monotone constraint make a model worse while its aggregate metrics barely move?**

The constraint restricts the admissible leaf values, so it can only increase training loss. The reason aggregate metrics barely move is that the constraint binds on a subset of rows — typically the tail where the feature is extreme — while the aggregate is dominated by the bulk. The consequence is that a constrained model can be materially wrong at the edges, exactly where business rules concentrate, with no visible signal in AUC or log-loss. The check is a slice-level comparison at the extremes of the constrained feature, not a global one.

**Q15. State, in mechanism terms, why boosting can overfit while its training loss is still falling.**

Training loss falls monotonically because each round is a descent step on the training objective. Population loss depends on the function's complexity, which grows with every accepted split. Once the accepted splits describe individual rows rather than structure, further descent reduces training loss by memorising and increases population loss. Nothing in the training loss curve signals the crossing, which is why a disjoint stopping set is mandatory rather than convenient.

**Q16. An identifier-like column with 3,000 distinct values tops gain importance. Give the competing explanations and the check that separates them.**

Three explanations: the column has genuine predictive content (it encodes a product or a region with real signal); it is a leakage path carrying information from after the decision point; or it is pure cardinality bias, because a column with 3,000 cut points offers many thresholds and each looks mildly profitable on training rows. The check is permutation importance on a disjoint holdout with repeated shuffles: genuine signal produces a large, stable loss increase; cardinality bias produces a small or unstable one; leakage produces a large increase that disappears when the column is rebuilt with point-in-time correctness. Only the leakage case requires re-deriving the feature.
