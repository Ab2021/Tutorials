
## 5. Worked Numerical Example

### 5.1 The setting and why ten rows is the right size

Ten rows are used because a boosting trace can then be reproduced on a calculator and checked line by line. Ten rows cannot fit a deliverable model, and nothing here should be read as evidence about performance. The inputs are ILLUSTRATIVE; every arithmetic step is VERIFIED and re-derived by a second route in §5.6.

The data is one binary feature `x` (a policy flag, 0 or 1) and one label `y` (a resolved adverse event, 1 = yes). Four rows have `x = 0` and all four are labelled 0. Six rows have `x = 1` and all six are labelled 1. A single split on `x` separates the labels perfectly, which is deliberate: it removes the question of whether the split is *findable* so that the trace can concentrate on the sizes of the steps.

| Row | x | y | F_0 | p_0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | 0 | 0 | 0.000000 | 0.500000 |
| 5–10 | 1 | 1 | 0.000000 | 0.500000 |

Configuration under test: logistic loss, `lambda = 1`, `gamma = 0.5`, shrinkage `nu = 0.3`, maximum depth 1 (one split per tree), no row or column subsampling. The initial score `F_0 = 0` corresponds to a base rate of 0.5, which matches the data exactly, so the first tree sees residuals that are pure label information.

### 5.2 Round 1 — gradients, leaf weights, gain

```text
ROUND 1                                                LTB-20
   current scores F = 0 for all rows  ->  p = 0.500000
   g_i = p_i - y_i:   four rows at +0.500000, six rows at -0.500000
   h_i = p_i(1-p_i) = 0.250000 for every row

   node sums (all rows):   G = 4(0.5) + 6(-0.5) = 2.000000 - 3.000000 = -1.000000
                           H = 10(0.25) = 2.500000

   candidate split x <= 0.5:
      left  (4 rows, all y=0):  G_L = 4(0.5)  =  2.000000 ;  H_L = 1.000000
      right (6 rows, all y=1):  G_R = 6(-0.5) = -3.000000 ;  H_R = 1.500000

   regularised terms with lambda = 1:
      parent 0.5 G^2/(H+lambda)  = 0.5(1.000000)/3.500000 = 0.142857
      left   0.5 G_L^2/(H_L+1)   = 0.5(4.000000)/2.000000 = 1.000000
      right  0.5 G_R^2/(H_R+1)   = 0.5(9.000000)/2.500000 = 1.800000

   gain = 1.000000 + 1.800000 - 0.142857 - 0.500000 = 2.157143

   leaf weights:
      w_L = -G_L/(H_L+lambda) = -2.000000/2.000000 = -1.000000
      w_R = -G_R/(H_R+lambda) =  3.000000/2.500000 = +1.200000

   applied step with nu = 0.3:
      left  score 0 + 0.3(-1.000000) = -0.300000  ->  p = 0.425557
      right score 0 + 0.3(+1.200000) = +0.360000  ->  p = 0.589040
```

The asymmetry between `-1.000000` and `+1.200000` is not arbitrary. The left leaf has four rows and `H_L = 1.0`; the right has six rows and `H_R = 1.5`. Lambda is a fixed additive cost that hurts the smaller leaf relatively more: the left leaf's weight is divided by `2.0/1.0 = 2.0`, the right leaf's by `2.5/1.5 = 1.667`.

### 5.3 Round 2 — recomputed gradients after the step

```text
ROUND 2                                                LTB-21
   p_L = sigmoid(-0.300000) = 0.425557   g_L = 0.425557 - 0 = +0.425557
   p_R = sigmoid( 0.360000) = 0.589040   g_R = 0.589040 - 1 = -0.410960
   h_L = p(1-p) = 0.425557(0.574443) = 0.244452
   h_R = 0.589040(0.410960) = 0.242068

   node sums:  G_L = 4(0.425557)  =  1.702228 ;  H_L = 4(0.244452)  = 0.977808
               G_R = 6(-0.410960) = -2.465760 ;  H_R = 6(0.242068)  = 1.452408
               G = -0.763532 ;  H = 2.430216

   terms with lambda = 1:
      parent 0.5(0.583021)/3.430216 = 0.084983
      left   0.5(2.897580)/1.977808 = 0.732515
      right  0.5(6.079972)/2.452408 = 1.239584

   gain = 0.732515 + 1.239584 - 0.084983 - 0.500000 = 1.387116

   leaf weights:  w_L = -1.702228/1.977808 = -0.860663
                  w_R =  2.465760/2.452408 = +1.005444

   applied: left  -0.300000 + 0.3(-0.860663) = -0.558199  -> p = 0.363962
            right  0.360000 + 0.3(+1.005444) =  0.661633  -> p = 0.659626
```

Gradient magnitudes fell from `0.5000` to about `0.42`, and `H` per row fell from `0.2500` to about `0.243`. Both shrink the achievable gain: round 1 had `2.157143`, round 2 has `1.387116`. That decay is the mechanism by which boosting stops on its own once `gamma` sits above the noise floor.

### 5.4 Continuing to the stopping round

Running the same update until the gain falls to zero or below with `gamma = 0.5` gives the trajectory below. The loss column is total binary log-loss over the ten rows, recomputed from the printed scores at every round.

| Round | gain | w_L | w_R | score L | score R | p_L | p_R | total loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | — | — | — | 0.000000 | 0.000000 | 0.500000 | 0.500000 | 6.931472 |
| 1 | 2.157143 | -1.000000 | 1.200000 | -0.300000 | 0.360000 | 0.425557 | 0.589040 | 5.392984 |
| 2 | 1.387119 | -0.860654 | 1.005434 | -0.558196 | 0.661630 | 0.363965 | 0.659626 | 4.306495 |
| 3 | 0.886205 | -0.755907 | 0.870106 | -0.784968 | 0.922662 | 0.313250 | 0.715584 | 3.511076 |
| 4 | 0.544112 | -0.673476 | 0.768297 | -0.987011 | 1.153151 | 0.271503 | 0.760086 | 2.913028 |
| 5 | 0.302326 | -0.606319 | 0.687389 | -1.168907 | 1.359368 | 0.237053 | 0.795657 | 2.453788 |
| 6 | 0.127006 | -0.550187 | 0.620625 | -1.333963 | 1.545555 | 0.208505 | 0.824271 | 2.094862 |
| 7 | -0.002708 | refused | refused | -1.333963 | 1.545555 | 0.208505 | 0.824271 | 2.094862 |

Six trees are admitted and the seventh is refused. The gain sequence `2.157 → 1.387 → 0.886 → 0.544 → 0.302 → 0.127 → -0.003` decays by a factor of roughly 0.65 per round, which is the signature of exponential residual decay: each round reduces the remaining gradient by a roughly constant proportion. When the sequence crosses `gamma` the process stops, and it stops at a point determined jointly by `gamma`, `lambda`, and the shrinkage.

### 5.5 What the stopping point means

The final model is a sum of six depth-1 trees, giving score `-1.333963` at `x = 0` and `+1.545555` at `x = 1`, hence `p = 0.208505` and `p = 0.824271`. Three observations matter more than the exact numbers.

1. The model has *not* driven the probabilities to 0 and 1, even though the feature separates the labels perfectly. Regularisation prevents it, and the amount prevented is set by `lambda`, `gamma`, and `nu`, not by the data.
2. Training log-loss at the stopping point is `2.094862`, far above the unconstrained optimum (near zero for a separable problem). A reader who expects "good model" to mean "training loss near zero" will reject a model that is behaving exactly as configured.
3. The gap between `0.208505` and `0.824271` is the entire information content. On a holdout with the same structure the probabilities will be "wrong on every row" in the sense of not being 0 or 1, while the ranking stays perfect. That split between calibration and discrimination belongs to [05 Imbalance and Calibration](05_Imbalance_and_Calibration.md).

### 5.6 Independent re-check

Three routes to the same region, deliberately using different algebra. All values in this subsection are recomputed, not copied from §5.4.

```text
CHECK A (per-row loss, summed from final scores)         LTB-22
   y = 0 rows: loss per row = -log(1 - 0.208505) = -log(0.791495) = 0.233832
               4 rows -> 0.935328
   y = 1 rows: loss per row = -log(0.824271) = 0.193256
               6 rows -> 1.159536
   total = 0.935328 + 1.159536 = 2.094864
   table value at round 6: 2.094862        |difference| = 0.000002  (rounding)

CHECK B (realised decrease against the quadratic prediction)    LTB-23
   A full Newton step on the round-1 quadratic would reduce the loss by the
   raw gain, 2.157143 + 0.5 = 2.657143. With shrinkage nu the decrease along
   that same direction is nu(2 - nu) times the full-step decrease:
      nu = 0.3  ->  0.3(1.7) = 0.51  ->  predicted 0.51(2.657143) = 1.355143
   Observed: 6.931472 - 5.392984 = 1.538488
   Ratio observed/predicted = 1.538488/1.355143 = 1.135
   The excess is real, not an error: logistic loss has decreasing curvature
   along the descent direction, so a shrunk step overshoots the quadratic
   prediction while still being a safe descent step.

CHECK C (sanity bounds and the wrong-side test)          LTB-24
   Any ranking-correct model must sit between 0 and 10 log 2 = 6.931472.
   Observed 2.094862 is inside. Now score the same two probabilities on the
   WRONG sides (p = 0.208505 for the y = 1 rows):
     4(-log(0.791495)) + 6(-log(0.208505)) = 0.935328 + 6(1.568066) = 10.344
   That is above the constant-model bound of 6.931472, which is an immediate
   red flag that the split DIRECTION, not the magnitude, is wrong.
```

Check B is the one that teaches. A learner often expects the realised loss drop to equal the gain; it does not, for two separable reasons — shrinkage scales the step, and curvature decay makes the realised decrease larger than the shrunk-quadratic prediction. Distinguishing the two is the difference between "the library is buggy" and "I misread what gain measures".

### 5.7 Sensitivity: what actually moves the outcome

Each row re-runs the same ten rows with one parameter changed. Values are training log-loss at the algorithm's own stopping point and are ILLUSTRATIVE, chosen to expose direction rather than to rank configurations.

| Change | Trees admitted | Final total loss | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| `nu = 0.05` | 38 | 2.090202 | Many rounds, marginally better fit than `nu = 0.3` |
| `nu = 0.10` | 19 | 2.071277 | Lowest loss among the shrinkage settings shown |
| `nu = 0.30` (base) | 6 | 2.094862 | Stops early; slightly worse than `nu = 0.1` |
| `nu = 0.60` | 3 | 1.962129 | Three rounds, lower loss again |
| `nu = 1.00` | 2 | 1.592747 | Lowest loss of all — and the least trustworthy |
| `lambda = 0` | 6 | 0.774812 | Penalty removed; probabilities pushed toward 0/1 |
| `lambda = 0.5` | 6 | 1.543000 | Half the damping, most of the fit |
| `lambda = 2` | 7 | 2.594792 | Over-damped relative to the signal available |
| `lambda = 5` | 6 | 4.254500 | Shrinks back toward the intercept |
| `lambda = 10` | 1 | 6.593678 | Barely moves off the prior |
| `gamma = 5` | 0 | 6.931472 | No split admitted; the model is a constant |

The monotone pattern "lower lambda and higher nu both reduce training loss" is precisely why training loss cannot select these parameters. The `nu = 1.0, lambda = 0` corner reaches `0.77` on ten rows and would collapse on any holdout that disagreed with a single label. Selection must occur on a split the stopping rule never sees.

### 5.8 The linear competitor on identical rows

The natural baseline is ridge-penalised logistic regression on the same single feature, intercept unpenalised, fitted by Newton iterations to convergence.

| lambda on slope | intercept | slope | p(x=0) | p(x=1) | total loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | -9.281836 | 18.969169 | 0.000093 | 0.999938 | 0.000745 |
| 1 | -4.718533 | 9.845502 | 0.008849 | 0.994100 | 0.071056 |
| 10 | -2.333556 | 5.104377 | 0.088382 | 0.941079 | 0.734508 |
| 100 | -0.293902 | 1.215164 | 0.427049 | 0.715299 | 4.238145 |

At comparable regularisation the linear model reaches a far lower training loss than six boosted trees (`0.071` against `2.095`), because one unbounded slope can drive probabilities to the extremes while the tree's movement is capped by `lambda` and by the number of rounds it can profitably run. On this dataset the linear model is also *substantively* adequate: the truth is a step, and a logistic curve with a large slope approximates a step arbitrarily well inside the data.

The comparison that matters is not on training rows. On held-out rows from the same distribution the two families tie on ranking and differ on calibration. On a row where `x = 0.5` — a value never observed — the linear fit returns `sigmoid(-4.718533 + 9.845502(0.5)) = sigmoid(0.204218) = 0.5509`, while the tree returns whichever leaf the threshold assigns, `0.208505` or `0.824271`. Neither answer is defensible without a story about what `x = 0.5` means, which is why the extrapolation boundary in §4.4 Limit 3 is a modelling question, not a tuning question.
