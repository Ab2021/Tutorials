# BOPIS Promise Accuracy

Every quantity in this case is **ILLUSTRATIVE**: the cohort of 520 order lines, the 447 kept
promises, the pick-time distribution, the slot capacities and the dollar figures are constructed
teaching inputs, computed here so that each step can be re-derived by a reader. Nothing in this
document is a measurement of any retailer, store, workforce, vendor system or customer population,
and no deployment, pilot or vendor selection is asserted. The arithmetic is verified and shown; the
inputs are invented and labelled as such wherever they drive a conclusion.

## 1. The One-Line Framing

At the moment an online basket is converted into a store pickup order, somebody must choose a
pickup-ready time — call it the promise — for each line, and that choice is a commitment the store
will be judged on for the next two hours to two days.

The decision this case studies is therefore narrow and operational: given a point-in-time snapshot
of one store's on-hand inventory, its open pick queue and its staffed pick capacity, which promise
tier should be attached to this order line right now, with the objective of maximising promises the
store can actually keep, subject to a pick-capacity ceiling and a service-level floor on the two-hour
tier.

The trap the case exists to teach is that the obvious machine-learning formulation — predict whether
this order will be ready in two hours, then quote two hours when the probability is high — optimises
something that is *not* the objective, and in the peak slot it makes the objective strictly worse.
The label "was it ready on time" is defined by the promise that was quoted. A policy that quotes two
hours less often generates fewer late two-hour orders by definition, so a model trained on that
label can improve its own score while the store's promises become less reliable at the tier customers
actually care about. That is the evaluation-mismatch pattern: the convenient target is a function of
the treatment.

The reusable discipline is three-part. First, keep the untransformed event — the timestamp at which
the order became physically collectable — as the evaluation substrate, so any candidate promise can
be re-scored against the same realised ready times. Second, treat capacity as a hard pre-gate applied
before scoring, not as a post-hoc filter or a business-rule footnote. Third, report the promise-kept
rate *per tier* plus the volume moved between tiers, because the pooled rate over all promises is a
mix statistic: it can rise while every tier gets worse, simply by shifting volume to the generous
tier.

Takeaway: when the label is generated by the policy, the policy's evaluation must be reconstructed
from raw timestamps and a capacity model, not read off the production label.

## 2. Context & Background

Buy-online-pickup-in-store turns a retail store into two businesses at once. The front of the store
serves walk-in demand with staff who can be interrupted; the back of the store runs a small
fulfilment cell — a picker walks the aisles with a handheld, collects an order's lines into a
tote, validates the tote against the order, and stages it at a counter or locker bank. The customer
is notified, drives over, presents a code, and receives the tote.

The promise is the customer-facing contract on the front end of that sequence. In the constructed
setting of this case the store offers four tiers:

| Tier | Quoted wording | Nominal deadline | Typical basket | Why the tier exists |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P2 | ready in 2 hours | quote time + 120 min | 1–8 lines, shelf-stable | captures urgent and impulse demand |
| P4 | ready in 4 hours | quote time + 240 min | 3–14 lines | absorbs the mid-day queue |
| PD | ready by 20:00 today | store close − 60 min | any size | protects same-day intent |
| PN | ready tomorrow from 10:00 | next-day 10:00 | bulk, bulky, case packs | offloads peak, allows replenishment |

Three structural facts make the tier choice hard, and none of them is visible to a model that only
sees order attributes.

The first fact is that pick capacity is shared and lumpy. A picker is a person with a shift, a break,
a handheld, and a physical walking route. Two pickers assigned to the ten-to-twelve window supply
240 picker-minutes in total; they do not supply 120 minutes of parallel work each on the same aisle,
because two pickers in the same narrow aisle collide and because the tote-staging counter has one
printer. Capacity is therefore a *slot* quantity, not a daily total, and the constraint binds exactly
when demand concentrates — which is also when a promise is most valuable to quote.

The second fact is that the inventory record is a belief, not a fact. On-hand quantity is updated by
receipts, sales and cycle counts, and between counts it drifts through mis-scans, unrecorded
damage, theft, and items left in a customer's hands at the till. A promise quoted on a belief of
"three on hand" is a promise that fails when the third unit is not on the shelf, and the failure
is discovered by the picker, not by the system.

The third fact is that the promise is quoted *before* the pick is assigned, so the queue that the
order joins is not observed at quote time. A model that predicts pick duration from basket size and
item weight can be perfectly accurate about the *service time* of an order and still be useless for
promising, because the realised ready time is service time plus queueing delay, and queueing delay
is a property of the slot's aggregate load rather than of this order.

The baseline in this case is the incumbent deterministic rule, and it is deliberately not a straw
man. The baseline quotes P2 when the store's remaining 120-minute capacity ledger shows a free slot
and the requested SKU's on-hand minus committed is at least the line quantity; otherwise it quotes
P4, and it quotes PN for case-pack lines and for SKUs flagged as bulky. The ledger is a spreadsheet
maintained by the fulfilment lead and is updated twice a day. The baseline's weakness is stale
capacity and coarse inventory, but its virtue is that it is auditable in one page, which is exactly
the property an operations manager needs when a customer complains.

The proposed method in this case is a gradient-boosted classifier trained on eighteen months of
history to predict, per order line, whether the line will reach the staging counter within 120
minutes of the quote. The model is legitimate: its features are basket attributes, SKU velocity,
day-of-week, hour-of-day, store, and a lagged on-hand snapshot. It has, in this constructed
setting, a held-out area under the ROC curve of 0.81 and a Brier score of 0.147. Both numbers are
plausible and neither is the point.

The point is what happens when you deploy the model behind the obvious decision rule — quote P2
whenever the predicted probability of a two-hour ready time exceeds 0.90. In the constructed cohort
the baseline promises P2 to 520 lines and keeps 447, a rate of 0.860. The model-gated rule promises
P2 to 468 lines — fewer, and by construction the ones the model likes — and keeps 389, a rate of
0.831. It promised fewer customers a two-hour pickup, failed a larger share of the promises it did
make, and finished 28 kept lines behind a rule it beats on every offline discrimination measure.

Two mechanisms produce that inversion, and both are the substance of the case. The first is the
promise-dependent label: the training cohort contains only lines that were quoted P2, so the model
estimates the probability of an on-time pickup *given that the store had already accepted a
two-hour obligation and given that such orders were then treated with priority*. Applied to a line
the incumbent rule would have deferred, that score answers a counterfactual question nobody asked
and it is biased upward for exactly the hard lines where the answer matters. The second is the
capacity ceiling: the model ranks lines one at a time and has no representation of a slot, so it
pours promises into the peak window until the pickers are the bottleneck, and every promise beyond
the feasible pick count is mechanically late no matter how good the ranking was. The rest of the
case diagnoses both mechanisms and designs the selection rule that survives them.

Two further context items matter for reading the rest of the document.

The organisational clock is not the data clock. The pick handheld timestamps a line when the picker
confirms the quantity; a separate integration timestamps the customer notification; a third
timestamp records the counter handoff scan. "Ready" in this case always means the *handoff-eligible*
timestamp, defined as the moment the notification was sent, because that is the earliest moment the
customer could have collected. A store that stages an order at 11:40 and notifies at 12:15 has a
ready time of 12:15 for promise purposes, and a metric built on the staging timestamp will
systematically flatter the store.

The incentive context matters too. Store managers are measured on sales per labour hour and on
customer satisfaction, and pickup complaints land in the same satisfaction stream as shelf
availability complaints. A promise policy that trades a small conversion loss on the generous tier
for a large reduction in late handoffs is therefore attractive to the store even when it is neutral
to the e-commerce channel — which is precisely why the decision needs a shared metric rather than
two departments arguing from two dashboards.

## 3. The Business Question

**Question:** for each BOPIS order line presented at quote time, which promise tier — P2, P4, PD or
PN — should be attached so that the realised promise-kept rate at the two-hour tier is maximised
subject to pick capacity in the assigned slot, a service-level floor on P2, and no worsening of the
pooled rate on the other tiers?

**Owner:** the fulfilment manager for the region, who holds the pick roster and is accountable for
late-handoff complaints. The e-commerce product owner holds the quote-time user interface and must
co-sign any change to the tier menu because it changes the customer's stated expectation.

**Trigger:** the decision is made per order line at quote time, which happens continuously. The
*review* of the policy is triggered by any of four events: a change to the pick roster template, a
change to the promise tier menu, a store on-hand accuracy audit that moves the estimate by more than
three percentage points, or a two-week stretch in which the pooled promise-kept rate moves by more
than two percentage points without a roster or catalogue change.

**Available actions:** quote P2; quote P4; quote PD; quote PN; or abstain from the automated choice
and route the line to the fulfilment lead for a manual tier decision. Abstention is a real action
with a real cost — manual decisions take about ninety seconds each and the lead has roughly forty
such minutes per day — so the abstention budget is a constraint, not an escape hatch.

**Objective:** maximise the number of order lines whose realised ready time is at or before the
promised time, with the two-hour tier weighted as the tier where the promise carries the most
customer value. The case uses kept-lines count and the per-tier rate as the primary quantities and
treats conversion loss from tier deferral as a cost to be netted, not as an objective to be ignored.

**Hard constraints:**
- no feature, join or lookup may use data timestamped after the quote-time snapshot, including
  outcome-derived fields such as "was the order eventually fulfilled";
- the number of lines promised into any 120-minute slot must not exceed that slot's feasible pick
  count under the staffed roster, with a documented exception path for roster changes;
- the P2 tier must retain a service-level floor of 0.90 kept in every store-week, evaluated on the
  corrected ready timestamp;
- every automated tier decision must be explainable in one sentence from logged inputs, and must be
  reversible within the same slot by an authorised operator;
- the manual abstention budget must not be exceeded in any store-day.

**Scope:** one region of six stores, fourteen consecutive days, 520 order lines that the incumbent
rule promised at the P2 tier, plus the full 1,180-line cohort those 520 belong to. The wider cohort
exists so that tier deferral can be costed; the 520 are the population where the baseline and the
candidate can be compared on identical lines.

**Out of scope:** claims about any real retailer's performance; the design of the customer-facing
countdown widget; the labour-relations question of how pickers are scheduled; the legal question of
whether a missed pickup promise creates a consumer-credit obligation in any jurisdiction; and the
choice of fulfilment technology vendor.

**Definition of done for the exercise:** a tier-selection rule that can be computed from the
point-in-time snapshot, a capacity model that is checked against realised slot load, an evaluation
that re-scores candidate policies on uncensored ready timestamps, a per-tier and pooled reporting
set with uncertainty, and an explicit list of the conditions under which the rule must fall back to
the incumbent baseline.

Takeaway: the question is a constrained selection problem over four discrete actions with a
capacity ceiling, not a probability-estimation problem with a threshold appended.

## 4. Assumptions & Constraints

The assumptions below are the load-bearing ones. Each has an owner-facing verification and a
fallback that keeps the store operational when the assumption fails, because a promise system that
halts on a failed assumption converts a modelling problem into a customer-service incident.

| ID | Assumption | Verification | Fallback if it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| A-01 | The quote-time snapshot is point-in-time correct for on-hand and queue depth | Replay ten sampled decisions against the raw event log; assert no field has a validity start after the quote | Serve the incumbent ledger value for that store-hour |
| A-02 | The handoff-eligible timestamp is the notification send time, not the staging scan | Join the two timestamp streams and measure the gap distribution; the gap is the correction you must apply if they diverge | Use the later of the two timestamps, which cannot overstate the rate |
| A-03 | On-hand accuracy is stable across the fourteen-day window | Cycle-count a random sample of 120 SKUs per store-week and compare to the record | Widen the P2 eligibility rule by a shrink margin until the audit closes |
| A-04 | Pick service time is a function of basket attributes and is stationary within a store-week | Recompute the service-time distribution per week; flag any week whose median shifts by more than 0.6 min | Refit the capacity model on the trailing four weeks only |
| A-05 | The roster published at 06:00 holds for the day | Compare the roster table to the clock-in log; measure deviations per store-day | Use the clock-in count for any slot with a deviation above one picker |
| A-06 | Tier deferral has a measurable conversion cost that is small relative to the late-handoff cost | Estimate the abandonment rate on deferred lines from the order funnel, then bound the cost with the highest plausible margin | Assume the worst plausible abandonment and test whether the decision still holds |
| A-07 | Manual abstention capacity is forty decisions per store-day | Count actual manual tier decisions per day for a week before the change | Cap automated deferral at the observed manual capacity, not the assumed one |
| A-08 | A line promised at P2 is comparable to a line promised at P4 in every respect except the promise | Compare basket composition, SKU bulk class, and hour-of-day across tiers; report standardised differences | Restrict the comparison to matched strata or drop the comparison |
| A-09 | Substitution is resolved at pick time by a documented rule and the substitute has similar pick difficulty | Sample 40 substituted lines and time both the original and substitute pick | Treat substituted lines as unknown duration and exclude them from capacity arithmetic |
| A-10 | The two-hour tier is the tier customers value most, so weighting it is defensible | Check the funnel: conversion rate and repeat rate by tier, not by opinion | If the funnel does not support the weighting, report both tier-weighted and volume-weighted results |

The constraints are harder-edged and each one has an owner who can refuse.

| ID | Constraint | Owner who can refuse | Test that it is respected |
|---|---|---|---|
| C-01 | No post-quote fields in features, including fulfilment outcome and cancellation reason | Data steward | Automated lineage assertion in the feature build; failure blocks publication |
| C-02 | Slot line count must not exceed the feasible pick count under the staffed roster | Fulfilment manager | Recompute slot load from the assignment log for every slot, every day |
| C-03 | P2 service-level floor of 0.90 kept per store-week | Store manager | Weekly per-tier report; a breach freezes automated tiering for that store |
| C-04 | One-sentence explanation available for every automated tier decision | E-commerce product owner | Sample fifty decisions and have an operator reproduce the sentence from the log |
| C-05 | Reversal within the slot by an authorised operator, with the reversal logged | Store manager | Exercise one reversal per store during the shadow period |
| C-06 | Manual abstention within observed capacity | Fulfilment manager | Daily count versus the A-07 observed capacity |
| C-07 | No tier menu change without co-signature of e-commerce and fulfilment | E-commerce product owner | Change log requires two named approvers |

Two of these deserve a paragraph each because they are the ones teams quietly relax.

C-01 is relaxed the moment somebody adds "historical cancellation rate for this SKU" as a feature.
That field is computed over a trailing window that includes the current order's own future: if the
order is later cancelled for stock, that cancellation enters the trailing window of the next order,
and the model learns a signal that is partly a function of the outcomes it is meant to predict. The
leak is small per order and large in aggregate, and it is invisible in a feature-importance chart
because the field looks like a legitimate prior. The only reliable defence is a lineage assertion
that refuses to publish a feature set containing any field whose validity start exceeds the snapshot
timestamp — a build-time failure, not a review checklist item.

C-02 is relaxed the moment the capacity ledger is treated as reporting rather than as a gate. The
ledger is updated twice a day in the incumbent process; the roster changes at 06:00; the promotion
calendar changes on Thursdays. A gate that reads a twice-daily ledger is a gate that is wrong during
exactly the four hours it matters. The verification in the table is therefore not "does the ledger
exist" but "does recomputing slot load from the assignment log agree with the gate's own count",
which is a different and much harder test.

The binding risk in this case is neither of those, however. It is the evaluation risk: the population
of lines that were promised P2 is a *selected* population, and selecting a different population
changes the denominator. Every number in sections 6, 9 and 11 has to be read with that in mind, and
section 9 states the population explicitly for each reported figure so the reader can check whether
two rates being compared actually share a denominator.

A note on what is deliberately not assumed: the case does not assume the model is bad. Its ranking
quality is real and it is used in the final design. The case also does not assume the baseline is
good; the baseline's stale ledger is the reason the candidate has room to improve. What the case
refuses is the inference from "better AUC" to "better promises", and the arithmetic in section 6
shows that inference failing by a measurable amount on the same 520 lines.

Takeaway: assumptions about timestamps and capacity silently determine the metric, so each one gets
a test whose failure has a defined operational consequence rather than a note in a risk register.
## 5. Data, Tools & Method

### 5a Data Specification

The analysis grain is **one order line at its quote instant**. Everything downstream — capacity,
lateness, cost — is aggregated from that grain and never from an order grain, because a four-line
order consumes four picks and one notification, and conflating the two double-counts either labour
or service.

| Element | Definition used here |
|---|---|
| Unit of analysis | one `line_id`, being one SKU in one quantity on one order |
| Primary key | `line_id` (stable, opaque) |
| Foreign keys | `order_id`, `store_id`, `sku_id`, `quote_id` |
| Partition key | `store_id` and `quote_date` |
| Decision timestamp | `t_quote`, the instant the tier is written into the customer's confirmation |
| Event timestamps | `t_assign` (pick task created), `t_pick_confirm` (handheld quantity confirm), `t_stage` (tote at counter), `t_notify` (customer notification sent), `t_handoff` (counter scan) |
| Outcome timestamp | `t_ready = t_notify` under the A-02 convention |
| Population | 520 lines the incumbent rule promised at P2 on one promotion Saturday in one store |
| Wider cohort | 1,180 lines in total that day, used only to cost tier deferral |
| Currency | USD, stated at the order's own price book version |
| Horizon | quote instant to `t_notify`, truncated at 240 minutes |

Field inventory, with the availability discipline stated per field. The right-hand column is the one
that matters for leakage, and it is the column reviewers skip.

| Field | Type | Units | Available at `t_quote`? |
|---|---|---|---|
| `line_qty` | integer | eaches | yes |
| `sku_velocity_28d` | float | eaches/day | yes, lagged one day |
| `on_hand_recorded` | integer | eaches | yes, as of the last cycle count or receipt |
| `on_hand_committed` | integer | eaches | yes, sum of open picks and holds |
| `store_zone` | category | none | yes |
| `slot_picker_count` | integer | pickers | yes, from the published roster |
| `open_pick_minutes` | float | picker-minutes | yes, from the assignment log as of `t_quote` |
| `basket_line_count` | integer | lines | yes |
| `hour_of_day`, `day_of_week` | integer | clock | yes |
| `promo_flag` | boolean | none | yes |
| `substitution_flag` | boolean | none | **no** — known only at pick time |
| `final_fulfilment_status` | category | none | **no** — outcome |
| `cancel_reason` | category | none | **no** — outcome |
| `t_notify` | timestamp | UTC | **no** — outcome |

The three "no" rows are the fields that make this problem easy to get wrong and impossible to detect
after the fact. Each of them is plausibly available in a warehouse table and plausibly useful, which
is why the lineage assertion in C-01 has to be mechanical rather than discretionary.

Three further specification points deserve statement because they are where two analysts would
silently diverge.

*Truncation.* A line whose `t_notify` never arrives — the order was cancelled, or the customer never
collected and the tote was returned to stock — has no ready time. The case treats such lines as
late for promise purposes rather than dropping them. Dropping them is the single most common way to
inflate a promise-kept rate, because non-collection correlates with the slowest picks.

*Clock alignment.* `t_notify` is generated by an integration service whose clock is synchronised
hourly, while `t_pick_confirm` comes from handhelds whose clocks drift. The case therefore uses only
`t_notify` and `t_quote` for the primary metric and uses handheld timestamps only for service-time
estimation, where a drift of seconds is immaterial. Mixing the two streams in one subtraction is a
real defect observed in fulfilment dashboards and it produces negative durations.

*Quantity.* A line of eight eaches is not eight picks in the sense of eight walks — the picker walks
once and counts eight. Service time therefore scales with the *cube travel* plus a per-each count
term, not with the line count alone. The case approximates this with a per-order fixed term plus a
per-line term and absorbs the per-each counting into the fixed term, then checks the approximation
against the observed service-time distribution in 5d.

**What is not available at decision time** is the crucial part of the specification and it is worth
a compact statement: the realised ready time, the substitution decision, the picker who will be
assigned, whether the pick will fail on stock, whether the customer will collect, whether a second
order will arrive in the same slot, and whether the roster will hold. Any model whose inputs include
a proxy for those quantities is answering a different question than the one the store asks.

### 5b Tools & Stack

The stack proposed here is deliberately boring. The case is about evaluation and decision structure,
and a sophisticated stack would obscure whether the failure came from the method or the machinery.

| Layer | Constructed choice | Why this and not something else |
|---|---|---|
| Source capture | append-only raw landing of the seven event types, with ingestion timestamp preserved separately from event timestamp | two timestamps per event is the minimum needed to diagnose late arrival without guessing |
| Snapshot builder | as-of join keyed on `t_quote`, publishing an immutable snapshot id per decision batch | makes replay exact; a snapshot that can be recomputed is not a snapshot |
| Feature build | deterministic transforms from the snapshot only, with a lineage manifest listing every source column and its validity start | allows a build-time refusal rather than a review discussion |
| Model | gradient-boosted trees, 400 rounds, depth 4, learning rate 0.05, monotone constraint on basket line count | depth 4 limits interaction mining on a cohort of this size; the monotone constraint encodes a known direction |
| Decision service | rule engine that applies capacity gate, then ranking, then abstention budget | keeps the gate inspectable and testable without retraining anything |
| Decision log | append-only record of snapshot id, gate inputs, rank, chosen tier, and operator override | the only artifact that lets a later audit reconstruct why a customer saw a tier |
| Review queue | manual tier decisions for lines the engine abstains on, with a hard daily cap | converts abstention from an escape hatch into a budgeted resource |
| Metric job | nightly per-tier rates, pooled rate, fixed-yardstick rate, and the mix decomposition | reports four numbers because one number is what caused the problem |

Two stack choices are load-bearing enough to justify explicitly.

The **immutable snapshot** is the difference between an audit and an anecdote. If the feature build
reads live tables, then the cohort you evaluate next month is not the cohort the model saw, and every
discrepancy becomes a debate about which run was right. Publishing a snapshot id per decision batch,
and refusing to score a batch without one, costs a little storage and removes an entire class of
arguing.

The **separate decision service** exists so the capacity gate can be changed without touching the
model. In the failure narrative of this case the model was fine and the gate was wrong; an
architecture that fuses them would have required a retrain to fix a scheduling bug, which is how
teams end up shipping a second model to correct the first.

Specific product names, versions, cloud services and licences are intentionally not asserted. The
relevant properties are: append-only capture, as-of joins, deterministic transforms, an inspectable
rule layer, and a log that survives schema change. Any stack with those properties supports the case.

### 5c Method Summary

The method has four separable stages, and separating them is itself part of the lesson, because the
failure in this case is a stage-boundary failure rather than an algorithmic one.

**Stage 1 — point-in-time assembly.** Build the quote-time snapshot, attach the realised `t_ready`,
and mark every line as kept or late against the tier that was actually quoted. Output: a table with
one row per line and an explicit quoted tier.

**Stage 2 — offline discrimination.** Fit the classifier on the trailing eighteen months of
P2-quoted lines and report AUC and Brier on a held-out final eight weeks. This stage answers "can the
model rank lines by their probability of an on-time two-hour ready time, among lines that were
promised two hours". It is a legitimate question and the answer here is yes.

**Stage 3 — counterfactual re-scoring.** This is the stage teams skip. For every candidate policy,
recompute kept/late from the raw `t_ready` values against the promise the candidate policy *would*
have quoted, and additionally from a *fixed* 120-minute yardstick that no policy can move. Two
yardsticks, because they answer different questions: the policy-relative one asks "did we keep the
promise we made", the fixed one asks "did the store get faster".

**Stage 4 — capacity-constrained selection.** Replace the per-line threshold with a per-slot
selection: compute the feasible line count for each quote slot from the roster and the service-time
model, rank the candidates in that slot by expected value of admission, admit the top `K`, and route
the remainder to the next tier, whose capacity is checked in the same pass. Selection is therefore
an assignment problem over slots, solved greedily within each slot and sequentially across tiers.

The baseline against which all four stages are compared is the incumbent deterministic rule
described in section 2: ledger-checked P2 when a nominal slot is free, P4 otherwise, PN for
case-pack and bulky lines. It is reproduced exactly, including its twice-daily ledger staleness,
because a baseline reproduced with better data is not the baseline and the comparison would be
flattering rather than informative.

Alternatives considered and not carried forward, with reasons:

| Alternative | Why not used here |
|---|---|
| Regression on `t_ready` minutes, then promise if predicted p95 below 120 | attractive and worth a follow-up; needs a quantile model with enough peak-slot mass, which this cohort lacks |
| Survival model with censoring for non-collection | correct in principle; the case has only 41 non-collection lines, too few to fit and validate honestly |
| Constrained optimisation over slots as a single LP | over-specified for 4 tiers and 14 slot-instances; greedy within slot is within one line of optimal here |
| Queueing simulation (M/M/c style) | informative for capacity sizing, but the service-time distribution is not exponential and the simulation needs parameters the store cannot estimate |
| Human-only tier assignment | removed from consideration because the 90-second manual cost exceeds the abstention budget at this volume |

### 5d Provenance, Quality, Exclusions, Leakage

Provenance for the constructed cohort is stated as a chain rather than a sentence, so a reader can
see exactly which link is invented.

```text
raw event capture  ->  as-of snapshot (id per batch)  ->  feature manifest  ->  model artifact
        |                       |                              |                    |
   [constructed]          [constructed]                  [constructed]        [constructed]
        v                       v                              v                    v
  ingestion vs event      snapshot immutability       validity-start check    version pinned
  timestamp retained      replayed 10 decisions       build fails on late col  in decision log
```

The quality checks are the ones that determine whether the numbers in section 6 mean anything.

| Check | Procedure | Constructed result |
|---|---|---|
| Key uniqueness | count distinct `line_id` against row count per partition | 1,180 rows, 1,180 distinct, no duplicate |
| Temporal validity | for each feature, compare validity start to `t_quote` | 15 of 18 features pass; 3 are outcome fields and are excluded |
| Snapshot replay | rebuild the snapshot for 10 sampled decisions and diff | 10 of 10 identical |
| Ready-time presence | count lines with `t_notify` null | 41 lines null, retained and counted late |
| Negative durations | count `t_ready < t_quote` | 2 lines, both traced to a handheld clock drift, both quarantined |
| Service-time fit | compare modelled `S` to observed per-order durations | model median 6.6 min vs observed 6.9 min, model understates by 0.3 min |
| Completeness by store-hour | share of lines with all 15 features non-null | 0.984 overall; 0.951 in the 10:00–12:00 slot |
| Duplicate orders | same customer, same SKU, two orders within 10 minutes | 7 pairs, retained, flagged as possibly split baskets |

The service-time check deserves comment because it is the one that changed the conclusion. The
modelled mean of 6.6 minutes understates the observed 6.9 minutes by 4.3%, which means the
constructed capacity numbers in section 6 are optimistic by roughly that amount. Recomputing the
10:00–12:00 capacity at 6.9 minutes gives `⌊384/6.9⌋ = 55` orders rather than 58, and 55 orders at
1.8 lines is 99 lines rather than 104 — a five-line reduction in the slot's feasible promise count.
The case keeps 104 as its working figure and states the 99 explicitly, because a capacity model whose
error is known and unapplied is the exact mechanism by which slot gates silently oversubscribe.

Exclusions, each with a rate because an unquantified exclusion is indistinguishable from a silent
one:

| Exclusion | Count | Rate of cohort | Justification | Effect if reversed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lines with negative duration | 2 | 0.4% | clock defect | immaterial |
| Lines from a store closed mid-day for a facilities fault | 34 | 6.5% | no capacity data for the affected hours | would raise capacity, lowering mechanical lateness |
| Lines on a SKU with no 28-day velocity (new item) | 19 | 3.7% | velocity feature undefined | would add uncertainty to ranking, not to capacity |
| Lines quoted during a 40-minute outage of the quote service | 11 | 2.1% | `t_quote` unreliable | would distort the slot assignment, not the totals |
| Lines on orders later merged with another order | 6 | 1.2% | `line_id` survives but slot may change | small |

Note that the closed-store exclusion is the largest and it is not random: a store that closes for a
facilities fault loses pickers for part of a day, which is precisely a capacity event. Excluding it
removes the most informative capacity observations from the cohort. The case retains the exclusion
because the affected hours have no roster record, and flags that the resulting capacity estimate is
biased toward well-staffed hours. This is the kind of honest limitation that a case can state and a
dashboard usually cannot.

**Leakage.** Three leak classes appear in this problem and they need different defences.

*Target leakage.* Fields computed from the outcome — `final_fulfilment_status`, `cancel_reason`,
`substitution_flag` — are the obvious ones and the lineage assertion catches them. In the constructed
build, adding `substitution_flag` to the feature set raised held-out AUC from 0.81 to 0.87 and raised
the Brier score from 0.147 to 0.139, a change large enough to look like a modelling win and
sufficient to prove the field is a leak: substitution is decided by the picker after seeing the shelf,
and a substituted line is systematically later than a clean pick.

*Temporal leakage through aggregates.* A trailing cancellation rate per SKU is computed over a window
that includes the current order's future. The constructed build includes such a feature in a
competing variant; it raises AUC by 0.02 and its effect is concentrated in low-velocity SKUs where the
trailing window contains few observations and the current order dominates it. The defence is a
window-end assertion: an aggregate's window end must be at or before the snapshot timestamp, checked
as a scalar comparison rather than by reading the SQL.

*Policy leakage.* The subtlest class. The target "kept the two-hour promise" is defined by a policy
that, in training, gave P2 lines priority in the pick queue. The model therefore learns the
probability of on-time delivery *under priority treatment*, and applies that estimate to lines that,
under the new policy, will not receive priority. This is not a bug in the data; it is a mismatch
between the training regime and the deployment regime, and no lineage assertion can detect it. The
only defences are (i) to model ready time rather than the promise outcome, or (ii) to evaluate any
policy change on a fixed yardstick so that the changed treatment cannot move the target. Section 9
adopts (ii) and section 13 explains why (i) is the better long-run choice.

## 6. Analysis Walkthrough

This walkthrough builds six results. Each is presented in the same five-part anatomy so that the
constraint and the failure boundary are as visible as the point estimate, and each is followed by
the diagnostic that distinguishes it from its nearest competing explanation. All inputs are
ILLUSTRATIVE; the arithmetic is exact under the stated inputs.

### 6.1 Ready-time decomposition: what the promise is actually competing with

```text
FORMULA [D-01]: T_ready = t_quote + W_q + S_pick + H_handoff
WHERE:
  T_ready     [timestamp]  notification send time, the customer-visible ready moment
  t_quote     [timestamp]  instant the tier is written to the confirmation
  W_q         [minutes]    queueing delay: from quote to pick-task start, >= 0
  S_pick      [minutes]    pick service: task start to tote staged, > 0
  H_handoff   [minutes]    staging to notification, >= 0
CONSTRAINT:
  all four terms measured on the same clock domain; no term may be derived from T_ready itself.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (constructed, store S3, 10:00-12:00 slot, order of 2 lines):
  W_q = 34.0 min, S_pick = 3.0 + 2.0*2 = 7.0 min, H_handoff = 4.5 min
  T_ready - t_quote = 34.0 + 7.0 + 4.5 = 45.5 min
INTERPRETATION:
  Only S_pick is a property of this order. W_q is a property of the slot's aggregate load, and
  45.5 min of an admittedly easy order is already 38% of a 120-minute promise consumed by waiting.
```

The decomposition is the whole case in one line: a per-line model can be excellent at `S_pick` and
still be unable to promise, because `W_q` is not a line attribute. In the constructed cohort the
median `S_pick` is 6.6 minutes against a 120-minute budget, which is 5.5% of the promise, while the
median `W_q` in the 10:00–12:00 slot is 41 minutes, which is 34%. A model that explains 90% of the
variance in a quantity worth 5.5% of the decision is not a promising instrument, however good its
AUC looks on the label.

Boundary where the formula breaks: if the picker works the task immediately on assignment, `W_q` is
zero by construction and the decomposition collapses to service time plus handoff. That happens in
low-traffic slots and it is exactly where the per-line classifier performs well — the model is not
wrong, it is applied outside its regime. The discriminating check is the ratio of `W_q` to
`120 − S_pick − H_handoff` computed per slot; above roughly 0.3 the slot is queue-dominated and a
per-line gate cannot control the outcome.

### 6.2 Slot capacity: converting a roster into a feasible line count

```text
FORMULA [D-02]: K_slot = floor( (P * L_eff) / S_mean ),  S_mean = a + b * n_mean
WHERE:
  K_slot   [orders]        feasible pick orders in the slot
  P        [pickers]       pickers assigned for the whole slot
  L_eff    [minutes]       effective picking minutes per picker after breaks and staging trips
  S_mean   [minutes/order] mean service time
  a        [minutes]       fixed per-order term: tote, walk to zone, staging
  b        [minutes/line]  marginal per-line term
  n_mean   [lines/order]   mean basket size in the slot
CONSTRAINT:
  P and L_eff from the published roster, not from the ledger; n_mean from lines quoted into the
  slot, not from the store's daily average.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (constructed):
  10:00-12:00: P = 4, L_eff = 96 min -> 384 picker-min. a = 3.0, b = 2.0, n_mean = 1.8
    S_mean = 3.0 + 2.0*1.8 = 6.6 min
    K = floor(384 / 6.6) = floor(58.18) = 58 orders
    lines = floor(58 * 1.8) = floor(104.4) = 104 lines
  16:00-18:00: P = 3 -> 288 picker-min
    K = floor(288 / 6.6) = floor(43.64) = 43 orders
    lines = floor(43 * 1.8) = floor(77.4) = 77 lines
INTERPRETATION:
  The two peak slots can absorb 104 + 77 = 181 promised lines. Demand into those slots is
  128 + 86 = 214 lines. The gap is 33 lines, and no ranking of those 214 can remove it.
```

Two details in this formula are usually got wrong.

The first is `L_eff`. A two-hour slot with a picker assigned is not 120 minutes of picking. The
constructed store loses twelve minutes to a scheduled break, eight minutes to tote-staging trips
(the staging counter is at the back, and a picker carrying a full tote walks it there), and four
minutes to handoff checks and handheld re-login. That leaves 96 minutes, an 80% availability factor.
Using 120 minutes instead of 96 inflates capacity by 25%, which in the 10:00–12:00 slot means
promising 130 lines instead of 104. A 25% capacity inflation is the difference between a gate that
works and a gate that is decoration.

The second is `n_mean`. Capacity in *orders* and capacity in *lines* move in opposite directions as
baskets grow, which is a trap worth working through because it invalidates a natural shortcut.

Boundary where the formula breaks — basket composition. Recompute at `n_mean = 2.4`:

```text
S_mean = 3.0 + 2.0*2.4 = 7.8 min
K      = floor(384 / 7.8) = floor(49.23) = 49 orders      (down from 58)
lines  = floor(49 * 2.4) = floor(117.6) = 117 lines       (up from 104)
```

Larger baskets reduce the number of orders the slot can serve and *increase* the number of lines,
because the fixed three-minute term is amortised over more lines. A gate calibrated on lines per
slot and then applied to a day with bigger baskets will under-promise; a gate calibrated on orders
and applied to a day with small baskets will over-promise in minutes while looking safe in orders.
The correct gate is on *picker-minutes*, with lines as a reporting convenience. The discriminating
check is to compute capacity both ways for the day in question and compare; if the two disagree by
more than 5%, the slot gate must run on minutes.

### 6.3 Mechanical lateness: the lower bound no ranking can beat

```text
FORMULA [D-03]: Late_mech(slot) = max(0, D_slot - K_slot)
WHERE:
  Late_mech [lines]   lines that must miss the promise because the slot is oversubscribed
  D_slot    [lines]   lines promised into the slot
  K_slot    [lines]   feasible lines from D-02, converted at the slot's own n_mean
CONSTRAINT:
  D_slot counts every promise whose 120-minute deadline falls inside the slot, including promises
  quoted in the previous slot. Omitting carried-over promises understates D_slot.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (constructed, baseline policy):
  10:00-12:00: D = 128, K = 104 -> Late_mech = 24
  16:00-18:00: D =  86, K =  77 -> Late_mech =  9
  total mechanical = 33 lines
  observed late at baseline = 73 lines
  residual attributable to other causes = 73 - 33 = 40 lines
INTERPRETATION:
  33 of the 73 observed late lines are already determined by arithmetic before any model is fitted.
  The remaining 40 are the only part any ranking could influence, and they split across stockouts,
  picker absence, and notification delay.
```

This is the single most useful number in the case because it partitions the problem. Of the 73
broken promises, 45% are a capacity fact and 55% are operational noise. Any evaluation that credits
a model with improving the whole 73 is claiming credit for arithmetic.

Boundary where the formula breaks: the bound assumes lines are served in some order and that the
slot cannot borrow capacity from an adjacent slot. Both assumptions are false in practice. A picker
who starts early can pre-pick lines whose quote arrives at 11:55, and a quiet 12:00–14:00 slot can
absorb spill. In the constructed data, spill absorbed 6 of the 33 mechanical lines, so the true
mechanical count is 27 and the residual is 46. The direction of the correction matters: ignoring
spill *overstates* mechanical lateness and therefore *understates* what a model could achieve, which
is a conservative error and acceptable. Ignoring carry-in, in contrast, understates mechanical
lateness and flatters every candidate — that is the error to guard against, and the guard is to
count every promise whose deadline lands in the slot, not every promise quoted in it.

### 6.4 The pooled rate is a mix statistic, and the promise defines the numerator

```text
FORMULA [D-04]: r_pooled = sum_s (w_s * r_s),  w_s = D_s / sum_s D_s
WHERE:
  r_pooled [fraction]  kept lines / promised lines across all tiers and strata
  r_s      [fraction]  kept rate in stratum s (a slot, a tier, or a store)
  w_s      [fraction]  volume weight of stratum s, summing to 1
CONSTRAINT:
  "kept" must be evaluated against the tier actually quoted, and the weights must come from the
  same cohort as the rates; mixing a baseline denominator with a candidate numerator is invalid.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (constructed, baseline):
  slot A: r = 100/128 = 0.7813, w = 128/520 = 0.2462 -> contribution 0.1924
  slot B: r =  70/ 86 = 0.8140, w =  86/520 = 0.1654 -> contribution 0.1346
  offpeak: r = 277/306 = 0.9052, w = 306/520 = 0.5885 -> contribution 0.5327
  r_pooled = 0.1924 + 0.1346 + 0.5327 = 0.8597, check 447/520 = 0.8596 (rounding)
INTERPRETATION:
  The pooled figure is dominated by the off-peak stratum, which carries 59% of the volume and runs
  at 0.905. A policy that fixes the peak and ignores off-peak moves the pooled number by at most
  0.14 points per 10 points of peak improvement.
```

Now the counterexample that makes this formula dangerous, and the reason the case insists on a fixed
yardstick.

Suppose a policy moves every one of the 73 late lines from P2 to P4 and each of them is then ready
within four hours. Under a policy-relative metric, the pooled kept rate becomes `520/520 = 1.000`.
Nothing about the store changed: not one pick started earlier, not one tote reached the counter
sooner, not one customer received an item faster. The metric moved by 14 points because the
definition of success moved with the promise.

Recompute the same policy on the fixed yardstick "fraction of P2-eligible lines notified within 120
minutes of `t_quote`": `447/520 = 0.860`, identical to the baseline, because the yardstick is
independent of what was promised. The two metrics disagree by 0.140, and the disagreement is
entirely definitional. This is the evaluation-mismatch pattern in its purest form: the label was
generated by the policy, so a policy change that improves the label without improving the operation
is indistinguishable from a genuine improvement unless the evaluation refuses to use the label.

```text
FORMULA [D-05]: r_fixed = N_notified_within_120 / N_eligible
WHERE:
  r_fixed              [fraction]  policy-independent speed metric
  N_notified_within_120 [lines]    lines with T_ready - t_quote <= 120 min
  N_eligible           [lines]     lines quoted at any tier whose basket was P2-eligible
CONSTRAINT:
  eligibility fixed before the policy runs; the denominator must not shrink when the policy defers.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (constructed):
  baseline:  447 lines within 120 min of 520 eligible -> 0.8596
  gated:     389 lines within 120 min of 520 eligible -> 0.7481
  shifted:   447 lines within 120 min of 520 eligible -> 0.8596  (deferral cannot change it)
INTERPRETATION:
  The fixed yardstick separates speed from promise-keeping. Under it the 0.90-threshold policy is
  visibly worse than baseline, which the policy-relative metric concealed behind a smaller
  denominator.
```

The gated figure of 389 is the same count that gave 0.844 against an admitted population of 468. Read
against the fixed 520 it is 0.748, because 52 lines were deferred and therefore did not reach the
customer within 120 minutes as promised. Two numbers, one dataset, and the choice of denominator
decides whether the policy looks like a mild disappointment or a clear regression. Section 9 fixes
the reporting convention; the point here is that the convention has to be fixed before the result is
seen, not after.

Takeaway: compute the pooled rate only alongside per-stratum rates and the fixed yardstick, because
the pooled rate alone is a statistic about the mix rather than about the store.
### 6.5 Selection under capacity: why the ranking must be applied to admission, not to the promise

The capacity gate converts the problem from "promise to everyone the model likes" into "admit the
best `K_slot` candidates into the tier and route the rest". The quantity that ranking should order is
the expected value of admitting a line, not the probability that the line is fast.

```text
FORMULA [D-06]: EV_admit(i) = p_i * v_2h + (1 - p_i) * (v_late - c_comp)  -  mu_i * c_pick
WHERE:
  EV_admit(i) [USD]   expected net value of promising line i at P2
  p_i         [fraction] predicted probability the line is notified within 120 min if admitted
  v_2h        [USD]   margin attributable to a kept two-hour promise
  v_late      [USD]   margin retained on a late pick (customer usually still collects)
  c_comp      [USD]   expected service-recovery cost per broken promise
  c_pick      [USD]   labour cost per picker-minute
  mu_i        [min]   expected service time for line i
CONSTRAINT:
  p_i must be estimated on the deployment regime, not on the priority-treated training regime;
  the gate is applied per slot before ranking, and only lines passing the gate may be ranked.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (constructed, one line in slot A):
  p = 0.86, v_2h = 9.00, v_late = 7.20, c_comp = 4.50, mu = 6.6 min, c_pick = 0.22
  EV = 0.86*9.00 + 0.14*(7.20 - 4.50) - 6.6*0.22
     = 7.74 + 0.378 - 1.452 = 6.666 USD
  compare a low-p line: p = 0.55 -> 4.95 + 0.45*2.70 - 1.452 = 4.95 + 1.215 - 1.452 = 4.713 USD
INTERPRETATION:
  The spread between a strong and a weak candidate is 1.95 USD per line. The marginal pick costs
  1.45 USD. A line with p = 0.30 has EV = 2.70 + 1.89 - 1.45 = 3.14 USD and is still worth admitting
  when capacity is free, which is why the gate — not the ranking — decides who is excluded.
```

Note what this arithmetic does and does not say. It says that in the off-peak slots, where capacity
is not binding, a threshold on `p` throws away value: every candidate has positive expected value and
the correct policy is to promise P2 broadly. It says that in the peak slots, where the 105th
candidate cannot be picked, the marginal decision is a comparison among candidates rather than
against zero.

That distinction explains a pathology the case's authors observed in the constructed shadow log: a
global 0.90 threshold suppressed 52 off-peak promises whose expected value was strongly positive,
because the model's `p` in off-peak slots clustered at 0.83 due to a base-rate effect in training.
The threshold was not too high in an absolute sense; it was applied to a quantity whose scale shifts
by slot, and a single cut across heterogeneous slots is a specification error regardless of its value.

Boundary where D-06 breaks: the formula treats `p_i` as independent across lines in a slot, which is
false when the lines compete for the same picker. Admitting 104 lines with `p = 0.86` is not the
same as admitting 104 lines whose events are correlated through a shared queue. If the slot's
realised load exceeds `K`, every admitted line's `p` falls together, and the per-line products
overstate value by roughly the correlation term. The discriminating check is whether the slot's
realised pick-task start times cluster; if the standard deviation of `W_q` within a slot is below
8 minutes, the lines are effectively batched and the independence assumption is wrong.

### 6.6 Why the model's own score moves when the policy changes

This is the mechanism that produced the inversion in section 2, and it can be shown arithmetically
rather than argued.

```text
FORMULA [D-07]: p_hat(i) = P(ready <= 120 | x_i, quoted = P2)  !=  P(ready <= 120 | x_i, do(P2))
WHERE:
  p_hat(i)   [fraction]  model output, conditioning on the historical policy having quoted P2
  x_i        [vector]    permitted features at the snapshot
  do(P2)     [operator]  the intervention of promising P2 under the new decision rule
  quoted=P2  [event]     the selection event; the training cohort is conditioned on it
CONSTRAINT:
  equality holds only if quoting P2 does not change the pick treatment or the queue position.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (constructed):
  training cohort: only lines the ledger cleared were quoted P2. Under the ledger, a P2 line is
  flagged priority at task creation and is picked before PD and PN lines in the same slot.
  Suppose 62% of the cohort received priority treatment and their kept rate is 0.897, while the
  38% that were quoted P2 without priority have a kept rate of 0.801.
  blend check: 0.62*0.897 + 0.38*0.801 = 0.5561 + 0.3044 = 0.8605 ~ 0.860 (baseline rate)
  now apply the model to lines the ledger would NOT have admitted: those lines, under the new
  rule, enter the same slot without displacing priority lines, so they receive priority 0% of the
  time and their expected kept rate is closer to 0.801 than to 0.860.
INTERPRETATION:
  The model's calibration is a weighted average of two regimes. Applying it to a population whose
  regime weights are different biases every prediction by up to 6 points, in the optimistic
  direction, exactly where the decision is hardest.
```

The blend check is the important part: it reproduces the baseline's 0.860 from two sub-populations,
which demonstrates that the cohort is a mixture rather than a homogeneous set. Once you see the
mixture, the counterfactual bias is not mysterious. The model learned `0.62 * 0.897 + 0.38 * 0.801`
because that is what the data contained, and a new policy that changes the mixture weights inherits
an estimate for a mixture it no longer has.

The discriminating check for this failure is a stratification of the training cohort by whether the
line received priority treatment, and a comparison of the model's calibration curve in each stratum.
If the calibration curves differ — in the constructed build they differ by 4 to 7 points in the
0.75–0.90 band — then the score is regime-dependent and a threshold on it is a threshold on the
historical policy. If they coincide, the bias is absent and the model is safer to deploy.

Boundary where D-07's warning does not apply: a fully randomised promise experiment, in which tier is
assigned independently of the model, breaks the conditioning because `quoted` no longer predicts
treatment. That is the clean fix and it costs conversion on the randomised-off arm. The case does not
have such an experiment, which is why the adopted design uses a fixed yardstick rather than a
corrected probability estimate.

### 6.7 The residual after the gate: what remains for a model to earn

With mechanical lateness removed by the gate, the 46 residual late lines are the addressable set. The
constructed attribution splits them by first failing event, assigned by reading the event sequence of
each line rather than by a model.

| First failing event | Lines | Share of residual | Addressable by ranking? | Addressable by data accuracy? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stockout at pick (on-hand wrong) | 22 | 0.478 | yes, by avoiding the promise | yes, by cycle counts |
| Picker absence mid-slot | 9 | 0.196 | no | no, roster control |
| Notification integration delay > 12 min | 8 | 0.174 | no | partially, by expediting |
| Pick error requiring a re-pick | 5 | 0.109 | no | partially, by validation |
| Customer arrived before notification retry | 2 | 0.043 | no | no |

Check the shares sum to 1.000: `0.478 + 0.196 + 0.174 + 0.109 + 0.043 = 1.000`. And check the counts:
`22 + 9 + 8 + 5 + 2 = 46`, consistent with the residual from D-03 after the 6-line spill correction
(73 observed − 27 mechanical = 46).

Only the 22 stockout lines are both large and addressable. Two interventions reach them. The first is
accuracy: a cycle count on the 40 highest-velocity SKUs per store-week moves the on-hand belief closer
to truth, and if it recovers half of the 22, the fixed yardstick rises to `(447 + 11)/520 = 458/520 =
0.8808`. The second is avoidance: a model that ranks lines by stockout risk lets the gate defer the
risky ones to P4 rather than promise them at P2, which does not move the fixed yardstick at all but
does move the per-tier rate — because the deferred lines no longer count as broken two-hour promises.

That second effect is worth isolating because it is the honest version of what the original model was
trying to do. Under the gate plus risk ranking that defers those 22 lines, the constructed result is
465 P2 promises and 447 kept, or `447/465 = 0.9613`, against the baseline's `447/520 = 0.8596`. The
kept *count* does not rise at all: nothing became faster, 22 risky promises were simply not made. The
first lever that raises the count is the cycle-count programme, and if it recovers all 22 lines the
result is 487 P2 promises with 469 kept, `469/487 = 0.9630`, and a fixed yardstick of
`469/520 = 0.9019`. So the count moves by 22 only when the shelf is fixed, and the rate moves by 10
points when the promise is honest, and confusing those two contributions is how a fulfilment
programme reports a victory it did not have. Every other late line was late for a reason a tier
decision cannot reach: 9 picker absences, 8 notification delays, 5 re-picks, 2 early arrivals.

This decomposition is the finding, and it is stated next.

## 7. The Finding

The supported conclusion is a three-part, conditional statement, and each part was earned by a
separate piece of arithmetic.

**First: the machine-learning model cannot be deployed as a per-line threshold, and the reason is
structural, not a matter of tuning.** `W_q` is 34% of the promise in the peak slot and is not a line
attribute (D-01). The 0.90 threshold therefore admits lines in proportion to a quantity the store
cannot control, and the constructed result is 468 promises with 389 kept, `0.831`, against a baseline
of 520 promises with 447 kept, `0.860`. The model's discrimination is real — AUC 0.81, Brier 0.147 —
and it is applied to the wrong object.

**Second: the capacity gate is what makes the promise honest, and it buys no speed whatsoever.**
Applying `K_slot` from D-02 removes 33 promises that arithmetic had already doomed: 104 admitted of
128 in the 10:00–12:00 slot, 77 of 86 in the 16:00–18:00 slot, all 306 off-peak lines unchanged.
The P2 rate rises from `447/520 = 0.8596` to `447/487 = 0.9179`, a gain of 5.83 points, achieved by
promising fewer people rather than by picking faster. The fixed yardstick is unchanged at 0.8596,
which is the proof that the gain is definitional rather than operational. That is still worth having:
a promise kept 92% of the time is a better basis for a customer relationship than one kept 86% of the
time, and the 33 deferred customers are told the truth at quote time rather than disappointed later.

**Third: the model earns its place only when it is used to choose which lines not to promise, and the
size of that prize is 22 lines, not 73.** Of the 46 residual late lines, 22 fail on a stockout. Used
this way the ranker withholds those 22 promises, and the constructed result is 465 P2 promises with
447 kept, `447/465 = 0.9613` — a rate gain with no count gain, because withholding a bad promise does
not create a good one. The count moves only when the shelf is fixed: a cycle-count programme that
recovers all 22 gives 487 promises with 469 kept, `469/487 = 0.9630`, and lifts the fixed yardstick to
`469/520 = 0.9019`. So the honest attribution is 6.11 points of promise integrity from the gate and
the ranker together, and 4.23 points of speed from a person with a scanner.

The conclusion is conditional on four things, and it is withdrawn if any of them fails. Capacity must
be computable from the roster with the `L_eff` correction — if the store cannot state `L_eff`, the gate
is a guess and should not be deployed. The ready timestamp must be the notification time; if the store
can only observe staging scans, every rate in this case is overstated by the median staging-to-notify
gap. The tier mix must be reportable, because otherwise a policy that defers everything will look
excellent. And the abandonment cost of deferral must remain below the recovery cost of a broken
promise; the break-even is computed in the next paragraph.

Break-even on deferral, derived rather than asserted. Let `delta` be the abandonment probability for
a line moved from P2 to P4, `m` the margin on a collected order, and `c` the recovery cost avoided by
not breaking a promise. Deferring is worthwhile when `delta * m < c`, so the break-even abandonment is
`delta* = c/m`. With `c = 4.50 USD` and `m = 12.00 USD`, `delta* = 0.375`. The constructed funnel shows
a 3.8% abandonment rate on deferred lines, well under the 37.5% break-even, so the decision is robust
to a tenfold error in the abandonment estimate. If margins were thin — say `m = 5.00 USD` — the
break-even would be `0.90`, and any meaningful abandonment would reverse the recommendation. This is
the kind of sensitivity a manager can act on: the decision does not depend on precise funnel
measurement at current margins, and it does depend on them at low margins.

The claim that is *not* made: that this design was deployed, that it produced these numbers in
production, or that any retailer's promise accuracy is described here. The numbers are constructed,
the arithmetic is verified, and the structure of the argument is the transferable part.

Takeaway: separate the three questions — who should be promised, how many can be promised, and how
fast can the store actually pick — because collapsing them into one model score is what produced the
inversion.

## 8. System/Solution Design

The design has four components and one rule connecting them: the gate runs before the model, never
after it.

```text
                    +-------------------------------+
  [order line] ---> | 1. snapshot assembler         |  as-of join on t_quote
                    |    publish snap_id, refuse    |  lineage manifest check (C-01)
                    |    post-quote columns         |
                    +---------------+---------------+
                                    |
                                    v
                    +-------------------------------+     roster v41 ----+
                    | 2. slot capacity resolver     |                   |
                    |    K_slot = floor(P*L_eff/S)  |<------------------+
                    |    counts carried-in promises |     n_mean from quoted lines
                    +---------------+---------------+
                                    |
                        gate: slot_load < K_slot ?
                          |                        |
                          no                       yes
                          |                        |
                          v                        v
              +-------------------+   +-------------------------------+
              | route to next tier|   | 3. risk ranker (GBM)          |
              | re-run gate there |   |    orders candidates by       |
              +-------------------+   |    stockout risk, not by p    |
                          |           +---------------+---------------+ 
                          |                           |
                          v                           v
              +---------------------------------------------------+
              | 4. decision writer: tier, snap_id, gate inputs,   |
              |    rank, one-sentence rationale, revert handle    |
              +-------------------+-------------------------------+
                                  |
                                  v
                        [decision log, append-only]
                                  |
                                  +--> [nightly metric job: per-tier, pooled, fixed yardstick]
                                  +--> [review queue, capped at 40/store-day]
```

**Component 1 — snapshot assembler.** Its only job is to produce a frozen input set and refuse to
produce one that violates the lineage rule. The refusal is a build failure, not a warning, because a
warning is a thing people scroll past. The manifest is a list of `(column, source table, validity
start, permitted)` tuples, and the check is `max(validity_start) <= t_quote` evaluated per decision
batch, with an exception list that is empty in the shipped configuration.

**Component 2 — slot capacity resolver.** It owns three numbers per slot: `P` from the published
roster, `L_eff` from a configured availability factor with a per-store override, and `n_mean` from
the lines already quoted into that slot. It counts promises *landing* in the slot, including those
quoted in the previous ninety minutes whose deadlines fall inside it. It exposes `K_slot` and the
current `slot_load` and its verdict, and it is the only component allowed to answer "can this slot
take another P2 promise".

The resolver's failure behaviour is the design decision worth stating. If the roster is missing for a
slot — a late publication, a store-level override — the resolver does not default to the previous
day's roster, because yesterday's roster is a guess dressed as data. It returns `K_slot = 0` for that
slot, which routes every candidate to the next tier, which means the store promises P4 rather than
gambling on P2. That is a deliberate degradation: the customer gets a true four-hour promise instead
of a 74%-likely two-hour promise, and the store avoids the recovery cost. The resolver logs the
degradation reason so the next morning's review can see how many slots were degraded.

**Component 3 — risk ranker.** This is the model, used narrowly. Its output is an ordering of
candidates within a slot by the probability that the pick will fail or be slow for a reason the
promise could avoid — stockout risk, substituted-line risk, bulky-item risk. It does not set the tier;
it decides which candidates occupy the scarce admissions. Two properties follow: the model can be
replaced without touching the gate, and the model can be refit on a population that is not selected
by the promise, because its target is "pick failed or re-picked", which is observed for every line
regardless of what tier was promised.

That second property is the quiet fix for the D-07 bias. A target defined on the pick event rather
than on the promise event is not conditioned on the policy, so the counterfactual bias that broke the
first design cannot enter through the label. It does not eliminate all regime dependence — a
priority-treated line is still picked sooner — but it removes the specific conditioning that made
the score a restatement of the historical rule.

**Component 4 — decision writer.** It emits one row per line containing: `snap_id`, `t_quote`,
`gate_K`, `gate_load`, `gate_verdict`, `rank_score`, `tier_chosen`, `rationale` (a template filled
from the gate inputs and the top two risk drivers), and `revert_handle`. The rationale template is
deliberately mechanical — "P2 granted: slot 10:00 load 91 of 104, stockout risk 0.12, two lines" —
because a rationale a human cannot reproduce from the log is not an explanation.

Two invariants are enforced in the writer rather than in a downstream check, because a downstream
check reports a violation while a writer prevents one:

```text
INV-1  per effective window: count(effective actions per decision_id) <= 1
       enforced by a unique constraint on (decision_id, effective_window)
INV-2  per slot: slot_load_after <= gate_K, unless gate_verdict = 'degraded'
       enforced by a conditional write that fails if the increment would exceed K
INV-3  every action row references a snap_id present in the snapshot registry
       enforced by a foreign key, which also blocks replay against a rebuilt snapshot
```

INV-2 is the interesting one. A conditional write that fails when the load would exceed `K` turns a
race between two concurrent quotes into a retry rather than an oversubscription. Without it, a
resolver that reads `slot_load = 103, K = 104` and is called twice concurrently will admit both
candidates and exceed the gate by one. At 4 pickers this is a rounding error; at 40 concurrent quote
requests it is the difference between a gate and a suggestion. The constructed store sees about 9
concurrent quote requests per second at peak, which is enough to make the race routine.

Recovery and replay:

| Failure | Detection | Behaviour | Replay check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Snapshot build fails lineage | manifest assertion throws | batch blocked, alert to steward, quotes served by incumbent ledger | rebuild from raw and diff |
| Roster missing for a slot | resolver returns `K = 0` | all candidates degrade to next tier | count degraded slots per day |
| Ranker unavailable | health probe fails | candidates ordered by a static risk table (bulky, low-velocity, promo) | compare admission sets |
| Decision writer unavailable | write timeout | order held, customer shown "confirming" for up to 20 s, then P4 default | count holds and defaults |
| Metric job fails | no output by 05:00 | previous day's report re-published with a staleness banner | compare cohort sizes |
| Reversal requested | operator action | writer emits compensating row, never an update | confirm both rows survive |

The replay check for each row is the difference between an operable system and a system that seems
operable. A reversal that updates in place destroys the evidence of the original decision and makes
the override-rate metric uncomputable; a reversal that appends a compensating row preserves both.

Takeaway: put the capacity gate in front of the model and the invariants in the writer, because a
gate applied after ranking cannot undo a promise that has already been shown to a customer.
## 9. Evaluation & Validation

Evaluation in this case is not a model report. It is a claim that a different tier-assignment policy
would have produced different outcomes on the same population, and that claim needs seven things
stated before the result is read: numerator, denominator, population, window, comparator, guardrails,
and the uncertainty convention. Each is fixed below, in that order, with the executed/not-executed
status attached.

### 9.1 Primary metric definition

| Element | Fixed definition | Why this and not the alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Numerator | lines whose `t_notify − t_quote ≤ 120 min` | notification is the earliest moment the customer can collect |
| Denominator | the 520 lines the incumbent rule quoted at P2 | a fixed, policy-independent population |
| Population | one store, one promotion day, 14 slot instances | enough slot instances to see capacity bind, few enough to trace by hand |
| Window | quote instant to 240 minutes, truncated | beyond four hours the line is PN regardless of policy |
| Comparator | incumbent ledger rule reproduced with its own staleness | a baseline given better data is not the baseline |
| Aggregation | per-tier rate, pooled rate, fixed yardstick | three numbers because one number was the defect |
| Rounding | rates to four decimals, counts integer, no re-rounding between steps | prevents 0.86 vs 0.8596 discrepancies in a review |
| Status | **executed** on constructed data; **not executed** on production data | the arithmetic is real, the cohort is invented |

The primary metric is paired with a mandatory companion, because the case's whole argument is that
the primary metric alone can be gamed by deferral:

```text
FORMULA [E-01]: r_kept(tier) = Kept(tier) / Promised(tier)
                r_fixed      = Kept_within_120 / Eligible
                mix_gain     = r_kept(P2) - r_fixed
WHERE:
  r_kept(tier) [fraction]  promise-relative rate, moves when the tier mix moves
  r_fixed      [fraction]  yardstick rate, does not move when the tier mix moves
  mix_gain     [fraction]  the part of an apparent improvement that is definitional
CONSTRAINT:
  Eligible is fixed before any policy runs and does not shrink when a policy defers.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (constructed):
  gate-only policy:        r_kept(P2) = 447/487 = 0.91786
                           r_fixed    = 447/520 = 0.85962
                           mix_gain   = 0.91786 - 0.85962 = 0.05824
  gate + risk ranking:     r_kept(P2) = 447/465 = 0.96129
                           r_fixed    = 447/520 = 0.85962
                           mix_gain   = 0.96129 - 0.85962 = 0.10167
  gate + ranking + cycle counts:
                           r_kept(P2) = 469/487 = 0.96304
                           r_fixed    = 469/520 = 0.90192
                           mix_gain   = 0.96304 - 0.90192 = 0.06112
INTERPRETATION:
  mix_gain is the portion of the improvement that a reader should not attribute to operational speed.
  Reporting it explicitly converts a potential accusation into a disclosed quantity.
```

The three rows separate the contributions cleanly. The gate alone buys 5.82 points, all definitional.
Adding the risk ranker buys another 4.34 points, also definitional — 22 risky promises withheld, not
22 picks accelerated. Only the cycle-count row moves `r_fixed` at all, and in that row `mix_gain`
falls to 6.11 points because 4.23 points of genuine speed appeared. A team reporting only `r_kept(P2)`
would describe a 10.34-point improvement; the honest decomposition is 6.11 points of promise integrity
and 4.23 points of shelf accuracy, and the second one was delivered by a person with a scanner.

### 9.2 Guardrail metrics

Guardrails are the quantities that must not degrade while the primary improves. Each has a threshold
and a consequence, because a guardrail without a consequence is a dashboard tile.

| Guardrail | Numerator / denominator | Threshold | Consequence of breach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deferral rate | lines moved off P2 / eligible | ≤ 0.10 | freeze gate; review `L_eff` and roster for the affected slots |
| Abandonment on deferred lines | orders cancelled within 2 h of deferral / deferred orders | ≤ 0.08 | re-price the tier menu before continuing |
| Manual abstention volume | manual tier decisions / store-day | ≤ 40 | cap automated abstention at observed capacity |
| Override rate | operator reversals / automated decisions | ≤ 0.05 | audit rationale templates for the reversed stratum |
| Stockout-driven lateness | lines late with first failure = stockout / late lines | ≤ 0.50 | escalate cycle-count coverage |
| P2 floor by store-week | kept P2 lines / promised P2 lines, per week | ≥ 0.90 | automated tiering suspended for that store |
| Notification delay | median `t_notify − t_stage` | ≤ 6 min | treat as an integration incident, not a model issue |

The override-rate guardrail is the most diagnostically useful and the least monitored in practice. A
rising override rate says an operator consistently disagrees with the engine on a stratum that the
engine believes is easy. In the constructed shadow log the override rate reached 0.11 in the
14:00–16:00 slot and the cause was a store-specific shelf reset that moved the high-velocity SKUs to
a second aisle, doubling `S_pick` for those lines while the roster was unchanged. No amount of
retraining would have found that; the overrides did, in three days.

### 9.3 Uncertainty

Three sources of uncertainty need different treatments, and conflating them is how a point estimate
acquires false precision.

*Sampling uncertainty on the rate.* With `n = 520` and `r̂ = 0.8596`, the standard error of a
proportion is `sqrt(r̂(1−r̂)/n) = sqrt(0.8596 × 0.1404 / 520) = sqrt(0.12069/520) = sqrt(0.0002321) =
0.01523`. A 95% interval is `0.8596 ± 1.96 × 0.01523 = [0.8298, 0.8894]`. Compare the gate-only
policy at `447/487`: `sqrt(0.9179 × 0.0821 / 487) = sqrt(0.07536/487) = sqrt(0.0001548) = 0.01244`,
interval `[0.8935, 0.9423]`. The two intervals do not overlap, so the difference survives sampling
error — but note that the difference is entirely a denominator change, which no confidence interval
can reveal.

Cluster the check by slot, because lines within a slot are not independent: they share pickers, a
queue, and a shelf state. With 14 slot-instances the effective sample size for capacity effects is
closer to 14 than to 520. The constructed within-slot intraclass correlation for the late indicator
is 0.18, which inflates the rate's variance by a design effect of `1 + (m−1)ρ` with `m ≈ 37` lines
per slot: `1 + 36 × 0.18 = 7.48`. The variance is 7.5 times the naive one, and the honest interval on
the baseline rate widens to roughly `± 0.042` rather than `± 0.030`. That interval now overlaps the
gate-only interval, which correctly says: with 14 slots, the gate's rate improvement is not
distinguishable from noise on this cohort, and the reason to adopt it is arithmetic (the gate cannot
exceed `K`) rather than the observed rate difference.

*Input uncertainty.* `L_eff = 96` minutes is an assumption with a plausible range of 88 to 104. At
88 minutes, `K` for the 10:00–12:00 slot is `floor(4 × 88 / 6.6) = floor(53.33) = 53` orders and 95
lines; at 104 minutes it is `floor(4 × 104 / 6.6) = floor(63.03) = 63` orders and 113 lines. The
feasible-line range `[95, 113]` brackets the point value of 104, and the gate's deferral count ranges
from 15 to 33. That is a wide band for a single input, and it is the strongest argument for measuring
`L_eff` from the assignment log rather than configuring it from memory.

*Structural uncertainty.* The largest unknown is whether the deployment regime would reproduce the
training regime's priority treatment. If P2 lines continue to receive pick priority, the gate's
deferral improves everyone's lot slightly by relieving the queue; if priority is removed as part of a
fairness change, the remaining P2 lines get slower and the gate's benefit shrinks. No data in this
case can settle that, and the case says so rather than assuming the favourable branch.

### 9.4 What was validated, and by what

| Claim | Validation performed | Status | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| The cohort is internally consistent | reconciliation to 1,180 rows, 5 exclusion classes, all counts matched | executed | constructed data, so consistency is by construction |
| Capacity arithmetic | recomputed `K_slot` for all 14 slot instances at three `L_eff` values | executed | service-time model understates observed mean by 0.3 min |
| The inversion is real | recomputed 447/520 vs 389/468 from the same cohort | executed | the inversion magnitude is an input, not a finding |
| The gate removes only mechanical lateness | attributed 73 late lines to 27 mechanical + 46 residual | executed | spill correction of 6 lines is estimated, not traced |
| Risk ranking earns 22 lines | attributed residual by first failing event, hand-read | executed | 5 lines in two categories were hard to assign |
| Fixed yardstick rises to 0.9019 | 469 lines within 120 min, requiring 11 recovered stockouts | **not executed** | depends on a cycle-count programme not modelled here |
| Customers prefer a kept four-hour promise to a broken two-hour one | not tested | **not executed** | needs a preference study or a choice experiment |
| The design improves revenue | not tested | **not executed** | the case makes no revenue claim |

The last three rows are the ones a reader should carry into any discussion. The case's arithmetic
supports a claim about promise integrity and a claim about which lines are addressable. It does not
support a claim about customer preference, revenue, or the effect of cycle counts.

### 9.5 Validation design for a real deployment

If a store wanted to test this rather than reason about it, the design that would answer the question
is a slot-level cluster randomisation, not a line-level one. Lines within a slot share capacity, so
line-level randomisation leaks: a control line and a treated line in the same slot compete for the
same picker, and the control's outcome is contaminated by the treatment. Slot-level assignment —
alternate the gate on and off by slot-instance, or by store-day — avoids the leak.

Power, computed rather than asserted. To detect a 5-point improvement in `r_fixed` from 0.86 at 80%
power with a two-sided 5% test, the required sample per arm is approximately
`n = 2 × (1.96 + 0.84)² × p(1−p) / Δ² = 2 × 7.84 × 0.1204 / 0.0025 = 2 × 7.84 × 48.16 = 755 lines`
per arm before clustering. With a design effect of 7.48 from slot-level clustering, the requirement
becomes roughly `755 × 7.48 ≈ 5,647` lines per arm, or about 11 days at 520 lines/day in one store —
or four stores for three days. That is the honest cost of measuring the effect, and it is why the
recommendation in section 10 is a shadow log rather than an immediate A/B test.

Takeaway: state the denominator and the design effect before reading the rate, because the same 5-point
difference is decisive at `n = 5,000` and indistinguishable from noise at `n = 520`.

## 10. Action Taken

No production change was made, and this section separates the proposed actions from any executed
result. The organisation in the case is a constructed one; the sequence below is the sequence the
analysis supports, with owners and exit criteria, not a log of what happened.

**A-1 Shadow the gate, keep the incumbent promise (proposed, first two weeks).** Compute `gate_K` and
`gate_verdict` for every quote in parallel with the incumbent rule, write both to the decision log,
and show the customer the incumbent tier. Owner: fulfilment manager; instrument: decision service in
shadow mode. Exit criterion: for ten consecutive days, the gate's `slot_load` agrees with the load
recomputed from the assignment log to within 2 lines in at least 90% of slot instances.

The shadow period is not ceremony. It is the only way to discover `L_eff` empirically, and in the
constructed sequence the shadow log moved the estimate from a configured 96 minutes to an observed
92, which cut `K` for the 10:00–12:00 slot from 58 orders to 55 and reduced the deferral count from 24
to 28. A gate deployed with a configured `L_eff` would have been 5% optimistic for as long as nobody
looked.

**A-2 Fix the ready timestamp (proposed, week one, parallel with A-1).** Adopt `t_notify` as the
ready time in every report, backfill 90 days, and publish the staging-to-notification gap
distribution. Owner: data steward. Exit criterion: all promise reports cite `t_notify`; the gap
median and p90 are published per store. This has no modelling content and it changes every number
the business has been reading, which is why it must precede any claim of improvement.

In the constructed backfill the gap median was 4.5 minutes and the p90 was 19 minutes. Historical
reports built on the staging scan therefore overstated the promise-kept rate by roughly 3 to 6 points
depending on the store, and the stores with the worst notification integration looked like the best
pickers. That is a specific, checkable defect rather than a general worry.

**A-3 Adopt the gate for one tier, one store (proposed, week three, conditional).** If A-1 and A-2
pass, enable the gate for the P2 tier in one store, with the P4 route as the automatic fallback and
the abstention cap at the observed manual capacity. Owner: fulfilment manager, co-signed by
e-commerce. Exit criteria: deferral rate ≤ 0.10; abandonment on deferred lines ≤ 0.08; override rate
≤ 0.05; P2 floor ≥ 0.90 for two consecutive weeks.

**A-4 Rank within the gate, not above it (proposed, week five, conditional on A-3).** Enable the risk
ranker to order candidates inside the gate, with the label changed from promise outcome to pick
failure or re-pick. Owner: data science, with the fulfilment manager as the accepting operator. Exit
criterion: at least 15 of the 22 residual stockout lines correctly ordered below the admission cut
over two weeks.

**A-5 Cycle counts on the top 40 velocity SKUs (proposed, week one, independent of the model).**
This is the only action that moves the fixed yardstick, and it does not depend on any modelling
result. Owner: store operations. Exit criterion: on-hand accuracy on the counted SKUs improves from
the audited baseline to within 2 percentage points, measured by recount.

A-5 deserves emphasis because it is the cheapest and the most likely to be dropped. It requires no
data science, no decision service, and no policy change; it requires a person with a scanner and a
list. In the constructed attribution it recovers about half of the 22 stockout lines, which is more
fixed-yardstick improvement than the entire modelling programme delivers. A team that ships A-4 and
skips A-5 has optimised the visible artifact and left the largest addressable cause untouched.

**Deliberately not done, with reasons.** No threshold tuning to improve the 0.831 figure: tuning a
threshold on a biased score produces a better number and the same defect. No retraining on the full
1,180-line cohort: the extra 660 lines were quoted at other tiers under different treatment, so
including them without a treatment indicator would add a second regime rather than more data. No
customer-facing countdown widget: the tier menu is the commitment, and a countdown adds precision the
store cannot honour. No extension to ship-from-store in the same change: the capacity model would
need a delivery-route term and the cohort has no route data.

Takeaway: order the actions by what they can move — timestamp correction and cycle counts first,
gate second, ranking third — and refuse the tuning that improves the number without touching the
mechanism.
## 11. Outcome & Results

**Status: not-measured.** No production system, pilot, or customer population is described here. What
is established is arithmetic on constructed inputs, and this section states which arithmetic, with
its numbers, and what remains unknown.

### 11.1 The four results that are established as arithmetic

| Result | Construction | Verified value | Independent re-check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline promise-kept rate at P2 | 447 kept of 520 promised | 0.85962 | `447 × 100 / 520 = 85.962`; the three strata sum to 447 |
| Per-line-threshold policy | 389 kept of 468 promised | 0.83120 | `389 × 100 / 468 = 83.120`; differs from baseline by −2.84 points |
| Gate-only policy | 447 kept of 487 promised | 0.91786 | `487 = 520 − 33`; `447 × 100 / 487 = 91.786` |
| Gate + rank + counts | 469 kept of 487 promised | 0.96304 | `469 = 447 + 22`; `469 × 100 / 487 = 96.304` |

The re-check column exists because the three rates share a numerator in two of four rows, which is
exactly the condition under which a transcription error hides: change 447 to 474 and every rate rises
by about 5 points while the ratios still look plausible. The check that catches this is the count
identity `kept + late = promised`, applied per row. For the gate-only row: `447 + 40 = 487`, and 40 is
the residual late count after mechanical deferral — consistent with the 46 residual minus the 6 lines
absorbed by spill. For the final row: `469 + 18 = 487`, and 18 is the residual after the 22 stockout
lines were withheld.

### 11.2 The quantity that did not change, and why it matters

The fixed yardstick is 447 lines within 120 minutes in the baseline, the gate-only policy, and the
gate-plus-ranking policy. It is the same number three times, and that invariance is the case's central
result rather than an omission.

Nothing in the first three policies makes a single pick faster. The gate moves 33 promises out of a
slot that cannot serve them; the ranker moves 22 more, chosen for stockout risk. Neither changes `W_q`
for the lines that remain, neither adds a picker, neither shortens a walk. The customer who would have
arrived at 12:20 for a 12:00 promise still arrives at 12:20 — they were simply told 14:00 instead, and
they arrived at 13:40 having been told the truth.

The fourth policy does change the yardstick, and only because a person recounts a shelf. That is the
distinction the case insists on: promise integrity is a communication property, and fulfilment speed
is a physical property, and a programme that reports one while claiming the other is the failure mode
this case was written to expose.

### 11.3 Effect sizes, with the honest intervals

Using the cluster-adjusted standard error from section 9.3 — design effect 7.48, so the naive
`0.01523` becomes `0.01523 × sqrt(7.48) = 0.01523 × 2.735 = 0.04166` — the baseline interval is
`0.8596 ± 1.96 × 0.04166 = [0.7779, 0.9413]`. The gate-only interval, with
`sqrt(0.9179 × 0.0821 / 487) = 0.01244` inflated by the same factor, is `0.01244 × 2.735 = 0.03402`,
giving `[0.8512, 0.9845]`.

These overlap across almost their whole width. The correct reading is not "the gate does not work" but
"a 14-slot cohort cannot establish the size of the gate's rate effect, and the case does not claim it
does". The case's argument for the gate rests on a feasibility identity — a slot with 104 feasible
lines cannot keep 128 promises — which needs no confidence interval, and on the fixed-yardstick
invariance, which is definitional.

That split between an identity argument and an estimate argument is worth generalising. When a
proposed change is justified by an inequality, the sample size question is irrelevant to whether the
change is correct and relevant only to how large the benefit is. When it is justified by an observed
difference, the sample size question is the whole argument. Teams routinely present an inequality
argument with an estimate's confidence interval attached, which makes a structural claim look fragile
and invites exactly the wrong objection.

### 11.4 What the constructed cohort shows about where lateness lives

| Category | Lines | Share of 73 late | Reaches the customer as an apology? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mechanical (capacity) | 27 | 0.370 | no — promise was never honourable |
| Stockout at pick | 22 | 0.301 | yes — substitution or refund offered |
| Picker absence | 9 | 0.123 | yes — delayed notification |
| Notification integration | 8 | 0.110 | sometimes — item was ready earlier |
| Re-pick after error | 5 | 0.068 | yes — wrong item corrected |
| Customer arrived early | 2 | 0.027 | no — customer was ahead of the notice |

Check the shares: `0.370 + 0.301 + 0.123 + 0.110 + 0.068 + 0.027 = 0.999`, within rounding of 1.000.
And the counts: `27 + 22 + 9 + 8 + 5 + 2 = 73`, matching the baseline's 520 − 447.

The table's fourth column is the one an operations review should look at first. Only 44 of the 73 late
lines — 60% — actually produce an apologetic customer interaction. The 27 mechanical lines produce a
customer who was told four hours and got their item in three and a half, which is a satisfied
customer; the 2 early arrivals produce a customer who was told two hours, arrived in one, and waited.
Counting all 73 as service failures overstates the harm by a factor of about 1.7, and counting only the
44 understates the harm caused by over-promising, because the mechanical lines were promises the
store should never have made.

### 11.5 Residual unknowns

Four questions remain open and no amount of arithmetic on this cohort can close them.

Whether customers accept the deferred tier. The abandonment estimate of 3.8% is constructed and the
break-even was 37.5%, so the decision tolerates a large error — but a real store should measure it,
because at thin margins the break-even collapses to 0.90 and any abandonment matters.

Whether the priority treatment persists. If P2 lines keep pick priority, the gate's benefit holds; if
priority is removed, the remaining P2 lines slow down and the gate's measured gain shrinks. The case
cannot resolve this and does not assume the favourable branch.

Whether `L_eff` is stable. The shadow recommendation exists to measure it. A store whose `L_eff` swings
between 80 and 105 minutes has a capacity gate that oscillates, and an oscillating gate produces
promise patterns customers notice.

Whether the notification delay is separable. Eight late lines were ready before the customer was told.
If that integration can be fixed, the yardstick rises by up to 8 lines for free — `455/520 = 0.8750` —
with no capacity or modelling work at all. This is the cheapest unexploited gain in the cohort and it
sits in an integration team's backlog, not in a data science one.

Takeaway: report the invariance, report the intervals honestly, and separate what the arithmetic
establishes from what an organisation would still have to measure.

## 12. Failure Modes & What Went Wrong

Six mechanisms broke this design or came close enough to be worth documenting. Each is stated with its
symptom, the competing causes that produce the same symptom, a check that discriminates among them,
and the correction with its own cost. The discipline of listing competing causes is what separates a
diagnosis from a guess: "the score looks too good" has at least four causes and they have four
different fixes.

### F-01 The label is generated by the policy under test

**Symptom.** Offline AUC improves from 0.78 to 0.81 across two refits, the model is promoted, and the
first month of live promise-kept rate falls by 2.8 points. The offline and live numbers move in
opposite directions and nobody can reconcile them.

**Competing causes.** (a) Distribution shift in the served population, because a promotion changed the
basket mix. (b) The label is policy-conditioned, so the refit learned the historical rule's priority
treatment. (c) A serving bug, where a feature computed differently in batch and online. (d) Genuine
model decay from a shelf reset.

**Discriminating check.** Stratify the held-out set by whether the line received pick priority and fit
a separate calibration curve for each stratum. If the curves differ by more than 3 points in the
operating band, cause (b) is present. To separate (b) from (c), score the same 500 records through the
batch path and the online path and diff the feature vectors; a mismatch of any magnitude identifies
(c) immediately. To separate (a) from (d), compare the basket-size and bulk-class distributions between
training and serving weeks; a shift identifies (a).

**Correction.** Move the target off the promise and onto the pick event — "was this line picked clean
on the first attempt" — which is observed for every line regardless of the tier quoted. Costs a refit
and a re-validation; buys a target that cannot be moved by the policy.

**Cost of the correction.** Two to three weeks of relabelling and re-validation, plus a period in which
the old score is still in the decision path. The cheaper alternative — a threshold retune — was tried
in the constructed sequence and moved the live rate by 0.4 points, because it corrected the symptom
without touching the conditioning.

### F-02 The capacity gate reads a stale ledger

**Symptom.** Slot load as recorded by the gate is 96 of 104 at 11:40; the assignment log shows 118
tasks created for that slot by 12:00. Twenty-two more promises were admitted than the gate believed.

**Competing causes.** (a) The ledger is updated twice daily, so the gate is reading a value up to six
hours old. (b) Concurrent quote requests race on a read-then-write without a conditional increment. (c)
Promises quoted in the previous slot land in this slot and are not counted. (d) Some tasks are created
without a promise, so the assignment log overstates promise load.

**Discriminating check.** Recompute slot load from the assignment log for every slot, then compare to
the gate's recorded load at 10-minute marks. If the discrepancy grows monotonically through the day,
the cause is (a). If it appears in a spike at a specific second, the cause is (b) — and the check is to
count quote requests within any 200-millisecond window, which in the constructed peak reaches 9. For
(c), compare the deadline histogram of promises to the creation histogram of tasks; a 90-minute offset
identifies carried-in promises. For (d), join task records to promise records and count unmatched
tasks.

**Correction.** Read capacity from the assignment log rather than the ledger, count promises by
deadline rather than by quote time, and make the admission a conditional write that fails on overflow
(INV-2). The three fixes address (a), (c) and (b) respectively and none of them requires a model.

**Residual risk.** Even with all three, a roster change mid-slot (a picker sent home) invalidates the
gate until the resolver re-reads the roster. The design accepts this and handles it by re-running the
resolver on every roster event, which is cheap because the roster changes rarely.

### F-03 The pooled rate rises while every tier falls

**Symptom.** The weekly dashboard shows the pooled promise-kept rate improving from 0.86 to 0.91 over
four weeks. Store managers report more complaints. Both are true.

**Competing causes.** (a) The tier mix shifted toward PN, which is nearly always kept. (b) The
denominator changed, because fewer lines were promised at all. (c) Complaints rose because absolute
volume rose even as the rate improved. (d) The notification timestamp was fixed mid-period, raising
every rate definitionally.

**Discriminating check.** Compute the pooled rate two ways: with actual weights and with the baseline
week's weights held fixed (a direct standardisation). If the fixed-weight series is flat while the
actual series rises, the cause is (a). Then check the promised-line count per week; a fall identifies
(b). Compare complaint counts to complaint rates per thousand orders to separate (c). For (d), look for
a step change coincident with a release date, and check whether the staging-to-notification gap
distribution shifted.

**Correction.** Publish per-tier rates and the fixed-weight pooled rate as the headline, and demote the
actual-weight pooled rate to a diagnostic. This changes no system and prevents the entire class of
misreading.

**Why it recurs.** A pooled rate is one number, one number fits in a slide, and the mix decomposition
takes three columns. The correction is a reporting convention, and reporting conventions decay when
the person who introduced them changes role — which is why the metric dictionary (§5e) names the
convention explicitly rather than relying on practice.

### F-04 The abstention queue becomes the decision

**Symptom.** After four weeks, 31% of automated tier decisions are abstentions, the fulfilment lead is
spending three hours a day on manual tiering, and the automated decisions that remain are all easy
cases.

**Competing causes.** (a) The abstention rule is triggered by missing features and the feature
completeness in the peak slot is 0.951, so abstention concentrates exactly where volume is highest.
(b) The ranker's uncertainty band is wide for new SKUs, and new-SKU share rose with a seasonal launch.
(c) Operators manually reverse easy decisions, which the engine interprets as evidence and routes
similar cases to abstention. (d) The cap on manual decisions was set at an assumed 40 per day and never
measured.

**Discriminating check.** Break abstentions down by trigger reason and by slot. If abstentions cluster
in peak slots and correlate with the completeness rate, the cause is (a). If they cluster on new SKUs,
(b). To test (c), check whether the override rate on abstained cases is higher than on automated cases;
if so, the engine is learning from its own overrides, which is a feedback loop rather than a signal.
For (d), count the actual manual decisions for a week; the constructed count was 67 per day against
the assumed 40.

**Correction.** Impute missing features from the store-SKU lagged value and mark the decision
"degraded-rationale" rather than abstaining; cap abstention at the measured capacity; and exclude
operator overrides from any feature or threshold derivation, logging them for audit only.

**The general mechanism.** An abstention path that has no budget will absorb the hard cases until it
becomes the primary decision path, at which point the automation is decorative. The budget is the
design, not the paperwork.

### F-05 The service-time model is fitted on clean picks

**Symptom.** Modelled `S_mean` is 6.6 minutes; the observed mean for the same slot is 6.9. The gate
admits 58 orders where 55 were feasible, and the overshoot is invisible because both numbers are
plausible.

**Competing causes.** (a) The service-time sample excludes substituted lines, which take longer. (b)
The sample excludes re-picks, which take roughly twice as long. (c) The handheld clocks drift, so
measured durations are biased. (d) The sample is drawn from off-peak slots where walking is
unobstructed.

**Discriminating check.** Recompute the mean including substituted and re-picked lines; if it rises to
6.9, causes (a) and (b) explain the gap. Compare handheld-derived durations to `t_notify`-derived
durations for lines with both; a systematic offset identifies (c). Compare peak and off-peak means
directly; a 0.3-minute difference concentrated in peak identifies (d).

**Correction.** Fit the service-time model on all completed pick tasks without exclusions, and apply a
slot-specific inflation of 1.05 in peak slots until the observed mean is re-estimated. Costs a
5% reduction in admitted promises, which is the honest number.

**Why this one is dangerous.** The error is 4.3% and in the optimistic direction, which means every
capacity-derived claim is slightly too good and no individual decision looks wrong. Errors of this size
do not trigger alarms; they accumulate into a slot that is chronically 3 orders oversubscribed, which
reads as "the pickers are slow today" rather than as a model defect.

### F-06 Replay is impossible because the snapshot was rebuilt

**Symptom.** Six weeks after a decision, a customer complaint requires reconstructing why a specific
line was promised two hours. The feature build is re-run, the on-hand value differs by two units, and
the rationale in the log no longer matches anything reproducible.

**Competing causes.** (a) The feature build reads live tables instead of a frozen snapshot. (b) The
snapshot exists but the build was re-pointed at a newer partition. (c) A backfill corrected the source
data and the correction propagated. (d) The rationale template was changed and old rows used a
different template.

**Discriminating check.** Take the `snap_id` from the decision row and check that the snapshot registry
contains it with a creation time before `t_quote`. If absent, cause (a) or (b). If present, rebuild
from that snapshot and diff the feature vector; a difference despite an identical snapshot id
identifies (c), because the snapshot was not actually immutable. For (d), group rationales by template
version; a mixture within one day identifies it.

**Correction.** Write the feature vector itself into the decision row, not just the snapshot id. The
storage cost is trivial at this volume — 15 numeric features at 8 bytes each is 120 bytes per line,
about 62 KB for a 520-line day — and it makes the decision self-describing forever.

**The trade-off worth naming.** Storing the vector duplicates data and creates a second source of
truth that can drift from the snapshot. The case prefers the duplication because a rationale that
cannot be reproduced is not an audit trail, and because the drift between a stored vector and its
snapshot is detectable by the very diff procedure that (a) and (b) make impossible.

Takeaway: attach each failure to a check that distinguishes it from its look-alikes, and prefer fixes
that remove a class of failure over fixes that remove an instance.

## 13. Transferable Lesson

The lesson generalises to any setting where an intervention is chosen using a model whose target is
defined by a previous version of that intervention. Five statements carry it.

**One: when the label is a function of the action, the label cannot evaluate the action.** The
promise-kept rate is not a property of the store; it is a property of the store relative to what the
store said. Any metric with this structure — SLA attainment, on-time delivery against quoted dates,
service-level compliance against a ticket priority, retention against a stated renewal term — moves
when the target moves. The defence is to keep an untransformed event (ready time, delivery scan,
resolution timestamp) and re-score candidate policies against it. The untransformed event is the only
quantity that a policy cannot negotiate with.

**Two: put the binding constraint in front of the ranking, not behind it.** Every ranked list has a
capacity behind it: picker-minutes, adjuster hours, truck slots, clinician appointments. A ranking
applied before the capacity check produces a queue that is longer than the resource, and the overflow
is not a modelling error — it is an unserved commitment. Applying the gate first converts the problem
into "which candidates deserve the scarce admissions", which is a question the ranking can actually
answer well.

**Three: separate the three questions a promise system answers.** Who should be promised, how many can
be promised, and how fast can the operation actually go are three questions with three different data
sources: a risk model, a roster, and a clock. Collapsing them into one score is convenient and it
destroys the ability to attribute a change to a cause. The case's decomposition — 6.11 points of
promise integrity from the gate, 4.23 points of speed from cycle counts — is only computable because
the three questions were kept apart.

**Four: measure the invariant, not the average.** `K_slot` is an inequality and it is either satisfied
or violated for every slot on every day. Averages hide violations: a store that meets capacity on
thirteen days and oversubscribes by 40% on the fourteenth has a good average and a broken gate. Report
the violation count, the maximum overshoot, and the number of degraded slots, because those are the
quantities that predict the complaint.

**Five: the cheapest fix is usually upstream of the model.** In this case the cheapest fix moved the
yardstick more than the whole modelling programme: recount 40 SKUs, and correct the ready timestamp
from the staging scan to the notification send. Neither needs a model, both need a person and a
decision, and both are routinely deferred because they do not produce a chart. A programme that funds
the model and defers the recount has chosen the interesting work over the effective work.

The transfer conditions matter as much as the lesson. The method transfers to a setting where a
discrete commitment is made under a shared capacity constraint and the outcome arrives later than the
commitment. It does not transfer to settings where the commitment is continuous (a price, a bid), where
capacity is elastic within the commitment window (a cloud service with autoscaling), or where the
outcome is observable at commitment time (a counter sale). In those settings the same pattern appears
in a different form and needs a different gate.

A concrete second application, stated briefly to show the transfer is not decorative. A claims
operation promises a settlement decision within five working days. The label "decided within five
days" is generated by the promise policy, and a model predicting it learns which claims were routed to
the fast lane. The transferable design is: keep the raw decision timestamp, gate on adjuster-minutes
per day rather than on a queue count, and rank within the gate by the risk that a claim needs
additional evidence. The numbers differ completely; the structure is identical, and so is the failure
if the gate is applied after the ranking.

Takeaway: the pattern is the ordering — untransformed event, capacity inequality, then ranking — and
it holds wherever a commitment outlives the information used to make it.

### Interview pack

Sixteen questions, ordered from framing through arithmetic to judgment. Questions 3–11 and 14 require
a computation, a selection, or a diagnosis; the rest require a defensible judgment. Each answer stands
alone because a candidate who has memorised one framing will otherwise answer all sixteen identically.

**Q1. In one sentence, why is a model with AUC 0.81 worse than a spreadsheet with no model?**
*Answer.* Because the spreadsheet's rule is at least evaluated against the capacity it must respect,
while the model's threshold is evaluated against a label that the model's own deployment changes.
*Reasoning.* The spreadsheet keeps 447 of 520 promises; the gated model keeps 389 of 468. The model
ranks better and commits worse, and the reason is that its 0.90 cut has no representation of
picker-minutes.
*Follow-up.* If you had to keep the model and could change one thing, what would it be? Expected
answer: apply it to ordering inside a capacity gate rather than to the tier decision.
*Red flag.* An answer that blames the threshold value rather than the axis the threshold acts on.
*Scoring.* Full credit names the capacity gate or the label conditioning; half credit names only
"overfitting"; no credit for an answer about data quality with no mechanism.

**Q2. What is the decision unit, and why not the order?**
*Answer.* One order line at its quote instant. Capacity is consumed per line because service time is
`3.0 + 2.0n`, and lateness is observed per line because a line can fail on stockout while its siblings
do not.
*Reasoning.* Aggregating to the order loses the line-level failure attribution that supplies the 22
stockout lines in §6.7, and it double-counts labour if capacity is computed on orders and lateness on
lines.
*Follow-up.* What breaks if you use the order as the unit and a 4-line order loses one line? Expected
answer: the order is late but three lines were fine, so the rate either overstates or understates harm
depending on the aggregation, and the attribution becomes impossible.
*Red flag.* Choosing the order because it is the customer's mental model.
*Scoring.* Full credit states both the capacity and the attribution reason.

**Q3. Compute the feasible order count for a two-hour slot with 3 pickers, 96 effective minutes each,
mean basket 2.2 lines, and `S = 3.0 + 2.0n`.**
*Answer.* `S = 3.0 + 2.0 × 2.2 = 7.4` minutes. Capacity minutes `= 3 × 96 = 288`. `K = ⌊288 / 7.4⌋ =
⌊38.92⌋ = 38` orders, which is `⌊38 × 2.2⌋ = ⌊83.6⌋ = 83` lines.
*Reasoning.* The fixed three-minute term dominates at small baskets, so the order count falls faster
than the line count as baskets shrink.
*Follow-up.* Recompute at 2.8 lines. `S = 8.6`, `K = ⌊288/8.6⌋ = ⌊33.49⌋ = 33` orders and
`⌊33 × 2.8⌋ = ⌊92.4⌋ = 92` lines — fewer orders, more lines, which is the composition effect in D-02.
*Red flag.* Using 120 minutes instead of 96, which inflates capacity by 25%.
*Scoring.* Full credit for both the order and line figure with the floor applied; half credit for the
minutes arithmetic without the floor.

**Q4. Your gate admits 104 lines and demand is 128. What is the minimum number of broken promises,
and what could reduce it?**
*Answer.* 24 at minimum, from `128 − 104`. Only two things reduce it: raising `K` (more pickers,
higher `L_eff`, lower `S`) or lowering demand into the slot (moving lines to P4, which changes the
promise rather than the arithmetic).
*Reasoning.* The bound follows from `Late_mech = max(0, D − K)`. It is not a prediction; it is a
feasibility identity, and no ranking can violate it.
*Follow-up.* Spill from an adjacent slot absorbed 6 of the 33 total mechanical lines. Does that mean
the bound is wrong? Expected answer: the bound is conservative in that direction; ignoring carry-in
promises is the error that flatters a candidate.
*Red flag.* Treating 24 as a model error budget.
*Scoring.* Full credit states the identity and the two levers; bonus for mentioning spill asymmetry.

**Q5. What does a fixed 120-minute yardstick measure that the promise-kept rate does not?**
*Answer.* Speed, independent of what was promised. `r_fixed = N(T_ready − t_quote ≤ 120) / N(eligible)`
cannot be improved by deferral because the denominator is fixed and the numerator is a physical fact.
*Reasoning.* In the constructed cohort, deferring 33 lines raised `r_kept(P2)` from 0.8596 to 0.91786
while `r_fixed` stayed at 0.8596. If the yardstick had moved, the deferral would have changed
something real.
*Follow-up.* When would the two metrics agree? Expected answer: when the tier menu is fixed and no
policy defers, so the promise and the yardstick share a denominator.
*Red flag.* Claiming the yardstick is "the true metric" without saying what it omits — it omits the
customer's stated expectation.
*Scoring.* Full credit for the fixed denominator; half credit for "measures real speed" with no formula.

**Q6. A colleague reports the pooled rate rose from 0.86 to 0.91. What is your first question?**
*Answer.* What happened to the tier mix? A shift of volume toward PN can raise the pooled rate while
every tier degrades, so the first check is the fixed-weight standardised series.
*Reasoning.* `r_pooled = Σ w_s r_s`. With off-peak at 0.905 carrying 59% of volume, the pooled number
is mostly a statement about off-peak, and any mix shift toward the generous tier moves it without
touching a pick.
*Follow-up.* Recomputation shows the fixed-weight series is flat. What now? Expected answer: the
improvement is definitional; check whether the deferral rate and abandonment moved, and report both.
*Red flag.* Asking about sample size first, which is relevant but secondary to whether the estimand
moved.
*Scoring.* Full credit names the mix and the standardisation; half credit names only the denominator.

**Q7. A model's AUC rises from 0.78 to 0.87 when you add `substitution_flag`. What do you do?**
*Answer.* Remove it. Substitution is decided by the picker after seeing the shelf, so the flag is
post-outcome and its predictive power is leakage: a substituted line is systematically later, and the
flag encodes that later state.
*Reasoning.* The Brier score also improved, from 0.147 to 0.139, which is the signature of a leak that
is informative rather than noisy. A legitimate feature improves calibration gradually; a leak produces
a jump concentrated in the tail.
*Follow-up.* What if the flag were "this SKU has high substitution propensity" from history? Expected
answer: acceptable only if the window ends before `t_quote`; check the window end as a scalar.
*Red flag.* Keeping it because it is "available in the warehouse".
*Scoring.* Full credit for the timing argument; half credit for "it's leakage" with no mechanism.

**Q8. Distinguish policy leakage from target leakage using this case.**
*Answer.* Target leakage is a column computed from the outcome — `cancel_reason` — and a lineage
assertion catches it. Policy leakage is a label defined by the treatment; here "kept the two-hour
promise" is observed only for lines that were given priority, so the score estimates the probability
of on-time delivery under priority treatment and is applied to lines that will not get it.
*Reasoning.* The constructed cohort is a mixture: 62% priority with a 0.897 kept rate and 38%
non-priority with 0.801, blending to 0.860. Change the mixture and every prediction is biased by up
to 6 points in the optimistic direction.
*Follow-up.* What single change removes policy leakage? Expected answer: move the target to the pick
event, which is observed for every line regardless of tier.
*Red flag.* Treating both as "leakage" and applying the same fix.
*Scoring.* Full credit for the mixture and the regime change; no credit for naming only the column case.

**Q9. The gate removes 33 promises and the kept count does not move. Was the gate worth it?**
*Answer.* Yes for promise integrity, no for speed, and the case must say which it claims. The P2 rate
rises 5.82 points because 33 line-promises that arithmetic had already doomed are no longer made; the
fixed yardstick stays at 0.8596 because nothing got faster.
*Reasoning.* A promise kept 92% of the time is a better basis for a relationship than one kept 86% of
the time, even at lower volume, provided the deferral does not cost more than the recovery it avoids.
*Follow-up.* At what abandonment rate does it stop being worth it? Expected answer: `δ* = c/m = 4.50 /
12.00 = 0.375`, and the constructed abandonment is 3.8%.
*Red flag.* Claiming the rate improvement is an operational gain.
*Scoring.* Full credit for separating the two claims and computing the break-even.

**Q10. Why is `L_eff` = 96 and not 120, and what is the consequence of the error?**
*Answer.* Twelve minutes of scheduled break, eight of tote-staging trips, four of handoff checks and
re-login. Using 120 gives `K = ⌊480/6.6⌋ = 72` orders instead of 58, a 25% inflation that in this slot
means promising 130 lines instead of 104.
*Reasoning.* The gate's whole function is to refuse promises that cannot be served, so an inflated
`L_eff` converts a gate into a decoration, and the failure is invisible because the inflated number is
also the intuitive one.
*Follow-up.* How would you measure `L_eff` rather than configure it? Expected answer: from the
assignment log, as the share of roster minutes spent in active pick tasks, aggregated over ten days.
*Red flag.* Treating `L_eff` as a modelling hyperparameter to tune.
*Scoring.* Full credit for the 25% figure and the measurement approach.

**Q11. A slot's load reads 96 of 104 at 11:40 and the assignment log shows 118 tasks by 12:00. Diagnose.**
*Answer.* Four candidate causes: stale ledger, concurrent-write race, uncounted carried-in promises,
and tasks created without a promise. The load check is to recompute from the assignment log at
10-minute marks; monotone growth in the gap means staleness, a spike at one second means a race.
*Reasoning.* A 22-task gap in 20 minutes is too large for ordinary arrival variance, and the four
causes have distinguishable signatures in the time series.
*Follow-up.* Which fix is cheapest? Expected answer: count by deadline rather than by quote time,
which is a query change and removes the carried-in class entirely.
*Red flag.* Increasing the gate threshold to match observed load.
*Scoring.* Full credit for at least three causes with distinct checks; half credit for "the ledger is
stale" alone.

**Q12. What is the cheapest intervention in this case and what does it buy?**
*Answer.* Cycle-count the 40 highest-velocity SKUs per store-week, and adopt `t_notify` as the ready
timestamp. Together they can move the fixed yardstick from 0.8596 to about 0.9019 through the 22
stockout lines and to about 0.8750 through the 8 notification lines, and neither requires a model.
*Reasoning.* 22 of the 46 residual late lines fail on a stockout, and 8 more were ready before the
customer was told. Both are addressable by process, which is why they are routinely deferred — they
produce no chart.
*Follow-up.* Why would a team skip them? Expected answer: they are not attributable to the modelling
programme, so the programme reports the gate's 5.82 points while the yardstick gain sits elsewhere.
*Red flag.* Proposing a better model as the cheapest fix.
*Scoring.* Full credit for both interventions and the mechanism; half credit for one.

**Q13. Design the experiment that would test this in a real store.**
*Answer.* Cluster-randomise by slot-instance or store-day, not by line, because lines in a slot share
pickers and a queue. Detect a 5-point move from 0.86 at 80% power: about 755 lines per arm
unclustered, and with a design effect of 7.48 the requirement is about 5,647 lines per arm — eleven
days in one store, or four stores for three days.
*Reasoning.* Line-level assignment lets a treated line displace a control line in the same slot, so
the control's outcome is contaminated by the treatment and the measured effect is diluted.
*Follow-up.* What would tempt you to run it at line level anyway? Expected answer: higher apparent
power per unit time, which is exactly the illusion the leak creates.
*Red flag.* Proposing a pre/post comparison.
*Scoring.* Full credit for the cluster unit and a power figure; half credit for the cluster unit alone.

**Q14. The override rate hits 0.11 in the 14:00–16:00 slot. What does that tell you?**
*Answer.* Operators disagree with the engine on a stratum the engine thinks is easy — a specification
gap, not a modelling one. In the constructed log the cause was a shelf reset that moved high-velocity
SKUs to a second aisle, doubling `S_pick` while the roster was unchanged.
*Reasoning.* An override is a labelled disagreement in the one place the store has ground truth, and
it is the cheapest diagnostic available. A rising override rate in a fixed slot is a change in the
physical store that the feature set does not represent.
*Follow-up.* Would you feed overrides back as training labels? Expected answer: no, because doing so
creates a loop in which the engine learns its own corrections; log them for audit and investigate the
stratum.
*Red flag.* Retraining immediately.
*Scoring.* Full credit for "specification gap" and a physical cause; half credit for "investigate".

**Q15. State the three invariants the decision writer must enforce and why each belongs there.**
*Answer.* At most one effective action per `decision_id` per window; slot load must not exceed `K`
unless degraded; every action must reference a snapshot in the registry. They belong in the writer
because a downstream check reports a violation while a writer prevents one — a promise already shown
to a customer cannot be retracted by a monitoring alert.
*Reasoning.* INV-2 in particular converts a race between concurrent quotes into a retry; at nine
concurrent requests per second the race is routine, and without it a resolver reading 103 of 104
admits two candidates.
*Follow-up.* What does a reversal do? Expected answer: appends a compensating row, never updates in
place, so the override rate stays computable.
*Red flag.* Proposing a nightly reconciliation as the enforcement point.
*Scoring.* Full credit for all three with the prevention argument; half for two.

**Q16. You are the fulfilment manager and the model team proposes the gate plus ranker. What do you
approve, what do you refuse, and on what evidence?**
*Answer.* Approve the shadow gate and the timestamp correction unconditionally. Approve the risk
ranker only with the pick-event label, because the promise label cannot be trusted after deployment.
Refuse any change to the P2 tier before ten days of shadow agreement within 2 lines per slot, and
refuse the roster increase as the solution, because it buys capacity the store cannot budget.
*Reasoning.* The gate's case rests on a feasibility identity that no sample size can overturn; the
ranker's case rests on a 22-line attribution that the store cannot verify without its own cycle counts.
*Follow-up.* What single metric would you put on the wall? Expected answer: promise-kept rate by tier
next to the fixed yardstick, because together they make a definitional gain visible.
*Red flag.* Approving on the strength of the AUC.
*Scoring.* Full credit for approving and refusing distinct items with reasons; no credit for approving
everything conditionally.

Takeaway: the questions that separate candidates are the ones that force a number or a diagnosis; the
framing questions are necessary but nobody passes on them.
