# Refreshing an MMM before a Budget Cycle: Calibration, Holdout Accuracy and the Cost of a Confident Wrong Answer

**Evidence declaration, placed before the first number.** Every figure in this case is
`ILLUSTRATIVE`. The channel response curves, the holdout weeks, the geo lift readings and
the budget envelope were constructed to make the arithmetic and the decision logic
reproducible. Nothing here is a measurement from a real advertiser, a vendor benchmark, or
a published platform result. The *methods* — holdout out-of-sample error, geo-lift
calibration factors, saturation curves with carryover, and marginal-return allocation under
binding constraints — are standard and `DERIVED` or `VERIFIED` as arithmetic. The
organization-specific inputs are `OPEN`, and no reader should quote a number from this file
as evidence about any company.

This case also separates three things that are routinely collapsed: **evidence level**
(`illustrative`), **implementation stage** (the refresh is `proposed`; no model was shipped),
and **editorial status** (`draft`; independent review is outstanding).

## 1. The One-Line Framing

Given **weekly marketing spend by channel and weekly contribution-margin outcomes over 156
weeks**, decide **whether a refreshed marketing-mix model is trustworthy enough to reallocate
an $80,000 weekly budget**, for **the media budget owner who must commit the next quarter's
plan**, to improve **incremental contribution margin per dollar of spend**, subject to **a
contractual $10,000/week display floor, an $8,000/week social minimum, a $20,000/week display
ceiling, and a fixed total envelope**, compared with **the allocation the current model
recommends and the allocation the business is running today**.

The task category is *allocation under a forecasting model*; the decision recurs quarterly,
with a monthly monitoring check. The primary success condition, stated plainly before its
formal definition in Section 9, is: the reallocation must produce a positive incremental
contribution margin measured against the current plan in an out-of-sample period, without
violating any contractual floor.

The reusable pattern is `evaluation-mismatch`: the refreshed model's *in-sample* and
*holdout* fit both improve substantially, and its recommended reallocation is nonetheless
worse than doing nothing. The secondary patterns are `causal-confounding` — the model's
coefficients are identified from observational spend series, so cross-channel correlation can
be read as effect — and `model-monitoring`, because the failure was detectable from a
calibration comparison that the team already had the data to run.

The decisive tradeoff is not accuracy versus interpretability. It is *relative accuracy*:
the refresh improved the model's ability to reproduce the historical contribution series
while making its estimate of one channel's absolute return roughly twice too large. A
forecast model can be made more accurate on the observed series precisely because it absorbs
confounded variation, and allocation decisions depend on the absolute marginal return, not on
the fit.

The obvious simpler framing — "the new model has lower holdout error, therefore use it" —
misses the part of the decision that matters. Holdout error on the observed outcome series
tests whether the model reproduces history under the historical spend policy. The decision
asks what happens under a *different* spend policy, in a region of the response curve the
historical data barely visited. Those are different questions, and the second one is the one
that moves money.

## 2. Context & Background

### Domain and product

The setting is a direct-to-consumer retailer with three paid channels — paid search (`A`),
paid social (`B`), and programmatic display (`C`) — selling a catalogue with a blended
contribution margin of 38% of net revenue after media, fulfilment and payment costs. Media is
planned weekly. The measurement function owns a marketing-mix model (MMM) that regresses
weekly contribution margin on channel spend transformed by carryover (adstock) and saturation
(Hill) terms, plus controls for seasonality, price index, and promotional calendar.

An MMM is a deliberately aggregate instrument. It sees weekly totals, not users. It cannot
distinguish "this spend caused this margin" from "this spend was scheduled when demand was
already high" unless the design or the controls earn that distinction. That limitation is not
a defect to be fixed by a better regressor; it is the reason calibration against an
experimental estimate is part of the workflow rather than an optional extra.

### Workflow, before and after the decision

Before: channel owners submit spend requests, finance reconciles them against a quarterly
envelope, and the measurement team produces a mix model that the budget owner uses as one
input among several. After the decision in this case: the budget owner either adopts the
model's recommended split, keeps the current split, or adopts a *calibrated* split produced by
rescaling one channel's contribution estimate and re-optimising under the same constraints.

The affected people are the channel managers whose budgets move, the finance partner who
signs the envelope, and the analytics engineers who maintain the weekly data mart. The
customer-facing consequence is indirect but real: spend moved out of a channel reduces the
volume of impressions and messages that customers see, so a misallocation changes both margin
and the customer contact pattern.

### Stakeholders and decision owner

| Role | Interest | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Media budget owner (VP Marketing) | Quarterly contribution target | Chooses the allocation; can reject the model |
| Finance partner | Envelope discipline, margin recognition | Can veto a plan that breaches the envelope or floors |
| Measurement lead | Model validity, calibration, audit trail | Recommends; owns the evidence quality statement |
| Channel managers (search, social, display) | Their budgets and their reported ROAS | Can escalate; cannot unilaterally reallocate |
| Analytics engineering | Data mart correctness, weekly refresh reliability | Owns pipeline; not the causal claim |

The decision owner is the budget owner. The measurement lead's job in this case is not to
defend the model; it is to state what the model can and cannot support before the owner
commits.

### Baseline and its documented weaknesses

The baseline is the current split (`A` $40,000, `B` $30,000, `C` $10,000 per week). It is not
a naive baseline — it was itself produced by the previous year's model — but it has two known
weaknesses. First, it was set when social was cheaper and before a platform auction change
raised social clearing prices. Second, the display floor is contractual and was negotiated for
reasons that include a co-marketing commitment, so the floor is not a free parameter to be
optimised away.

The current model (`v1`) is a year old. Its holdout error has been trending upward for two
quarters. That trend is what triggered the refresh, and it is a legitimate trigger: a model
whose out-of-sample error grows is a model whose coefficients are being asked to explain a
world they did not see.

### Scale and timing

The series is 156 weekly observations, 2022-W40 through 2025-W38 in the illustrative
calendar. Weekly spend totals roughly $80,000, so annual media is about $4.16M. Contribution
margin on that media, under the current plan, is estimated at $86,139 per week — a
contribution-to-spend ratio of about 1.077, meaning the media is roughly at its break-even
frontier under the *uncalibrated* model. That frontier position is itself the crux: when a
plan sits near break-even, a modest error in one channel's return estimate flips the sign of
the recommended action.

Peak season is the eight weeks before the winter holiday, when spend rises 40% and the
response curves are least well identified because the historical peak weeks are few. The
decision in this case happens in the four weeks before that peak, which is the worst possible
time to discover a calibration error — the plan is about to be tested at exactly the spend
levels the data least supports.

### Geography and boundaries

The business operates in one country with three regions. Regional spend is not separately
identifiable in a 156-week national series, so the model is national. Any regional
reallocation is out of scope and is explicitly a handoff to a geo-experiment track.

### Prior attempts

Two prior attempts matter. A year earlier, the team tried to identify channel effects with
weekly variation alone and found that search and social spend were correlated at 0.71 across
the series, which produced unstable coefficients that flipped sign between refits. The
resolution at that time was to impose informative priors on the saturation parameters. That
prior imposition is why `v1` under-reacts to spend changes: the priors pull the curves toward
a shape that the data does not strongly contradict.

The second attempt, six months before this case, was a geo holdout test on the social channel.
It ran for six weeks across twelve matched metro groups. Its result is the calibration input
used in Section 6, and its limitation — six weeks, one channel, one season — is one of the
reasons the calibration factor carries a wide interval rather than being treated as a
constant.

### Applicable requirements

No regulatory determination is made in this case. The relevant internal requirements are the
quarterly envelope, the contractual display floor and social minimum, the display ceiling
negotiated with the programmatic partner, and the audit requirement that any booked number be
reproducible from a frozen dataset version. Contract terms are described generically and
`OPEN`; a reader must not infer actual negotiated values.

## 3. The Business Question

**Question (illustrative stakeholder paraphrase, not a quotation).** "The new mix model says
social deserves more money and search a little less. Last year's model said roughly the
opposite about display. I have one quarterly envelope and a floor I cannot break. Which split
do I commit to, and what has to be true for that split to be the right one?"

| Decision contract | Content |
|---|---|
| Question | Which weekly channel split should be committed for the next quarter, and on what evidence? |
| Decision owner | VP Marketing (media budget owner), advised by the measurement lead |
| Trigger and deadline | Holdout error on `v1` rose for two consecutive quarters; the refresh completed; the quarterly commitment is due in ten business days |
| Available actions | (i) keep the current split; (ii) adopt the refreshed model's uncalibrated recommendation; (iii) adopt a calibrated, constraint-respecting recommendation; (iv) defer and run a longer geo test |
| Value and downside | Each $1,000/week of misallocated spend costs contribution margin; a wrong commitment persists for a full quarter (13 weeks) before the next review |
| Objective | Maximise expected incremental contribution margin per week, subject to the envelope and channel constraints |
| Hard constraints | Total spend fixed at $80,000/week; display ≥ $10,000 and ≤ $20,000; social ≥ $8,000; no channel may be cut below the level at which its contractual or operational commitments break |
| Scope | National weekly totals, three paid channels, next quarter (13 weeks) |
| Out of scope | Regional reallocation, creative decisions, pricing, organic channel effects, long-run brand equity |

### Why the objective and the proxy can disagree

The model reports *contribution*. The business objective is *incremental contribution*. Those
coincide only if the model's contribution estimate is an unbiased estimate of what would be
lost by removing the spend. In an observational aggregate model, contribution is an
attribution of observed margin to channels, and it inherits every confound in the spend
series. The proxy can therefore rank channels plausibly while being wrong about the level, and
allocation depends on the level — specifically on the marginal level.

The guardrail that protects against this is not a second metric. It is a *calibration
requirement*: the model's implied lift for at least one channel must be reconciled against an
experimental or quasi-experimental estimate before the model's marginal returns are used to
move more than a stated fraction of budget. Section 9 defines the fraction and the test.

## 4. Assumptions & Constraints

### Assumptions to test

| ID | Assumption | How it is tested | Consequence if false |
|---|---|---|---|
| A-1 | Weekly contribution margin is measured consistently across the series | Reconcile mart totals to finance's weekly margin ledger for 12 sampled weeks | Response-curve levels shift; ROI comparisons across years break |
| A-2 | Adstock carryover is geometric with a stable decay per channel | Compare geometric fit against a delayed-adstock variant on holdout | Marginal returns are mis-timed; optimisation over-reacts to the current week |
| A-3 | Saturation follows a Hill form with an estimated half-saturation point | Profile likelihood over the shape parameter; check holdout | Curves may be too flat near the current operating point, understating marginal return |
| A-4 | No unmeasured common cause drives both a channel's spend and margin | Calibration against the geo test; falsification via placebo channels | Coefficients absorb confounded variation; calibration factor absorbs the bias |
| A-5 | The geo test's lift generalises to national spend levels | Compare test-period spend to planned spend; check saturation position | Calibration factor is level-dependent and must not be applied as a scalar |
| A-6 | Channel response is stable over the 13-week commitment | Monitor weekly residuals against the pre-period distribution | The commitment's value decays within the quarter |

### Externally imposed constraints

The envelope is fixed by finance and is not the analytics team's variable. The display floor
and ceiling come from a partner agreement; the social minimum reflects a staffing commitment
in the social team. These are constraints on the *feasible set*, and a recommendation that
violates them is not a recommendation — it is an infeasibility report.

### Choices under the team's control

The team chooses the response-curve family, the prior structure, the holdout split, the
calibration method, the optimisation grid resolution, the monitoring thresholds, and the
governance rule for how large a reallocation can be made on model evidence alone. Those
choices are the substance of Sections 5, 8 and 9.

### Explicit non-assumptions

The case does **not** assume that a lower holdout error implies a better allocation. It does
not assume that a geo test on one channel in one season yields a national, all-season constant.
It does not assume the model's confidence intervals are calibrated — they are conditional on
the prior structure, and this case treats them as optimistic. Where a claim depends on one of
these, the claim is narrowed in place rather than in a closing paragraph.
