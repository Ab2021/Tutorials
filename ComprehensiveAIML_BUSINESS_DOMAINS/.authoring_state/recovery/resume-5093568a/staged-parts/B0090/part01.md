# BOPIS Promise Accuracy

Every quantity in this case is **ILLUSTRATIVE**: the cohort of 520 order lines, the 447 kept
promises, the pick-time distribution, the slot capacities and the dollar figures are constructed
teaching inputs, computed here so that each step can be re-derived by a reader. Nothing in this
document is a measurement of any retailer, store, workforce, vendor system or customer population,
and no deployment, pilot or vendor selection is asserted. The arithmetic is verified and shown; the
inputs are invented and labelled as such wherever they drive a conclusion.

## 1. The One-Line Framing

At the moment an online basket is converted into a store pickup order, somebody must choose a
pickup-ready time — call it the promise — for each line, and that choice is a commitment the store
will be judged on for the next two hours to two days.

The decision this case studies is therefore narrow and operational: given a point-in-time snapshot
of one store's on-hand inventory, its open pick queue and its staffed pick capacity, which promise
tier should be attached to this order line right now, with the objective of maximising promises the
store can actually keep, subject to a pick-capacity ceiling and a service-level floor on the two-hour
tier.

The trap the case exists to teach is that the obvious machine-learning formulation — predict whether
this order will be ready in two hours, then quote two hours when the probability is high — optimises
something that is *not* the objective, and in the peak slot it makes the objective strictly worse.
The label "was it ready on time" is defined by the promise that was quoted. A policy that quotes two
hours less often generates fewer late two-hour orders by definition, so a model trained on that
label can improve its own score while the store's promises become less reliable at the tier customers
actually care about. That is the evaluation-mismatch pattern: the convenient target is a function of
the treatment.

The reusable discipline is three-part. First, keep the untransformed event — the timestamp at which
the order became physically collectable — as the evaluation substrate, so any candidate promise can
be re-scored against the same realised ready times. Second, treat capacity as a hard pre-gate applied
before scoring, not as a post-hoc filter or a business-rule footnote. Third, report the promise-kept
rate *per tier* plus the volume moved between tiers, because the pooled rate over all promises is a
mix statistic: it can rise while every tier gets worse, simply by shifting volume to the generous
tier.

Takeaway: when the label is generated by the policy, the policy's evaluation must be reconstructed
from raw timestamps and a capacity model, not read off the production label.

## 2. Context & Background

Buy-online-pickup-in-store turns a retail store into two businesses at once. The front of the store
serves walk-in demand with staff who can be interrupted; the back of the store runs a small
fulfilment cell — a picker walks the aisles with a handheld, collects an order's lines into a
tote, validates the tote against the order, and stages it at a counter or locker bank. The customer
is notified, drives over, presents a code, and receives the tote.

The promise is the customer-facing contract on the front end of that sequence. In the constructed
setting of this case the store offers four tiers:

| Tier | Quoted wording | Nominal deadline | Typical basket | Why the tier exists |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P2 | ready in 2 hours | quote time + 120 min | 1–8 lines, shelf-stable | captures urgent and impulse demand |
| P4 | ready in 4 hours | quote time + 240 min | 3–14 lines | absorbs the mid-day queue |
| PD | ready by 20:00 today | store close − 60 min | any size | protects same-day intent |
| PN | ready tomorrow from 10:00 | next-day 10:00 | bulk, bulky, case packs | offloads peak, allows replenishment |

Three structural facts make the tier choice hard, and none of them is visible to a model that only
sees order attributes.

The first fact is that pick capacity is shared and lumpy. A picker is a person with a shift, a break,
a handheld, and a physical walking route. Two pickers assigned to the ten-to-twelve window supply
240 picker-minutes in total; they do not supply 120 minutes of parallel work each on the same aisle,
because two pickers in the same narrow aisle collide and because the tote-staging counter has one
printer. Capacity is therefore a *slot* quantity, not a daily total, and the constraint binds exactly
when demand concentrates — which is also when a promise is most valuable to quote.

The second fact is that the inventory record is a belief, not a fact. On-hand quantity is updated by
receipts, sales and cycle counts, and between counts it drifts through mis-scans, unrecorded
damage, theft, and items left in a customer's hands at the till. A promise quoted on a belief of
"three on hand" is a promise that fails when the third unit is not on the shelf, and the failure
is discovered by the picker, not by the system.

The third fact is that the promise is quoted *before* the pick is assigned, so the queue that the
order joins is not observed at quote time. A model that predicts pick duration from basket size and
item weight can be perfectly accurate about the *service time* of an order and still be useless for
promising, because the realised ready time is service time plus queueing delay, and queueing delay
is a property of the slot's aggregate load rather than of this order.

The baseline in this case is the incumbent deterministic rule, and it is deliberately not a straw
man. The baseline quotes P2 when the store's remaining 120-minute capacity ledger shows a free slot
and the requested SKU's on-hand minus committed is at least the line quantity; otherwise it quotes
P4, and it quotes PN for case-pack lines and for SKUs flagged as bulky. The ledger is a spreadsheet
maintained by the fulfilment lead and is updated twice a day. The baseline's weakness is stale
capacity and coarse inventory, but its virtue is that it is auditable in one page, which is exactly
the property an operations manager needs when a customer complains.

The proposed method in this case is a gradient-boosted classifier trained on eighteen months of
history to predict, per order line, whether the line will reach the staging counter within 120
minutes of the quote. The model is legitimate: its features are basket attributes, SKU velocity,
day-of-week, hour-of-day, store, and a lagged on-hand snapshot. It has, in this constructed
setting, a held-out area under the ROC curve of 0.81 and a Brier score of 0.147. Both numbers are
plausible and neither is the point.

The point is what happens when you deploy the model behind the obvious decision rule — quote P2
whenever the predicted probability of a two-hour ready time exceeds 0.90. In the constructed cohort
the baseline promises P2 to 520 lines and keeps 447, a rate of 0.860. The model-gated rule promises
P2 to 468 lines — fewer, and by construction the ones the model likes — and keeps 389, a rate of
0.831. It promised fewer customers a two-hour pickup, failed a larger share of the promises it did
make, and finished 28 kept lines behind a rule it beats on every offline discrimination measure.

Two mechanisms produce that inversion, and both are the substance of the case. The first is the
promise-dependent label: the training cohort contains only lines that were quoted P2, so the model
estimates the probability of an on-time pickup *given that the store had already accepted a
two-hour obligation and given that such orders were then treated with priority*. Applied to a line
the incumbent rule would have deferred, that score answers a counterfactual question nobody asked
and it is biased upward for exactly the hard lines where the answer matters. The second is the
capacity ceiling: the model ranks lines one at a time and has no representation of a slot, so it
pours promises into the peak window until the pickers are the bottleneck, and every promise beyond
the feasible pick count is mechanically late no matter how good the ranking was. The rest of the
case diagnoses both mechanisms and designs the selection rule that survives them.

Two further context items matter for reading the rest of the document.

The organisational clock is not the data clock. The pick handheld timestamps a line when the picker
confirms the quantity; a separate integration timestamps the customer notification; a third
timestamp records the counter handoff scan. "Ready" in this case always means the *handoff-eligible*
timestamp, defined as the moment the notification was sent, because that is the earliest moment the
customer could have collected. A store that stages an order at 11:40 and notifies at 12:15 has a
ready time of 12:15 for promise purposes, and a metric built on the staging timestamp will
systematically flatter the store.

The incentive context matters too. Store managers are measured on sales per labour hour and on
customer satisfaction, and pickup complaints land in the same satisfaction stream as shelf
availability complaints. A promise policy that trades a small conversion loss on the generous tier
for a large reduction in late handoffs is therefore attractive to the store even when it is neutral
to the e-commerce channel — which is precisely why the decision needs a shared metric rather than
two departments arguing from two dashboards.

## 3. The Business Question

**Question:** for each BOPIS order line presented at quote time, which promise tier — P2, P4, PD or
PN — should be attached so that the realised promise-kept rate at the two-hour tier is maximised
subject to pick capacity in the assigned slot, a service-level floor on P2, and no worsening of the
pooled rate on the other tiers?

**Owner:** the fulfilment manager for the region, who holds the pick roster and is accountable for
late-handoff complaints. The e-commerce product owner holds the quote-time user interface and must
co-sign any change to the tier menu because it changes the customer's stated expectation.

**Trigger:** the decision is made per order line at quote time, which happens continuously. The
*review* of the policy is triggered by any of four events: a change to the pick roster template, a
change to the promise tier menu, a store on-hand accuracy audit that moves the estimate by more than
three percentage points, or a two-week stretch in which the pooled promise-kept rate moves by more
than two percentage points without a roster or catalogue change.

**Available actions:** quote P2; quote P4; quote PD; quote PN; or abstain from the automated choice
and route the line to the fulfilment lead for a manual tier decision. Abstention is a real action
with a real cost — manual decisions take about ninety seconds each and the lead has roughly forty
such minutes per day — so the abstention budget is a constraint, not an escape hatch.

**Objective:** maximise the number of order lines whose realised ready time is at or before the
promised time, with the two-hour tier weighted as the tier where the promise carries the most
customer value. The case uses kept-lines count and the per-tier rate as the primary quantities and
treats conversion loss from tier deferral as a cost to be netted, not as an objective to be ignored.

**Hard constraints:**
- no feature, join or lookup may use data timestamped after the quote-time snapshot, including
  outcome-derived fields such as "was the order eventually fulfilled";
- the number of lines promised into any 120-minute slot must not exceed that slot's feasible pick
  count under the staffed roster, with a documented exception path for roster changes;
- the P2 tier must retain a service-level floor of 0.90 kept in every store-week, evaluated on the
  corrected ready timestamp;
- every automated tier decision must be explainable in one sentence from logged inputs, and must be
  reversible within the same slot by an authorised operator;
- the manual abstention budget must not be exceeded in any store-day.

**Scope:** one region of six stores, fourteen consecutive days, 520 order lines that the incumbent
rule promised at the P2 tier, plus the full 1,180-line cohort those 520 belong to. The wider cohort
exists so that tier deferral can be costed; the 520 are the population where the baseline and the
candidate can be compared on identical lines.

**Out of scope:** claims about any real retailer's performance; the design of the customer-facing
countdown widget; the labour-relations question of how pickers are scheduled; the legal question of
whether a missed pickup promise creates a consumer-credit obligation in any jurisdiction; and the
choice of fulfilment technology vendor.

**Definition of done for the exercise:** a tier-selection rule that can be computed from the
point-in-time snapshot, a capacity model that is checked against realised slot load, an evaluation
that re-scores candidate policies on uncensored ready timestamps, a per-tier and pooled reporting
set with uncertainty, and an explicit list of the conditions under which the rule must fall back to
the incumbent baseline.

Takeaway: the question is a constrained selection problem over four discrete actions with a
capacity ceiling, not a probability-estimation problem with a threshold appended.

## 4. Assumptions & Constraints

The assumptions below are the load-bearing ones. Each has an owner-facing verification and a
fallback that keeps the store operational when the assumption fails, because a promise system that
halts on a failed assumption converts a modelling problem into a customer-service incident.

| ID | Assumption | Verification | Fallback if it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| A-01 | The quote-time snapshot is point-in-time correct for on-hand and queue depth | Replay ten sampled decisions against the raw event log; assert no field has a validity start after the quote | Serve the incumbent ledger value for that store-hour |
| A-02 | The handoff-eligible timestamp is the notification send time, not the staging scan | Join the two timestamp streams and measure the gap distribution; the gap is the correction you must apply if they diverge | Use the later of the two timestamps, which cannot overstate the rate |
| A-03 | On-hand accuracy is stable across the fourteen-day window | Cycle-count a random sample of 120 SKUs per store-week and compare to the record | Widen the P2 eligibility rule by a shrink margin until the audit closes |
| A-04 | Pick service time is a function of basket attributes and is stationary within a store-week | Recompute the service-time distribution per week; flag any week whose median shifts by more than 0.6 min | Refit the capacity model on the trailing four weeks only |
| A-05 | The roster published at 06:00 holds for the day | Compare the roster table to the clock-in log; measure deviations per store-day | Use the clock-in count for any slot with a deviation above one picker |
| A-06 | Tier deferral has a measurable conversion cost that is small relative to the late-handoff cost | Estimate the abandonment rate on deferred lines from the order funnel, then bound the cost with the highest plausible margin | Assume the worst plausible abandonment and test whether the decision still holds |
| A-07 | Manual abstention capacity is forty decisions per store-day | Count actual manual tier decisions per day for a week before the change | Cap automated deferral at the observed manual capacity, not the assumed one |
| A-08 | A line promised at P2 is comparable to a line promised at P4 in every respect except the promise | Compare basket composition, SKU bulk class, and hour-of-day across tiers; report standardised differences | Restrict the comparison to matched strata or drop the comparison |
| A-09 | Substitution is resolved at pick time by a documented rule and the substitute has similar pick difficulty | Sample 40 substituted lines and time both the original and substitute pick | Treat substituted lines as unknown duration and exclude them from capacity arithmetic |
| A-10 | The two-hour tier is the tier customers value most, so weighting it is defensible | Check the funnel: conversion rate and repeat rate by tier, not by opinion | If the funnel does not support the weighting, report both tier-weighted and volume-weighted results |

The constraints are harder-edged and each one has an owner who can refuse.

| ID | Constraint | Owner who can refuse | Test that it is respected |
|---|---|---|---|
| C-01 | No post-quote fields in features, including fulfilment outcome and cancellation reason | Data steward | Automated lineage assertion in the feature build; failure blocks publication |
| C-02 | Slot line count must not exceed the feasible pick count under the staffed roster | Fulfilment manager | Recompute slot load from the assignment log for every slot, every day |
| C-03 | P2 service-level floor of 0.90 kept per store-week | Store manager | Weekly per-tier report; a breach freezes automated tiering for that store |
| C-04 | One-sentence explanation available for every automated tier decision | E-commerce product owner | Sample fifty decisions and have an operator reproduce the sentence from the log |
| C-05 | Reversal within the slot by an authorised operator, with the reversal logged | Store manager | Exercise one reversal per store during the shadow period |
| C-06 | Manual abstention within observed capacity | Fulfilment manager | Daily count versus the A-07 observed capacity |
| C-07 | No tier menu change without co-signature of e-commerce and fulfilment | E-commerce product owner | Change log requires two named approvers |

Two of these deserve a paragraph each because they are the ones teams quietly relax.

C-01 is relaxed the moment somebody adds "historical cancellation rate for this SKU" as a feature.
That field is computed over a trailing window that includes the current order's own future: if the
order is later cancelled for stock, that cancellation enters the trailing window of the next order,
and the model learns a signal that is partly a function of the outcomes it is meant to predict. The
leak is small per order and large in aggregate, and it is invisible in a feature-importance chart
because the field looks like a legitimate prior. The only reliable defence is a lineage assertion
that refuses to publish a feature set containing any field whose validity start exceeds the snapshot
timestamp — a build-time failure, not a review checklist item.

C-02 is relaxed the moment the capacity ledger is treated as reporting rather than as a gate. The
ledger is updated twice a day in the incumbent process; the roster changes at 06:00; the promotion
calendar changes on Thursdays. A gate that reads a twice-daily ledger is a gate that is wrong during
exactly the four hours it matters. The verification in the table is therefore not "does the ledger
exist" but "does recomputing slot load from the assignment log agree with the gate's own count",
which is a different and much harder test.

The binding risk in this case is neither of those, however. It is the evaluation risk: the population
of lines that were promised P2 is a *selected* population, and selecting a different population
changes the denominator. Every number in sections 6, 9 and 11 has to be read with that in mind, and
section 9 states the population explicitly for each reported figure so the reader can check whether
two rates being compared actually share a denominator.

A note on what is deliberately not assumed: the case does not assume the model is bad. Its ranking
quality is real and it is used in the final design. The case also does not assume the baseline is
good; the baseline's stale ledger is the reason the candidate has room to improve. What the case
refuses is the inference from "better AUC" to "better promises", and the arithmetic in section 6
shows that inference failing by a measurable amount on the same 520 lines.

Takeaway: assumptions about timestamps and capacity silently determine the metric, so each one gets
a test whose failure has a defined operational consequence rather than a note in a risk register.
