## 5. Data, Tools & Method

### 5a Data Specification

The analysis grain is **one order line at its quote instant**. Everything downstream — capacity,
lateness, cost — is aggregated from that grain and never from an order grain, because a four-line
order consumes four picks and one notification, and conflating the two double-counts either labour
or service.

| Element | Definition used here |
|---|---|
| Unit of analysis | one `line_id`, being one SKU in one quantity on one order |
| Primary key | `line_id` (stable, opaque) |
| Foreign keys | `order_id`, `store_id`, `sku_id`, `quote_id` |
| Partition key | `store_id` and `quote_date` |
| Decision timestamp | `t_quote`, the instant the tier is written into the customer's confirmation |
| Event timestamps | `t_assign` (pick task created), `t_pick_confirm` (handheld quantity confirm), `t_stage` (tote at counter), `t_notify` (customer notification sent), `t_handoff` (counter scan) |
| Outcome timestamp | `t_ready = t_notify` under the A-02 convention |
| Population | 520 lines the incumbent rule promised at P2 on one promotion Saturday in one store |
| Wider cohort | 1,180 lines in total that day, used only to cost tier deferral |
| Currency | USD, stated at the order's own price book version |
| Horizon | quote instant to `t_notify`, truncated at 240 minutes |

Field inventory, with the availability discipline stated per field. The right-hand column is the one
that matters for leakage, and it is the column reviewers skip.

| Field | Type | Units | Available at `t_quote`? |
|---|---|---|---|
| `line_qty` | integer | eaches | yes |
| `sku_velocity_28d` | float | eaches/day | yes, lagged one day |
| `on_hand_recorded` | integer | eaches | yes, as of the last cycle count or receipt |
| `on_hand_committed` | integer | eaches | yes, sum of open picks and holds |
| `store_zone` | category | none | yes |
| `slot_picker_count` | integer | pickers | yes, from the published roster |
| `open_pick_minutes` | float | picker-minutes | yes, from the assignment log as of `t_quote` |
| `basket_line_count` | integer | lines | yes |
| `hour_of_day`, `day_of_week` | integer | clock | yes |
| `promo_flag` | boolean | none | yes |
| `substitution_flag` | boolean | none | **no** — known only at pick time |
| `final_fulfilment_status` | category | none | **no** — outcome |
| `cancel_reason` | category | none | **no** — outcome |
| `t_notify` | timestamp | UTC | **no** — outcome |

The three "no" rows are the fields that make this problem easy to get wrong and impossible to detect
after the fact. Each of them is plausibly available in a warehouse table and plausibly useful, which
is why the lineage assertion in C-01 has to be mechanical rather than discretionary.

Three further specification points deserve statement because they are where two analysts would
silently diverge.

*Truncation.* A line whose `t_notify` never arrives — the order was cancelled, or the customer never
collected and the tote was returned to stock — has no ready time. The case treats such lines as
late for promise purposes rather than dropping them. Dropping them is the single most common way to
inflate a promise-kept rate, because non-collection correlates with the slowest picks.

*Clock alignment.* `t_notify` is generated by an integration service whose clock is synchronised
hourly, while `t_pick_confirm` comes from handhelds whose clocks drift. The case therefore uses only
`t_notify` and `t_quote` for the primary metric and uses handheld timestamps only for service-time
estimation, where a drift of seconds is immaterial. Mixing the two streams in one subtraction is a
real defect observed in fulfilment dashboards and it produces negative durations.

*Quantity.* A line of eight eaches is not eight picks in the sense of eight walks — the picker walks
once and counts eight. Service time therefore scales with the *cube travel* plus a per-each count
term, not with the line count alone. The case approximates this with a per-order fixed term plus a
per-line term and absorbs the per-each counting into the fixed term, then checks the approximation
against the observed service-time distribution in 5d.

**What is not available at decision time** is the crucial part of the specification and it is worth
a compact statement: the realised ready time, the substitution decision, the picker who will be
assigned, whether the pick will fail on stock, whether the customer will collect, whether a second
order will arrive in the same slot, and whether the roster will hold. Any model whose inputs include
a proxy for those quantities is answering a different question than the one the store asks.

### 5b Tools & Stack

The stack proposed here is deliberately boring. The case is about evaluation and decision structure,
and a sophisticated stack would obscure whether the failure came from the method or the machinery.

| Layer | Constructed choice | Why this and not something else |
|---|---|---|
| Source capture | append-only raw landing of the seven event types, with ingestion timestamp preserved separately from event timestamp | two timestamps per event is the minimum needed to diagnose late arrival without guessing |
| Snapshot builder | as-of join keyed on `t_quote`, publishing an immutable snapshot id per decision batch | makes replay exact; a snapshot that can be recomputed is not a snapshot |
| Feature build | deterministic transforms from the snapshot only, with a lineage manifest listing every source column and its validity start | allows a build-time refusal rather than a review discussion |
| Model | gradient-boosted trees, 400 rounds, depth 4, learning rate 0.05, monotone constraint on basket line count | depth 4 limits interaction mining on a cohort of this size; the monotone constraint encodes a known direction |
| Decision service | rule engine that applies capacity gate, then ranking, then abstention budget | keeps the gate inspectable and testable without retraining anything |
| Decision log | append-only record of snapshot id, gate inputs, rank, chosen tier, and operator override | the only artifact that lets a later audit reconstruct why a customer saw a tier |
| Review queue | manual tier decisions for lines the engine abstains on, with a hard daily cap | converts abstention from an escape hatch into a budgeted resource |
| Metric job | nightly per-tier rates, pooled rate, fixed-yardstick rate, and the mix decomposition | reports four numbers because one number is what caused the problem |

Two stack choices are load-bearing enough to justify explicitly.

The **immutable snapshot** is the difference between an audit and an anecdote. If the feature build
reads live tables, then the cohort you evaluate next month is not the cohort the model saw, and every
discrepancy becomes a debate about which run was right. Publishing a snapshot id per decision batch,
and refusing to score a batch without one, costs a little storage and removes an entire class of
arguing.

The **separate decision service** exists so the capacity gate can be changed without touching the
model. In the failure narrative of this case the model was fine and the gate was wrong; an
architecture that fuses them would have required a retrain to fix a scheduling bug, which is how
teams end up shipping a second model to correct the first.

Specific product names, versions, cloud services and licences are intentionally not asserted. The
relevant properties are: append-only capture, as-of joins, deterministic transforms, an inspectable
rule layer, and a log that survives schema change. Any stack with those properties supports the case.

### 5c Method Summary

The method has four separable stages, and separating them is itself part of the lesson, because the
failure in this case is a stage-boundary failure rather than an algorithmic one.

**Stage 1 — point-in-time assembly.** Build the quote-time snapshot, attach the realised `t_ready`,
and mark every line as kept or late against the tier that was actually quoted. Output: a table with
one row per line and an explicit quoted tier.

**Stage 2 — offline discrimination.** Fit the classifier on the trailing eighteen months of
P2-quoted lines and report AUC and Brier on a held-out final eight weeks. This stage answers "can the
model rank lines by their probability of an on-time two-hour ready time, among lines that were
promised two hours". It is a legitimate question and the answer here is yes.

**Stage 3 — counterfactual re-scoring.** This is the stage teams skip. For every candidate policy,
recompute kept/late from the raw `t_ready` values against the promise the candidate policy *would*
have quoted, and additionally from a *fixed* 120-minute yardstick that no policy can move. Two
yardsticks, because they answer different questions: the policy-relative one asks "did we keep the
promise we made", the fixed one asks "did the store get faster".

**Stage 4 — capacity-constrained selection.** Replace the per-line threshold with a per-slot
selection: compute the feasible line count for each quote slot from the roster and the service-time
model, rank the candidates in that slot by expected value of admission, admit the top `K`, and route
the remainder to the next tier, whose capacity is checked in the same pass. Selection is therefore
an assignment problem over slots, solved greedily within each slot and sequentially across tiers.

The baseline against which all four stages are compared is the incumbent deterministic rule
described in section 2: ledger-checked P2 when a nominal slot is free, P4 otherwise, PN for
case-pack and bulky lines. It is reproduced exactly, including its twice-daily ledger staleness,
because a baseline reproduced with better data is not the baseline and the comparison would be
flattering rather than informative.

Alternatives considered and not carried forward, with reasons:

| Alternative | Why not used here |
|---|---|
| Regression on `t_ready` minutes, then promise if predicted p95 below 120 | attractive and worth a follow-up; needs a quantile model with enough peak-slot mass, which this cohort lacks |
| Survival model with censoring for non-collection | correct in principle; the case has only 41 non-collection lines, too few to fit and validate honestly |
| Constrained optimisation over slots as a single LP | over-specified for 4 tiers and 14 slot-instances; greedy within slot is within one line of optimal here |
| Queueing simulation (M/M/c style) | informative for capacity sizing, but the service-time distribution is not exponential and the simulation needs parameters the store cannot estimate |
| Human-only tier assignment | removed from consideration because the 90-second manual cost exceeds the abstention budget at this volume |

### 5d Provenance, Quality, Exclusions, Leakage

Provenance for the constructed cohort is stated as a chain rather than a sentence, so a reader can
see exactly which link is invented.

```text
raw event capture  ->  as-of snapshot (id per batch)  ->  feature manifest  ->  model artifact
        |                       |                              |                    |
   [constructed]          [constructed]                  [constructed]        [constructed]
        v                       v                              v                    v
  ingestion vs event      snapshot immutability       validity-start check    version pinned
  timestamp retained      replayed 10 decisions       build fails on late col  in decision log
```

The quality checks are the ones that determine whether the numbers in section 6 mean anything.

| Check | Procedure | Constructed result |
|---|---|---|
| Key uniqueness | count distinct `line_id` against row count per partition | 1,180 rows, 1,180 distinct, no duplicate |
| Temporal validity | for each feature, compare validity start to `t_quote` | 15 of 18 features pass; 3 are outcome fields and are excluded |
| Snapshot replay | rebuild the snapshot for 10 sampled decisions and diff | 10 of 10 identical |
| Ready-time presence | count lines with `t_notify` null | 41 lines null, retained and counted late |
| Negative durations | count `t_ready < t_quote` | 2 lines, both traced to a handheld clock drift, both quarantined |
| Service-time fit | compare modelled `S` to observed per-order durations | model median 6.6 min vs observed 6.9 min, model understates by 0.3 min |
| Completeness by store-hour | share of lines with all 15 features non-null | 0.984 overall; 0.951 in the 10:00–12:00 slot |
| Duplicate orders | same customer, same SKU, two orders within 10 minutes | 7 pairs, retained, flagged as possibly split baskets |

The service-time check deserves comment because it is the one that changed the conclusion. The
modelled mean of 6.6 minutes understates the observed 6.9 minutes by 4.3%, which means the
constructed capacity numbers in section 6 are optimistic by roughly that amount. Recomputing the
10:00–12:00 capacity at 6.9 minutes gives `⌊384/6.9⌋ = 55` orders rather than 58, and 55 orders at
1.8 lines is 99 lines rather than 104 — a five-line reduction in the slot's feasible promise count.
The case keeps 104 as its working figure and states the 99 explicitly, because a capacity model whose
error is known and unapplied is the exact mechanism by which slot gates silently oversubscribe.

Exclusions, each with a rate because an unquantified exclusion is indistinguishable from a silent
one:

| Exclusion | Count | Rate of cohort | Justification | Effect if reversed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lines with negative duration | 2 | 0.4% | clock defect | immaterial |
| Lines from a store closed mid-day for a facilities fault | 34 | 6.5% | no capacity data for the affected hours | would raise capacity, lowering mechanical lateness |
| Lines on a SKU with no 28-day velocity (new item) | 19 | 3.7% | velocity feature undefined | would add uncertainty to ranking, not to capacity |
| Lines quoted during a 40-minute outage of the quote service | 11 | 2.1% | `t_quote` unreliable | would distort the slot assignment, not the totals |
| Lines on orders later merged with another order | 6 | 1.2% | `line_id` survives but slot may change | small |

Note that the closed-store exclusion is the largest and it is not random: a store that closes for a
facilities fault loses pickers for part of a day, which is precisely a capacity event. Excluding it
removes the most informative capacity observations from the cohort. The case retains the exclusion
because the affected hours have no roster record, and flags that the resulting capacity estimate is
biased toward well-staffed hours. This is the kind of honest limitation that a case can state and a
dashboard usually cannot.

**Leakage.** Three leak classes appear in this problem and they need different defences.

*Target leakage.* Fields computed from the outcome — `final_fulfilment_status`, `cancel_reason`,
`substitution_flag` — are the obvious ones and the lineage assertion catches them. In the constructed
build, adding `substitution_flag` to the feature set raised held-out AUC from 0.81 to 0.87 and raised
the Brier score from 0.147 to 0.139, a change large enough to look like a modelling win and
sufficient to prove the field is a leak: substitution is decided by the picker after seeing the shelf,
and a substituted line is systematically later than a clean pick.

*Temporal leakage through aggregates.* A trailing cancellation rate per SKU is computed over a window
that includes the current order's future. The constructed build includes such a feature in a
competing variant; it raises AUC by 0.02 and its effect is concentrated in low-velocity SKUs where the
trailing window contains few observations and the current order dominates it. The defence is a
window-end assertion: an aggregate's window end must be at or before the snapshot timestamp, checked
as a scalar comparison rather than by reading the SQL.

*Policy leakage.* The subtlest class. The target "kept the two-hour promise" is defined by a policy
that, in training, gave P2 lines priority in the pick queue. The model therefore learns the
probability of on-time delivery *under priority treatment*, and applies that estimate to lines that,
under the new policy, will not receive priority. This is not a bug in the data; it is a mismatch
between the training regime and the deployment regime, and no lineage assertion can detect it. The
only defences are (i) to model ready time rather than the promise outcome, or (ii) to evaluate any
policy change on a fixed yardstick so that the changed treatment cannot move the target. Section 9
adopts (ii) and section 13 explains why (i) is the better long-run choice.

The method-selection ledger records what was rejected and why, because a case that only records the

| Candidate | Discrimination | Operational fit | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Incumbent ledger rule | none stated | high; auditable in one page | kept as baseline | cannot be improved without changing data freshness |
| Classifier, per-line threshold at 0.90 | AUC 0.81, Brier 0.147 | low; 22 lines over capacity in the constrained slot | rejected | gate is on the wrong axis |

The ledger's last row matters for the argument in section 11. A policy that promises nothing is
trivially accurate, and any evaluation that fails to fix the tier mix will rank it highly. The
metric dictionary therefore requires the tier distribution to be reported alongside every rate.

Reproducibility caveat, stated plainly: the inputs are constructed, so "reproducible" here means
internally consistent and hand-checkable, not re-runnable against a real feed. Every arithmetic line
in section 6 can be verified with a calculator from the numbers printed next to it, and section 11
gives the independent re-check for the headline figures.

Takeaway: reproducibility is a property of the decision record, not of the notebook, and the fields
that make it reproducible are the ones a hurry removes.

## 6. Analysis Walkthrough

This walkthrough builds six results. Each is presented in the same five-part anatomy so that the
constraint and the failure boundary are as visible as the point estimate, and each is followed by
the diagnostic that distinguishes it from its nearest competing explanation. All inputs are
ILLUSTRATIVE; the arithmetic is exact under the stated inputs.

### 6.1 Ready-time decomposition: what the promise is actually competing with

```text
FORMULA [D-01]: T_ready = t_quote + W_q + S_pick + H_handoff
WHERE:
  T_ready     [timestamp]  notification send time, the customer-visible ready moment
  t_quote     [timestamp]  instant the tier is written to the confirmation
  W_q         [minutes]    queueing delay: from quote to pick-task start, >= 0
  S_pick      [minutes]    pick service: task start to tote staged, > 0
  H_handoff   [minutes]    staging to notification, >= 0
CONSTRAINT:
  all four terms measured on the same clock domain; no term may be derived from T_ready itself.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (constructed, store S3, 10:00-12:00 slot, order of 2 lines):
  W_q = 34.0 min, S_pick = 3.0 + 2.0*2 = 7.0 min, H_handoff = 4.5 min
  T_ready - t_quote = 34.0 + 7.0 + 4.5 = 45.5 min
INTERPRETATION:
  Only S_pick is a property of this order. W_q is a property of the slot's aggregate load, and
  45.5 min of an admittedly easy order is already 38% of a 120-minute promise consumed by waiting.
```

The decomposition is the whole case in one line: a per-line model can be excellent at `S_pick` and
still be unable to promise, because `W_q` is not a line attribute. In the constructed cohort the
median `S_pick` is 6.6 minutes against a 120-minute budget, which is 5.5% of the promise, while the
median `W_q` in the 10:00–12:00 slot is 41 minutes, which is 34%. A model that explains 90% of the
variance in a quantity worth 5.5% of the decision is not a promising instrument, however good its
AUC looks on the label.

Boundary where the formula breaks: if the picker works the task immediately on assignment, `W_q` is
zero by construction and the decomposition collapses to service time plus handoff. That happens in
low-traffic slots and it is exactly where the per-line classifier performs well — the model is not
wrong, it is applied outside its regime. The discriminating check is the ratio of `W_q` to
`120 − S_pick − H_handoff` computed per slot; above roughly 0.3 the slot is queue-dominated and a
per-line gate cannot control the outcome.

### 6.2 Slot capacity: converting a roster into a feasible line count

```text
FORMULA [D-02]: K_slot = floor( (P * L_eff) / S_mean ),  S_mean = a + b * n_mean
WHERE:
  K_slot   [orders]        feasible pick orders in the slot
  P        [pickers]       pickers assigned for the whole slot
  L_eff    [minutes]       effective picking minutes per picker after breaks and staging trips
  S_mean   [minutes/order] mean service time
  a        [minutes]       fixed per-order term: tote, walk to zone, staging
  b        [minutes/line]  marginal per-line term
  n_mean   [lines/order]   mean basket size in the slot
CONSTRAINT:
  P and L_eff from the published roster, not from the ledger; n_mean from lines quoted into the
  slot, not from the store's daily average.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (constructed):
  10:00-12:00: P = 4, L_eff = 96 min -> 384 picker-min. a = 3.0, b = 2.0, n_mean = 1.8
    S_mean = 3.0 + 2.0*1.8 = 6.6 min
    K = floor(384 / 6.6) = floor(58.18) = 58 orders
    lines = floor(58 * 1.8) = floor(104.4) = 104 lines
  16:00-18:00: P = 3 -> 288 picker-min
    K = floor(288 / 6.6) = floor(43.64) = 43 orders
    lines = floor(43 * 1.8) = floor(77.4) = 77 lines
INTERPRETATION:
  The two peak slots can absorb 104 + 77 = 181 promised lines. Demand into those slots is
  128 + 86 = 214 lines. The gap is 33 lines, and no ranking of those 214 can remove it.
```

Two details in this formula are usually got wrong.

The first is `L_eff`. A two-hour slot with a picker assigned is not 120 minutes of picking. The
constructed store loses twelve minutes to a scheduled break, eight minutes to tote-staging trips
(the staging counter is at the back, and a picker carrying a full tote walks it there), and four
minutes to handoff checks and handheld re-login. That leaves 96 minutes, an 80% availability factor.
Using 120 minutes instead of 96 inflates capacity by 25%, which in the 10:00–12:00 slot means
promising 130 lines instead of 104. A 25% capacity inflation is the difference between a gate that
works and a gate that is decoration.

The second is `n_mean`. Capacity in *orders* and capacity in *lines* move in opposite directions as
baskets grow, which is a trap worth working through because it invalidates a natural shortcut.

Boundary where the formula breaks — basket composition. Recompute at `n_mean = 2.4`:

```text
S_mean = 3.0 + 2.0*2.4 = 7.8 min
K      = floor(384 / 7.8) = floor(49.23) = 49 orders      (down from 58)
lines  = floor(49 * 2.4) = floor(117.6) = 117 lines       (up from 104)
```

Larger baskets reduce the number of orders the slot can serve and *increase* the number of lines,
because the fixed three-minute term is amortised over more lines. A gate calibrated on lines per
slot and then applied to a day with bigger baskets will under-promise; a gate calibrated on orders
and applied to a day with small baskets will over-promise in minutes while looking safe in orders.
The correct gate is on *picker-minutes*, with lines as a reporting convenience. The discriminating
check is to compute capacity both ways for the day in question and compare; if the two disagree by
more than 5%, the slot gate must run on minutes.

### 6.3 Mechanical lateness: the lower bound no ranking can beat

```text
FORMULA [D-03]: Late_mech(slot) = max(0, D_slot - K_slot)
WHERE:
  Late_mech [lines]   lines that must miss the promise because the slot is oversubscribed
  D_slot    [lines]   lines promised into the slot
  K_slot    [lines]   feasible lines from D-02, converted at the slot's own n_mean
CONSTRAINT:
  D_slot counts every promise whose 120-minute deadline falls inside the slot, including promises
  quoted in the previous slot. Omitting carried-over promises understates D_slot.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (constructed, baseline policy):
  10:00-12:00: D = 128, K = 104 -> Late_mech = 24
  16:00-18:00: D =  86, K =  77 -> Late_mech =  9
  total mechanical = 33 lines
  observed late at baseline = 73 lines
  residual attributable to other causes = 73 - 33 = 40 lines
INTERPRETATION:
  33 of the 73 observed late lines are already determined by arithmetic before any model is fitted.
  The remaining 40 are the only part any ranking could influence, and they split across stockouts,
  picker absence, and notification delay.
```

This is the single most useful number in the case because it partitions the problem. Of the 73
broken promises, 45% are a capacity fact and 55% are operational noise. Any evaluation that credits
a model with improving the whole 73 is claiming credit for arithmetic.

Boundary where the formula breaks: the bound assumes lines are served in some order and that the
slot cannot borrow capacity from an adjacent slot. Both assumptions are false in practice. A picker
who starts early can pre-pick lines whose quote arrives at 11:55, and a quiet 12:00–14:00 slot can
absorb spill. In the constructed data, spill absorbed 6 of the 33 mechanical lines, so the true
mechanical count is 27 and the residual is 46. The direction of the correction matters: ignoring
spill *overstates* mechanical lateness and therefore *understates* what a model could achieve, which
is a conservative error and acceptable. Ignoring carry-in, in contrast, understates mechanical
lateness and flatters every candidate — that is the error to guard against, and the guard is to
count every promise whose deadline lands in the slot, not every promise quoted in it.

### 6.4 The pooled rate is a mix statistic, and the promise defines the numerator

```text
FORMULA [D-04]: r_pooled = sum_s (w_s * r_s),  w_s = D_s / sum_s D_s
WHERE:
  r_pooled [fraction]  kept lines / promised lines across all tiers and strata
  r_s      [fraction]  kept rate in stratum s (a slot, a tier, or a store)
  w_s      [fraction]  volume weight of stratum s, summing to 1
CONSTRAINT:
  "kept" must be evaluated against the tier actually quoted, and the weights must come from the
  same cohort as the rates; mixing a baseline denominator with a candidate numerator is invalid.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (constructed, baseline):
  slot A: r = 100/128 = 0.7813, w = 128/520 = 0.2462 -> contribution 0.1924
  slot B: r =  70/ 86 = 0.8140, w =  86/520 = 0.1654 -> contribution 0.1346
  offpeak: r = 277/306 = 0.9052, w = 306/520 = 0.5885 -> contribution 0.5327
  r_pooled = 0.1924 + 0.1346 + 0.5327 = 0.8597, check 447/520 = 0.8596 (rounding)
INTERPRETATION:
  The pooled figure is dominated by the off-peak stratum, which carries 59% of the volume and runs
  at 0.905. A policy that fixes the peak and ignores off-peak moves the pooled number by at most
  0.14 points per 10 points of peak improvement.
```

Now the counterexample that makes this formula dangerous, and the reason the case insists on a fixed
yardstick.

Suppose a policy moves every one of the 73 late lines from P2 to P4 and each of them is then ready
within four hours. Under a policy-relative metric, the pooled kept rate becomes `520/520 = 1.000`.
Nothing about the store changed: not one pick started earlier, not one tote reached the counter
sooner, not one customer received an item faster. The metric moved by 14 points because the
definition of success moved with the promise.

Recompute the same policy on the fixed yardstick "fraction of P2-eligible lines notified within 120
minutes of `t_quote`": `447/520 = 0.860`, identical to the baseline, because the yardstick is
independent of what was promised. The two metrics disagree by 0.140, and the disagreement is
entirely definitional. This is the evaluation-mismatch pattern in its purest form: the label was
generated by the policy, so a policy change that improves the label without improving the operation
is indistinguishable from a genuine improvement unless the evaluation refuses to use the label.

```text
FORMULA [D-05]: r_fixed = N_notified_within_120 / N_eligible
WHERE:
  r_fixed              [fraction]  policy-independent speed metric
  N_notified_within_120 [lines]    lines with T_ready - t_quote <= 120 min
  N_eligible           [lines]     lines quoted at any tier whose basket was P2-eligible
CONSTRAINT:
  eligibility fixed before the policy runs; the denominator must not shrink when the policy defers.
NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (constructed):
  baseline:  447 lines within 120 min of 520 eligible -> 0.8596
  gated:     389 lines within 120 min of 520 eligible -> 0.7481
  shifted:   447 lines within 120 min of 520 eligible -> 0.8596  (deferral cannot change it)
INTERPRETATION:
  The fixed yardstick separates speed from promise-keeping. Under it the 0.90-threshold policy is
  visibly worse than baseline, which the policy-relative metric concealed behind a smaller
  denominator.
```

The gated figure of 389 is the same count that gave 0.844 against an admitted population of 468. Read
against the fixed 520 it is 0.748, because 52 lines were deferred and therefore did not reach the
customer within 120 minutes as promised. Two numbers, one dataset, and the choice of denominator
decides whether the policy looks like a mild disappointment or a clear regression. Section 9 fixes
the reporting convention; the point here is that the convention has to be fixed before the result is
seen, not after.

Takeaway: compute the pooled rate only alongside per-stratum rates and the fixed yardstick, because
the pooled rate alone is a statistic about the mix rather than about the store.
